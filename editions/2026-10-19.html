Lead

India warns deepfake videos of the Prime Minister and ministers are selling fake welfare schemes in Facebook and Instagram ads. India's Indian Cyber Crime Coordination Centre (I4C) issued an advisory dated October 19, 2026 warning that ads on Meta's Facebook and Instagram use manipulated/deepfake videos of the Prime Minister, the Finance Minister and chief ministers to promote non-existent government welfare schemes and cashback offers — victims are routed to WhatsApp chats, phone numbers or fake sites and charged repeated "taxes, processing and registration fees," or tricked into approving UPI collect requests that send money out instead of in. It was a fragmented news day: no Reuters/AP/Bloomberg wire dated Oct 19 on a new global platform takedown was found, and this edition labels every carryover and thinly-sourced item as such. INTERPOL's deepfake campaign also continued rolling out Oct 18–19, Japan reported a 15% monthly surge in phishing reports with Amazon and Apple the most-impersonated brands, and police raids in Thailand and Poland targeted scam and laundering networks.

NEW & EMERGING SCAMS

1. INTERPOL launches AI-made videos warning about deepfake boss / voice-clone / romance scams

  • What's new: INTERPOL launched a public campaign on Monday, October 19, 2026 centred on three short videos that were themselves produced with AI: a criminal impersonating a boss on a video call asking for a $30,000 supplier payment, a cloned woman's voice, and a fabricated romantic partner. Slogan: "Wait. Validate. Don't take the bait." INTERPOL says deepfake crime has moved from public figures to private citizens via investment fraud, impersonation, romance scams and sextortion, and that tools can now copy a face/voice from a small amount of online material with little skill or cost. One secondary roundup cites INTERPOL's official announcement as published Oct 18 — treat Oct 18–19 as the launch window, with Oct 19 as the public launch date reported by most outlets.
  • Who's targeted: Employees asked to pay invoices, family members receiving distress calls, and people in fast-moving online romances — ordinary citizens, not just executives/celebrities.
  • Date/source: Campaign announced Oct 19, 2026 in multiple syndicated reports; INTERPOL HQ Facebook reel and Nepal Police Instagram reel of the same PSA both posted 2026-10-19
  • Tip: Pause on any urgent video/voice request for money. Call back on a number you already trust, use a family safe word, and never approve a transfer on the call itself.
  • Source: https://wisconsintelegraph.com/interpol-videos-warn-public-deepfake-scams/

2. WhatsApp "vote for Sophie / Eliska" voting scam hijacks accounts via linked devices

  • What's new: A message reportedly from a known contact asks the recipient to vote for "Sophie" or "Eliska" in a supposed children's contest. One version leads to a page asking for a mobile number or showing a verification QR code; another asks for personal data. In the documented related pattern, victims are tricked into authorising an unfamiliar WhatsApp linked device or entering a linking code — giving the attacker a live session to read chats and send messages as the victim until removed. Opening the link alone does not by itself prove the account was stolen; a verification/linking step is typically involved.
  • Who's targeted: WhatsApp users in Galicia, Spain in these reports; the voting-lure pattern is global.
  • Date/source: Reported October 19, 2026 by La Voz de Galicia and Faro de Vigo, as summarised in the opened NeoTeo article
  • Tip: Never scan a QR code or enter a code from a voting/contest page. Check WhatsApp > Linked devices and sign out anything you don't recognise; turn on two-step verification.
  • Source: https://www.neoteo.com/en/galicia-outlets-report-a-whatsapp-voting-scam

3. Recruitment / task-job texts enter UK top 10 — Virgin Media O2 Scam Index

  • What's new: Virgin Media O2's quarterly Scam Index (released Oct 15, widely republished Oct 19) moved recruitment scams into its top 10 most commonly seen scams in the UK (9th, up from 11th). Example texts promise £100–£300 for 60–90 minutes of simple remote work, or pose as a named recruiter asking to reply YES. In a Censuswide survey of 2,000 UK adults (Oct 2–6), 37% had encountered a job/employment scam, 47% of 18–24s had received an unsolicited job offer they hadn't applied for, and 29% of victims were tricked into sending money/fees for training, equipment or an application — the classic task-scam pay-to-unlock step.
  • Who's targeted: Jobseekers, especially 18–24-year-olds and people mass-applying who may mistake a scam text for a real reply.
  • Date/source: HotMinute article published Oct 19, 2026, reporting the Oct 15 VMO2 release — verified; labelled fresh attention Oct 19, underlying index Oct 15
  • Tip: Real work never requires you to deposit money to unlock earnings. Verify any recruiter via contact details you find yourself on the company's official site; in the UK, forward scam texts free to 7726.
  • Source: https://hotminute.co.uk/2026/10/19/the-fake-job-texts-hitting-phones-in-the-uk/

RESEARCH DESK (2025–2026 research; preprints labelled; all six are new to this newsletter)

1. The psychological and communication tactics behind "pig butchering" scams (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Authors: Ayşe Nur Asyalı, Muriel-Larissa Frank, Pol Hölzmer
  • Venue/date: Journal of Cybersecurity, Vol. 12, Issue 1, 2026, article tyag003 (Oxford University Press) — peer-reviewed
  • Key finding: Most pig-butchering research studies victims; this study studied the scammers' own training material. Analysing 26 scam manuals, the authors found manuals systematically weave seven psychological and communication theories — including Social Penetration Theory and Self-Determination Theory — across the scam's three stages (hunting, raising/grooming, killing/harvesting), exploiting relationship-building and motivational tactics to gain trust. They propose a unified stage model to help law enforcement anticipate scams and design prevention targeted at the specific human vulnerabilities each stage exploits.
  • Source: https://academic.oup.com/cybersecurity/article/12/1/tyag003/8449214?searchresult=1

2. Experiencer, Helper, or Observer: online fraud intervention for older adults through role-based simulation (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Authors: Yue Deng, Xiaowei Chen, Junxiang Liao, Bo Li, Yixin Zou
  • Venue/date: Proceedings of CHI '26 (ACM), April 13–17, 2026, DOI 10.1145/3772318.3791003; arXiv:2601.12324 — peer-reviewed
  • Key finding: Playing the victim is not the only way to learn to spot fraud — playing the helper works too. ROLESafe put 144 older adults in China into Experiencer, Helper or Observer simulation roles. The Experiencer and Helper roles significantly improved participants' ability to identify online fraud; Helpers outperformed Observers (Z=2.63, adjusted P=.025). Simply watching (Observer) was the weakest active role — a direct argument for active, multi-perspective simulation over static warnings for older adults.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.12324

3. Blind giving predicts longitudinal increases in scam susceptibility in later life (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Authors: Belinda Y Zhang, Anya Samek, Cassidy P Molinare, Jenna Axelrod, Daisy T Noriega-Makarskyy, Emma Oyen, Melanie Leguizamon, Jordan T Williams, Laura Mosqueda, S Duke Han
  • Venue/date: Innovation in Aging (Oxford University Press), 2026, article igag118, DOI 10.1093/geroni/igag118; published October 7, 2026 — peer-reviewed (accepted manuscript / advance article)
  • Key finding: Being generously trusting may become a risk factor with age — not because of cognitive decline alone. In 116 cognitively unimpaired older adults (aged 51–83; 72% female) followed annually across three visits over two years, greater baseline "blind giving" in a behavioural economic task predicted increasing scam susceptibility over time among relatively older participants (interaction p=.022), while susceptibility stayed stable in relatively younger participants. The link held after adjusting for global cognition, socioeconomic status and race. The authors' takeaway: prevention should identify at-risk people before a scam, not only teach generic warnings.
  • Source: https://academic.oup.com/innovateage/advance-article/doi/10.1093/geroni/igag118/8876760?searchresult=1

4. Fraud-R1: a multi-round benchmark for assessing LLM robustness against augmented fraud and phishing inducements (PREPRINT)

  • Authors: Shu Yang, Shenzhe Zhu, Zeyu Wu, Keyu Wang, Junchi Yao, Junchao Wu, Lijie Hu, Mengdi Li, Derek F. Wong, Di Wang
  • Venue/date: arXiv:2502.12904, submitted Feb 18, 2025, revised May 26, 2025 — not peer-reviewed
  • Key finding: AI assistants themselves can be talked into fraud — especially when they are role-playing. Fraud-R1 is a benchmark of 8,564 fraud cases drawn from phishing scams, fake job postings, social media and news, in 5 major fraud types, tested over multiple rounds (credibility building, urgency creation, emotional manipulation). Evaluating 15 LLMs in helpful-assistant and role-play/persona settings, the authors found significant challenges defending against fraud inducement, with role-play settings and fake job postings the hardest, and a substantial performance gap between Chinese and English — a warning that multilingual fraud defence lags English defence.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.12904

5. The Human Vulnerabilities & Exploits (HVE) Framework (PREPRINT — framework paper)

  • Authors: Avichai Ben, Tom Rahav, Daniel Illaev, Aviv Nahon, Avi Grushka (Charm Security, per paper header)
  • Venue/date: arXiv:2606.10083, submitted June 8, 2026 — not peer-reviewed; framework/synthesis paper, not an experimental study
  • Key finding: Software bugs have CVE IDs and CVSS scores; human vulnerabilities exploited by scams have no equivalent — this paper proposes one. The HVE Framework provides a shared, machine-readable taxonomy with structured identifiers for behavioural/psychological vulnerabilities, a Human Vulnerability Severity Score (HVSS), and "Human Vulnerability Patches" for remediation guidance, synthesising dual-process theory, prospect theory, social-influence frameworks and visceral-state models. No headline accuracy number is reported; treat it as a proposed standard from an industry-affiliated team, not yet validated in the field.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.10083

6. Modeling behavioral signals in job scams: a human-centered security study (PREPRINT)

  • Authors: Goni Anagha, Vishakha Dasi Agrawal, Gargi Sarkar, Kavita Vemuri, Sandeep Kumar Shukla (IIIT Hyderabad, per paper header)
  • Venue/date: arXiv:2601.19342, submitted January 27, 2026 — not peer-reviewed; small exploratory survey (n=91 analysed), so percentages are indicative, not population estimates
  • Key finding: Urgency, not FOMO, is the trigger that most reliably predicts paying a job scammer. In a university-population survey, 37 participants (40.7%) reported encountering a suspicious job/internship offer in the past 12 months, and 16 of those (43.2%) reported making a payment; formal reporting was rare. Urgency/time-pressure cues were significantly associated with payment behaviour, while opportunity-loss/FOMO cues were not reliably identifiable, and social proof acted only as a context-dependent legitimacy cue. Payment-based scams (upfront fees) and task-based scams (small rewards, then escalating deposits) were the two dominant pathways — directly relevant to today's Vizag and UK job-text stories.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.19342

TODAY'S SAFETY TIP

Never pay a fee to "release" government money — and never enter a PIN to receive money. Monday's I4C advisory (Oct 19) described Facebook/Instagram ads using deepfake videos of real leaders announcing welfare schemes, then charging repeated "tax, processing and registration fees" and tricking people into approving UPI collect requests to "receive" a prize. Do this instead: (1) treat any video of an official announcing a cash scheme in an ad as fake until proven otherwise — check the scheme on the government department's official website, typed in yourself, never via the ad's WhatsApp number or link; (2) remember a payment PIN / approval is always for sending money — a real benefit, refund or prize never requires one to receive it; (3) if you already paid a "release fee," stop — further fees are the scam continuing, not bad luck — and report it to your bank and national cybercrime portal immediately.
