TOP STORIES (ranked by importance)

1. SEC charges eight entities over $37.4M fake crypto-platform fraud

  • Outlet: SEC (Press Release 2026-115), reported by Crypto.news
  • Date: November 19, 2026
  • Summary: The SEC charged eight Colorado-registered entities with defrauding more than 1,500 U.S. investors of at least $37.45 million through fake investment advisers and fake crypto trading platforms. The scheme ran from August 2024 to May 2026 as four rotating adviser–platform pairs (Advisor WeChat Capital/Tech Brokerage Asset Alliance, HB Wealth Advisors/IdeaCrypto, Convergent Wealth Advisors/RealSimple Crypto, Associated Wealth Advisors/Essential Crypto), each operating a few months before rebranding. Victims were lured through social media ads into group and private chats — including WhatsApp investment groups — then steered to platforms that showed fake rising balances while no trading took place.
  • Why it matters: A textbook pig-butchering playbook with a new twist: the operators forged SEC certificates and pointed skeptical victims at real EDGAR, Form ADV and FinCEN filings to manufacture legitimacy — the SEC has since removed those filings from its site. It shows even official-looking regulatory paperwork can be weaponized, and that "check the registration" is no longer sufficient due diligence on its own.
  • Source: https://crypto.news/sec-charges-eight-entities-37-million-crypto-fraud/

2. U.S. sanctions Cambodian and Burmese enablers of Southeast Asian scam centers

  • Outlet: U.S. Department of State (press statement, mirrored by U.S. Embassy in China); also reported by Blockonomi
  • Date: November 19, 2026
  • Summary: The U.S. sanctioned five individuals and entities that own, operate and enable cyber scam centers in Cambodia and Burma. Treasury's OFAC designated Cambodian operator Zhao Hui and two companies he controls, plus two senior Democratic Karen Benevolent Army commanders — Gen. Saw Shwe Wah and Col. Saw A One — accused of protecting scam compounds and facilitating trafficking of workers forced to run fraud schemes. The action was coordinated with the DOJ Scam Center Strike Force, FBI, DEA and Homeland Security, and supports Executive Order 14390 on combating cybercrime and fraud against Americans. Southeast Asia–based scam centers stole an estimated $18.2 billion from Americans in 2025.
  • Why it matters: Sanctions are now reaching beyond the scam compounds themselves to the armed groups that protect them and the landlords who lease them facilities — an escalation from takedowns to cutting off the physical and financial infrastructure that makes the compounds possible.
  • Source: https://china.usembassy-china.org.cn/targeting-cyber-scam-center-enablers-in-southeast-asia/

3. Trump administration launches Threat Disruption Center to choke off scam payments

  • Outlet: Cointelegraph (primary coverage; also Malwarebytes, Insurance Journal)
  • Date: Announced Wednesday, November 18, 2026; widely reported November 19, 2026
  • Summary: The Homeland Security Task Force's National Coordination Center — co-led by the Departments of Homeland Security and Justice — launched a Threat Disruption Center to stop money flowing to foreign criminal networks behind scams, cybercrime and sextortion. The center connects more than 30 federal agencies and 440+ state and local partners with banks and crypto platforms to pause victim payments, freeze funds, take down malicious domains and ban scam accounts in near real time. Blockchain intelligence firm TRM Labs is the primary private-sector partner, supplying its Ardence platform to map scam networks and identify victims. FBI data cited: $20.9 billion in reported online fraud losses in 2025.
  • Why it matters: This is the operational follow-through to the Scam Center Strike Force — a standing mechanism, not a one-off sweep, built on the premise that speed is everything: intervening while victims are still sending money, rather than investigating after the funds are gone. It is not a public recovery service; victims should still report to the FBI's IC3.
  • Source: https://cointelegraph.com/news/trump-administration-unveils-new-center-choke-off-scam-payments

4. Dutch gambling operators file EU complaint accusing Meta of failing to stop illegal gambling ads

  • Outlet: iGaming Times
  • Date: November 19, 2026 (VNLOK statement dated Nov 19)
  • Summary: VNLOK, the association of licensed Dutch online gambling operators, filed a Digital Services Act complaint with the European Commission alleging Meta breaches the DSA by doing too little against ads for illegal online gambling on Facebook and Instagram. Research it commissioned found 98.8% of gambling ads on Meta's platforms in June 2026 came from operators without a Dutch licence (95.5% in Oct 2025, 96% in May 2026), many using iDEAL payment branding, the Dutch flag, or deepfakes of well-known Dutch people, and leading to sites with no limits and no connection to CRUKS, the Dutch self-exclusion register. The complaint runs alongside a summons VNLOK served on Meta in Amsterdam District Court in September, and follows FDJ United's own court action against Meta in France four days earlier.
  • Why it matters: It reframes scam-ad enforcement as a systemic platform failure under EU law rather than a game of whack-a-mole with individual ads — and it comes with a number (98.8% unlicensed) that is hard for Meta to dismiss as a few bad actors slipping through. If the Commission opens formal proceedings, the outcome would apply EU-wide.
  • Source: https://igaming-times.com/news/regulatory/vnlok-european-commission-complaint-meta-gambling-ads

5. Meta and South African partners step up fight against online scams

  • Outlet: Sowetan (South Africa)
  • Date: November 19, 2026
  • Summary: Meta convened a closed-door policy roundtable in Johannesburg on November 12 with the Southern African Fraud Prevention Service, the Financial Sector Conduct Authority and the National Gambling Board, ahead of International Fraud Awareness Week (Nov 15–21), and launched an on-platform anti-scam awareness campaign in South Africa. The campaign teaches users to spot common tactics and use in-app reporting, strong passwords and two-factor authentication.
  • Why it matters: A reminder that the scam fight is increasingly local: Meta is pairing global takedowns with country-level partnerships and user education in markets where enforcement capacity is thinner and scam losses are rising fast.

NEW & EMERGING SCAMS

1. Paid search ads now lead shoppers straight to fake stores

  • Date: November 19, 2026 (FTC consumer alert)
  • How it works: The FTC warned that scammers buy paid search ads that appear at the top of results — or beside legitimate retailers — for holiday shopping searches. Clicking one lands on a convincing fake store built to steal payment details or take money for goods that never arrive. The FTC's advice: scroll past paid ads, check unfamiliar sellers, and type the retailer's address directly.
  • Who it targets: Holiday shoppers searching for deals on Google/Bing ahead of Black Friday (Nov 27).
  • Why it's new: This is the FTC's freshest holiday warning, dated the coverage day itself, and it targets the search box itself — the place shoppers trust most. It follows the FTC's Nov 2 alert on imposter ads across social feeds, search, and shopping platforms.
  • Source: https://consumer.ftc.gov/features/paid-search-ad-impersonation

2. "FaceStealer": AI deepfake video calls in a $400M crypto ring

  • Date: DOJ announcement November 12, 2026; fresh coverage November 16, 2026 — recent context, still fresh this week
  • How it works: The DOJ disrupted cloud infrastructure behind "FaceStealer," a North Korean operation that stole over $400 million by hijacking real video-call accounts (Telegram, LinkedIn, Slack, Discord) and using recorded faces and fake video meetings to deceive victims and their contacts into fraudulent crypto transfers. The FBI seized more than 900 victim webcam videos in the takedown. The same playbook now appears in AI-driven romance-investment ("pig-butchering") scams, where syndicates use agentic AI to run thousands of simultaneous conversations funneling victims into fake crypto platforms.
  • Who it targets: Crypto holders, professionals contacted via LinkedIn/Telegram/Discord, and romance-scam victims.
  • Why it's new: Deepfake video — not just voice or text — is now a core tool of large-scale fraud, and a state-linked ring industrialized it.
  • Source: https://www.avasc.org/briefings/fbi-breaks-up-400m-ai-deepfake-crypto-ring-imposters-persist-2026-11-16

3. Fake FDIC "agents" and bogus FDIC websites surge

  • Date: FDIC Office of Inspector General warning November 18, 2026
  • How it works: The FDIC OIG warned of a sharp increase in impersonation scams: victims get unsolicited calls, texts, letters, or emails claiming to be from the FDIC, the FDIC OIG, other law enforcement, or their bank. Victims are told their accounts are compromised or their bank is under investigation, then directed to move money to a purported "FDIC-managed" secure account — and sent to a fictitious website (with an "FDIC case number" and login) that mimics fdic.gov's look but does not end in .gov. The FDIC OIG's advice is blunt: "HALT!" — its agents will never ask for money or personal information by phone.
  • Who it targets: Bank customers, especially anyone rattled by real bank-outage news (Chase, BofA, U.S. Bank had service trouble Nov 16).
  • Why it's new: A regulator impersonation wave with a full fake-portal infrastructure — fake case numbers, fake account balances — timed to exploit a real bank outage.
  • Source: https://www.fdicoig.gov/news/oig-issued-press-releases/fdic-oig-alerts-consumers-and-banks-about-impersonation-scams

4. Phishing lures now hide inside images, canvas, and SVG QR codes

  • Date: Palo Alto Networks Unit 42 report November 4, 2026
  • How it works: Unit 42 reported three graphics-based phishing campaigns tracked May–October 2026 (1,740 detections total) that render their lures as graphics instead of HTML text, defeating conventional DOM-based inspection: (1) full phishing verification cards delivered as a single hosted image (1,188 detections); (2) fake financial documents/receipts drawn on HTML5 canvas (361); (3) QR codes assembled from SVG vector paths and coordinate transforms (191) — the last impersonating the MAX Messenger web-login experience to harvest account access via a QR-code sign-in.
  • Who it targets: Employees and consumers receiving "account verification" emails; MAX Messenger users in the SVG campaign.
  • Why it's new: Attackers are rebuilding phishing pages so there's almost no readable text for security filters to catch — the malicious content exists only as pixels and vector shapes. The QR-code-as-SVG twist also weaponizes the familiar "scan to log in" flow.
  • Source: https://www.threatbeaconx.com/threat-intelligence/three-phishing-campaigns-hide-malicious-lures-in-images-canvas-and-svg-qr-codes

5. Digital gift cards drained before they're ever used

  • Date: November 16, 2026 reports
  • How it works: Dozens of Woolworths customers in Australia reported their digital Everyday WISH gift cards were spent by someone else before the purchaser ever used them, with losses up to $500 per card and purchases appearing at stores in different states. Complaints first surfaced on OzBargain before spreading to Facebook and Reddit. In the same week, New Zealand's MBIE warned Kiwis lost $68 million to online shopping fraud over the past year. Norton's 2026 Holiday Report found nearly 1 in 6 US adults (16%) have been scammed while holiday shopping online, with 88% of victims losing money (average $394).
  • Who it targets: Gift-card buyers and holiday shoppers; digital gift cards are the weak point.
  • Why it's new: A fresh, concrete holiday-season incident showing gift-card balances can be stolen in transit — no phishing link needed — landing during International Fraud Awareness Week.
  • Source: https://scamwatchhq.com/holiday-shopping-scams-2026-watch-fraud-awareness-week-highlights-gift/

6. Jury-duty phone scams and health-plan texts ride official-looking numbers — developing

  • Date: November 18–19, 2026
  • How it works: Police in Toms River, NJ and Glasgow, KY warned on Nov 19 of a jury-duty phone scam wave — callers spoof police numbers and demand payment for missed jury duty. Separately, polished health-plan scam texts are arriving from official-looking short 5–6 digit numbers during open enrollment (reported Nov 18, WSMV Nashville).

RESEARCH DESK

1. Phishing can evade email reporting systems by exploiting tracking pixels (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Title: Doubly Dangerous: Evading Phishing Reporting Systems by Leveraging Email Tracking Techniques
  • Authors: Anish Chand (Louisiana State University), Nick Nikiforakis (Stony Brook University), Phani Vadrevu (Louisiana State University)
  • Venue / Date: Peer-reviewed — 34th USENIX Security Symposium (USENIX Security '25), August 13–15, 2025, Seattle, WA — pp. 3181–3200
  • Key finding: The researchers repurposed email-tracking techniques to profile and evade the anti-phishing reporting systems of all major email services they tested — including Gmail and Microsoft Outlook — affecting more than 2 billion users worldwide. In plain terms: a scammer can tell which email provider a target uses just from how the provider's servers handle tracking pixels, then serve a phishing page tuned to slip past that provider's reporting/detection. The authors disclosed the flaws to the providers, which led to remedial changes and a vulnerability reward.
  • Source: https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity25/presentation/chand

2. Almost nobody spots a QR-code scam — only 13% catch it (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Title: Scanned and Scammed: Insecurity by ObsQRity? Measuring User Susceptibility and Awareness of QR Code-Based Attacks
  • Authors: Marvin Kowalewski, Leona Lassak (Ruhr University Bochum), Markus Dürmuth (Leibniz University Hannover), Theodor Schnitzler (Maastricht University)
  • Venue / Date: Peer-reviewed — 34th USENIX Security Symposium (USENIX Security '25), August 13–15, 2025, Seattle, WA
  • Key finding: Across three experiments with 1,876 participants simulating everyday payments and shopping, almost no one spotted a QR-code-based attack — even though several participants did catch traditional phishing. Only 13% recognized a fraudulent QR payment request, compared with 46% who spotted the fraud when payment details were typed in manually. In plain terms: hiding a scam link behind a QR code makes it roughly three times harder for people to notice, because the destination is invisible until scanned.
  • Source: https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity25/presentation/kowalewski

3. Scammers hook victims through five emotions — fear, guilt, trust, hope, belonging (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Title: "It didn't feel right but I needed a job so desperately": Understanding People's Emotions & Help Needs During Financial Scams
  • Authors: Jake Chanenson (Google / University of Chicago), Tara Matthews, Sunny Consolvo, Patrick Gage Kelley, Abhishek Roy, Renee Shelby, Kurt Thomas (Google), Jessica McClearn (Google / Royal Holloway), Sarah Meiklejohn (Google / UCL), Amelia Hassoun (Google / University of Cambridge)
  • Venue / Date: Peer-reviewed — Proceedings of the 2026 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems (CHI '26), April 13–17, 2026, Barcelona, Spain — DOI: 10.1145/3772318.3790556
  • Key finding: Analyzing 405 Reddit posts from people seeking help during scams across 12 scam types, the researchers found scammers hook victims through five emotional motivations — Fear, Guilt & Goodness, Trust, Hope, and Belonging — e.g., hope for a job in a tough economy. Financial insecurity and legal precarity raised the risk of engaging and being harmed. The paper maps four help needs (sensemaking, guidance, therapeutic support, external action) to each stage of a scam, arguing interventions must meet people where they are emotionally, not just warn them. Context: FTC-reported fraud losses exceeded $12.5 billion in 2024.
  • Source: https://storage.googleapis.com/gweb-research2023-stg-media/pubtools/1036464.pdf

4. We can no longer tell fake videos from real ones — humans barely beat a coin flip (PREPRINT)

  • Title: DF26: We Cannot Tell Fake From Real Anymore
  • Authors: Severyn Shykula, Andrii Yermakov, Ivan Samarskyi, Dmytro Mishkin, Jan Cech, Anastasiia Mishchuk
  • Venue / Date: Preprint — not yet peer-reviewed. arXiv:2609.07369 — submitted September 7, 2026; revised November 19, 2026 (v2, i.e., the coverage date itself)
  • Key finding: On a new benchmark of 271 real and 2,420 synthetic videos of people speaking to camera, generated by seven modern text-to-video and image-to-video models, human volunteers correctly identified AI-generated videos only 52.6% of the time — barely better than a coin flip — while recognizing real videos 76.0% of the time. State-of-the-art deepfake detectors performed close to random chance on this setup. In plain terms: with current AI video generators, neither people nor today's detectors can reliably tell a fake video of someone speaking from a real one.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.07369

5. LLMs now run "phishing-as-a-service" at scale within minutes (PREPRINT)

  • Title: SoK: Large Language Model-Generated Textual Phishing Campaigns — End-to-End Analysis of Generation, Characteristics, and Detection
  • Authors: Fengchao Chen, Tingmin Wu, Van Nguyen, Carsten Rudolph
  • Venue / Date: Preprint — not yet peer-reviewed. arXiv:2508.21457 — submitted August 29, 2025 (v1); latest version August 29, 2026 (v4)
  • Key finding: The first systematization of knowledge (SoK) on LLM-generated phishing, covering the full attack lifecycle. Its core point: LLMs let attackers run "phishing-as-a-service" at scale within minutes, producing fluent, tailored messages far more cheaply than human-written campaigns, and the paper's Generation–Characterization–Defense framework shows LLM phishing differs from traditional phishing in how it is generated, what it looks like, and how it evades detection — so defenses tuned for old-style spam miss it. This is a synthesis/review of the field, not a single new experiment, so it reports no single headline number.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.21457

TODAY'S SAFETY TIP

Don't trust the paperwork — verify the person and the money yourself. Today's lead story shows scammers forging SEC certificates and pointing victims at real government filings to look legitimate, and the FTC warns that paid search ads at the top of your results may be fake stores. So: before you invest or buy, check the seller through a channel you open yourself — type the regulator's or retailer's address into your browser, call a number from the official site (never from the message), and log in to your own bank or payment app to confirm money actually arrived before handing anything over. Official-looking documents, top search results, and payment screenshots are all trivially faked; the only check that counts is one you run yourself.
