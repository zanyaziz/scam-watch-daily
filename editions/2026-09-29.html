Lead

DOJ's new National Fraud Enforcement Division debuts with a $1.3M Social Security fraud surge — while the FBI warns that the old "call the office back" verification trick no longer defeats agent-impersonation scams. Federal prosecutors charged 17 people across 11 districts in the new division's first major action; separately, FBI Newark says impersonators now spoof real field-office numbers, use real agents' names, and stage fake FBI backdrops on video calls. The SEC also hit overseas operators behind $15M in WhatsApp "investment confidence" scams, and Amazon Prime members are facing a billing-hold phishing wave exploiting FTC refund news.

3. FBI Newark warns of "insidious new twist" on federal-agent impersonation fraud — callback verification can be defeated

  • Outlet: FBI Newark field office (official press release)
  • Date: September 29, 2026
  • Summary: Scammers posing as FBI, DEA, and Secret Service agents now spoof real field-office phone numbers and use the names of actual agents — so victims who call the office back to "verify" are told the agent exists, turning the standard verification advice into part of the deception. One fraudster staged a fake FBI office on a video meeting, complete with backdrop, seal, and suit. The FBI's updated guidance: state on any callback that you may be a scam target, record video meetings, and call 1-800-CALL-FBI.
  • Why it matters: This upgrades a routine impersonation scam into something that defeats the textbook defense. If you've taught family members to "call back the office," that advice needs an update today.
  • Source: https://www.fbi.gov/contact-us/field-offices/newark/news/fbi-newark-issues-warning-about-insidious-new-twist-on-an-old-fraud-scheme

4. "QR Jacking": abandoned branded QR domains can be hijacked to redirect genuine scans

  • What's new: Researcher Farzan Karimi found QR Tiger's "Own Short Domain" feature doesn't verify domain ownership — if a company stops using QR Tiger but leaves its DNS record in place, anyone can claim that subdomain and redirect every already-printed QR code (packaging, signage, flyers) to a phishing site while phones still show the company's real domain. Hundreds of exposed subdomains were found; the flaw was reported to QR Tiger around April 2026 and remains unpatched.
  • Who flagged it: Cyber Insider / SC Media coverage of independent research, September 28, 2026.
  • Why it matters: It's retroactive: existing printed codes turn malicious with no reprint and no way for consumers to tell. For businesses: audit your DNS for stale vendor records.
  • Source: https://cyberinsider.com/hackers-can-hijack-qr-code-domains-to-redirect-users-to-phishing-sites/

RESEARCH DESK (2025–2026 research; preprints labeled)

1. Sociotechnical framework for QR-code phishing — "quishing" (peer-reviewed)

  • Title: "Quishing: A Sociotechnical Framework for Understanding QR-Code Phishing Risks and User-Centered Protection Strategies"
  • Authors: Pedro-David Filio-Aguilar, Rubén Mil-Martínez, Lourdes López-García (Escuela Militar de Ingeniería / Tecnológico Nacional de México)
  • Venue/date: Journal of Cybersecurity and Privacy 2026, 6(4), 134 (received 19 Jun 2026, accepted 5 Aug 2026, published 8 Aug 2026; DOI 10.3390/jcp6040134)
  • Key finding: Synthesis of 27 QR-code-phishing studies (drawn from 71 records; 19 high-quality, 8 moderate; mean quality score 10.04/12). Quishing succeeds through contextual legitimacy (trust inherited from a physical object or document), routine scanning behavior, destination invisibility, and insufficient verification. The authors' protection framework is conceptual/heuristic — not yet empirically validated.
  • Source: https://www.mdpi.com/2624-800X/6/4/134

2. Automated engagement of 1,900+ job scammers traces millions in crypto losses (PREPRINT — not peer-reviewed)

  • Title: "Anansi: Scalable Characterization of Message-Based Job Scams"
  • Authors: Abisheka Pitumpe, Amir Rahmati (Stony Brook University)
  • Venue/date: arXiv:2602.24223, submitted 27 Feb 2026 — preprint
  • Key finding: An automated pipeline using LLMs plus browser agents engaged more than 1,900 job scammers and collected 29,000+ scam messages, tracing "millions of dollars" in crypto losses. The most common fraudulent task (67.51%) was product-review manipulation; heavy reuse of templates, domains, and wallets was observed (262 BTC and 209 ETH addresses, 205/149 unique after deduplication).
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.24223

3. Economic model of which interventions survive AI-enhanced scams (PREPRINT — not peer-reviewed)

  • Title: "Effective Interventions Against AI-Enhanced Scams"
  • Authors: Kyle Fredrickson
  • Venue/date: arXiv:2609.00806, submitted 1 Sep 2026 — preprint
  • Key finding: An economic model of anti-scam intervention in the AI era. Context figures: an estimated $442B in direct global scam losses in 2025, with U.S. reported losses up nearly 400% between 2020 and 2025. Three levers — reporting rate, centralization of reporting, and report accuracy — multiply together in their effect on expected victims per scam channel, reducing scammer revenue while raising their costs.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.00806

4. LLM-based multi-agent counter-scam response system (PREPRINT — not peer-reviewed)

  • Title: "An LLM-based Chain-of-Response Counter-Scam System"
  • Authors: Heedou Kim et al.
  • Venue/date: arXiv:2606.01475, submitted 31 May 2026 — preprint
  • Key finding: A multi-agent LLM response framework built on 185,300 scam cases and 38,587 knowledge entries (with input from 37 stakeholders). Fine-tuned small LLMs beat commercial models by more than 10% on all nine role-aligned tasks, and scam-specific named-entity recognition improved by 0.24 F1.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.01475

5. Minor transcript wording changes push deepfake-speech detector bypass past 60% (PREPRINT — not peer-reviewed)

  • Title: "What You Read Isn't What You Hear: Linguistic Sensitivity in Deepfake Speech Detection"
  • Authors: Binh Nguyen, Shuji Shi, Ryan Ofman, Thai Le
  • Venue/date: arXiv:2505.17513, submitted 23 May 2025 — preprint
  • Key finding: Minor changes to transcript wording — not audio manipulation — pushed attack success rates past 60% against several open-source detector-voice pairs; one commercial detector collapsed from 100% to 32% accuracy. A replication of the Brad Pitt audio deepfake scam fully bypassed commercial detectors.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.17513

6. Position paper: the field built detectors for a deepfake threat that didn't arrive (PREPRINT — not peer-reviewed)

  • Title: "The Deepfakes We Missed: We Built Detectors for a Threat That Didn't Arrive"
  • Authors: Shaina Raza (Vector Institute)
  • Venue/date: arXiv:2605.12075, submitted 12 May 2026 — position paper, preprint
  • Key finding: An evidence-backed argument that the field prepared for the wrong threat: incident accounting from 2022–2026 shows the dominant real harms are peer-generated non-consensual intimate imagery, voice-clone scam calls targeting families and finance workers, and emotional-manipulation fraud — while the predicted large-scale public-figure deepfake catastrophe never arrived in 2024. Label as a position paper, not an empirical study.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.12075

TODAY'S SAFETY TIP

If someone calls claiming to be a federal agent, hang up — and update your "call back to verify" playbook. FBI Newark warns that impersonators now spoof real field-office numbers and use real agents' names, so calling the number they give you — or even the office number you look up — isn't a sure defense: the person who picks up may still be the scammer. The upgraded drill: hang up, then call 1-800-CALL-FBI from a number you look up yourself; if you call a field office back, open with "I may be talking to a scam target" and assume nothing until a real case file is referenced. And real agents never demand payment by wire, gift card, or crypto on a call — that is an instant hang-up signal. Pair this with the QR-code lesson from today's emerging scams: preview any link before you scan or click.
