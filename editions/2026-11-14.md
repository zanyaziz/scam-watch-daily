## TOP STORIES (ranked by importance)

### 1. Italy fines Meta €805 million ($887M) over Facebook's access to Android users' photos without consent
- **Outlet:** Reuters (via republication)
- **Date:** November 14, 2026 (verified — Reuters dateline "MILAN, Nov 14")
- **Summary:** Italy's data protection authority fined Meta Platforms €805 million ($887 million) after finding Facebook accessed Android users' photographs and videos without obtaining their consent or informing them. The access came through a Facebook feature, introduced in 2023 and enabled by default, that suggested content from users' personal galleries for posting. Around 20 million Android users in Italy were affected. Meta was given 60 days to disable the feature by default and seek separate consent; a Meta spokesperson said the company would appeal, calling the fine "disproportionate."
- **Why it matters:** One of the largest fines ever imposed on a tech company by an Italian authority, it strikes at how platforms quietly harvest private photo libraries — the same personal imagery scammers and impersonators exploit. It also signals regulators are now scrutinizing gallery-access features separately from AI training, with a new cloud-processing feature already flagged for possible further action.
- **Source:** https://cruisin929.com/2026/11/14/italy-fines-meta-887-million-over-android-users-data-access/

### 2. DOJ moves to seize $1.2 billion in stolen Binance crypto and takes down North Korea's $400M "FaceStealer" deepfake scheme
- **Outlet:** U.S. Department of Justice (press release); reported by CybersecurityDive, Cointelegraph
- **Date:** November 12, 2026 (DOJ announcement) / reported November 13, 2026 — **late-breaking story from Nov 13, included as the day's big enforcement news**
- **Summary:** The Justice Department announced court authorization to seize or restrain more than $1.2 billion in cryptocurrency stolen from Binance in the October 29 hack, attributed to North Korea's TraderTraitor unit, which stole roughly $6.3 billion in total; over $2.7 billion has now been frozen with private-sector help. In a parallel action, the FBI seized cloud infrastructure behind "FaceStealer," a North Korean social-engineering scheme that used stolen webcam footage and AI deepfakes of trusted contacts on fake Zoom, Teams and Google Meet calls to trick crypto and Web3 workers into installing malware — stealing over $400 million in 18 months. The FBI seized more than 900 webcam videos of victims and published a public alert and resource page at fbi.gov/facestealer.
- **Why it matters:** FaceStealer is the most sophisticated video-call impersonation scam yet documented at state scale — it weaponizes deepfakes and hijacked identities on the very meeting platforms people trust at work, and has now expanded from crypto firms to open-source software maintainers. The $1.2 billion seizure is also one of the largest crypto recoveries ever.
- **Source:** https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/justice-department-announces-actions-disrupt-north-koreas-massive-theft-cryptocurrency

### 3. DOJ's Scam Center Strike Force marks one year: $1 billion in crypto restrained, 17 million scam accounts deplatformed
- **Outlet:** U.S. Attorney's Office for the District of Columbia (via WLT Report)
- **Date:** November 14, 2026 (WLT report; anniversary totals posted by the U.S. Attorney's Office on Friday, Nov 13)

Pirro also announced a new memorandum of understanding with Singapore on combating online scams, cyber fraud and money laundering, as the networks spread into Africa and Europe.
- **Why it matters:** The first-year totals give the clearest public measure yet of the industrial scale of scam compounds — and of enforcement finally treating them as organized crime rather than isolated complaints. The Singapore pact and the Africa pivot show the fight is following the networks as they relocate.

### 5. Cambodia admits it should have regulated crypto sooner as scam networks outlive the compound crackdowns

## NEW & EMERGING SCAMS

### 1. AI voice-cloned "kidnapped child / cartel" panic calls target parents — developing
- **Date:** Instagram video posted November 12, 2026; Victoria Police warning shared November 13, 2026
- **How it works:** A mother received a call in which a crying voice sounding exactly like her son said "Mom, I got into an accident," before a second voice claimed to be working with a cartel and warned her not to hang up or call police or she would never see her child again. She hung up, called the school, and confirmed her children were safe. Hundreds of commenters reported nearly identical calls. A separate Victoria Police warning shared November 13 describes the same pattern — a mother's voice cloned from a short sample to demand money — and notes fraudsters can clone a voice from as little as a 10-second sample.
- **Who it targets:** Parents and grandparents. The cloned crying child's voice creates instant panic; the "don't hang up" instruction stops victims from verifying.
- **Why it's new:** The panic-call format is old, but AI voice cloning makes the impersonation convincing from a few seconds of audio scraped from social media. The defense repeated across the posts: agree on a family safe word or code word in advance — and always hang up and call back on a number you know.
- **Source:** https://www.instagram.com/reel/Dfn0XQWqxu-/
- **Note:** Developing — based on a social-media account corroborated by multiple similar reports in comments and a police warning; the underlying calls are not independently verified case by case.

### 2. Phishing riding the Microsoft 365 outage — fake "messages held" emails
- **Date:** Warning flagged publicly November 14, 2026; outage began November 12, 2026
- **How it works:** Within two days of the real Microsoft 365 disruption (affecting Outlook, Teams, SharePoint and Copilot), scammers began sending fake emails styled with the Microsoft logo claiming "(4) messages held after the outage" and warning they will be deleted within 24 hours unless restored via a "RESTORE MESSAGES" button. The link leads to a credential-harvesting page.
- **Who it targets:** Microsoft 365 users, especially at work. The outage makes the message plausible — recipients genuinely had missing mail — and the 24-hour deletion threat creates urgency to click before checking with IT.
- **Why it's new:** Same outage, new channel: yesterday's edition covered the fake support calls; today the phishing emails arrived. Check outage status only through the Microsoft 365 admin centre or the official service-health page — never through a link in an email.
- **Source:** https://www.instagram.com/p/DfumaD2GDAz/
- **Note:** Developing — social-media warning about an in-progress phishing wave; the underlying outage is confirmed.

### 3. Quishing: fake "Carousell invoice" QR codes drain sellers (S$180,000+ lost)
- **Date:** Singapore Police Force advisory figures as of October 23, 2026; write-up updated November 2026 — **article date unverified beyond "November 2026"**
- **How it works:** An eager "buyer" agrees to full asking price, then sends an official-looking PDF titled "Carousell Payment Invoice & Disbursement Order" telling the seller to scan an embedded QR code to "authorize" their bank account so payment can clear. Scanning leads to a replica site mimicking Carousell's branding that harvests banking credentials. According to a joint Singapore Police Force advisory, as of October 23, 2026 at least 63 victims had fallen for this variant, with losses of at least S$180,000 since July 2026. The same QR-code trick is also appearing as fake stickers placed over real codes on parking meters, EV chargers and restaurant tables, leading to look-alike payment pages.
- **Who it targets:** People selling items on marketplaces (and drivers paying for parking). Sellers are told they must scan a code or click a link to *receive* money — a step a real platform never requires.
- **Why it's new:** The QR code hides the destination URL, so it slips past both email filters and the victim's own inspection. The rule is absolute: you never have to scan anything or click a link to receive a payment.
- **Source:** https://www.singaporefiguredout.com/2026/11/selling-on-carousell-before-you-leave.html

### 4. Fake job interviews that install malware — "AccuraSoftHire" and the Binance attack
- **Date:** The Hindu BusinessLine, November 11, 2026 (JFrog research; Binance disclosure November 3, 2026)
- **How it works:** Security firm JFrog exposed a fake hiring operation called "AccuraSoftHire" that targets software developers with a polished decoy company — careers site, client testimonials, LinkedIn page, job ads on mainstream boards, ID-verified recruiters. Applicants are asked to complete a take-home test and install material that delivers malware. The same fake-interview playbook was behind the October 29, 2026 attack on Binance: an engineer approached on a legitimate job platform in September was put through a live video interview and a chat-app "interview" with a fake CEO, then asked to download a file; Binance disclosed on November 3, 2026 that about $6.34 billion in digital assets were impacted. The FBI attributes the Binance attack to North Korea.
- **Who it targets:** Job seekers, especially developers. The interview builds trust over weeks, then the "assessment" step is the malware delivery. The FBI's IC3 also describes fake online job interviews using voice spoofing or possible voice deepfakes where lip movements don't match the audio.
- **Why it's new:** The hiring operation is a complete, professional-looking fake company — not a sloppy listing — and the same lure opened one of the largest crypto thefts ever. A real employer never asks you to install software or download files as part of an interview.
- **Source:** https://www.thehindubusinessline.com/info-tech/jfrog-flags-fake-job-interview-scam-masquerading-as-hiring-platforms/article71688283.ece

### 5. Holiday smishing surge — fake package-delivery texts
- **Date:** Darkside, November 12, 2026; Better Business Bureau warning November 15, 2026
- **How it works:** Fake delivery texts — "your package couldn't be delivered," "incomplete address," or a small $1–$3 "redelivery fee" — are surging as holiday shopping ramps up, because in November and December almost everyone has a package in transit. The FTC found fake package-delivery alerts were the single most-reported type of text scam in 2024, and consumers lost an estimated $470 million to all text scams that year.
- **Who it targets:** Anyone expecting deliveries. The text carries a link to a look-alike carrier page (USPS, FedEx, UPS) that harvests card numbers and personal details. Real carriers never text unsolicited delivery-problem notices demanding payment, and USPS messages you initiate yourself contain no link.
- **Why it's new:** A seasonal surge of a known scam, now amplified by the holiday shopping rush. Delete the text, forward it to 7726, and track parcels only in the carrier's own app or site.
- **Source:** https://www.darkside.io/articles/fake-package-delivery-texts-how-the-holiday-smishing-scam-works-and-how-to-spot-it

### 6. AI-built fake Black Friday stores — 10,000-domain counterfeit network
- **Date:** ScamDrill, November 9, 2026 (reporting NordVPN's November 4 announcement and Savi Security's October 20 report)
- **How it works:** NordVPN's threat intelligence team traced more than 10,000 counterfeit shop domains to a single cloned template impersonating 166 fashion and luxury brands across 26 countries (most commonly Adidas, Ralph Lauren, Dr. Martens, Crocs, Massimo Dutti, Birkenstock, Stradivarius); as of November 3, 1,549 stores were fully active. The stores use near-identical domain tricks (an extra "s," a hyphen, a country suffix), display PayPal/Klarna/Visa/Mastercard logos but don't actually accept PayPal or Klarna — they just collect card details. Separately, Savi Security's Scamwise 2026 holiday report (October 20, 2026) says users report a new fake store roughly every 3 minutes (~500 a day), fake holiday-themed stores grew 5x in 60 days, and 85% of fake stores disappear within two weeks. Perplexity reportedly recommended two of the counterfeit shops as trustworthy.
- **Who it targets:** Holiday shoppers clicking paid ads, sponsored search results, or AI-assistant recommendations for brand-name goods at steep discounts. The stores look professional, use AI-generated photos and text, and vanish before chargebacks catch up.
- **Why it's new:** Polish is no longer proof: AI writes flawless shop text, realistic product photos, and fake reviews, and the stores are promoted through real ads — and even surfaced by AI assistants. Pay by credit card, never by bank transfer, gift card, or crypto, and check the seller and domain before paying, not the look of the site.
- **Source:** https://scamdrill.com/blog/fake-store-imposter-ads-black-friday-2026

## RESEARCH DESK

### 1. Novices with AI can build a full phishing campaign — and a detector that catches the jailbreaks (PREPRINT)
- **Title:** Jailbreaking Generative AI: Multivector Phishing Threats and Transformer-Based Defenses
- **Authors:** Rina Mishra, Gaurav Varshney (IIT Jammu)
- **Venue / Date:** Preprint — not yet peer-reviewed. arXiv:2507.12185, submitted July 2025
- **Key finding:** Novices jailbreaking ChatGPT-4o-Mini with role-play prompts could generate complete phishing campaigns across email, web, SMS, and voice, including credential-harvesting pages. In controlled lab tests, GenAI assistance raised novices' perceived phishing competence by 240% and task completion by 400%, while cutting implementation time by 57%; GenAI-generated phishing content compromised 25% of security-aware participants. On defense, the authors' XLNet-based detector identified malicious jailbreak prompts with an F1-score of 0.9864.
- **Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.12185

### 2. An AI agent can run a whole scam call by itself (PREPRINT / WORKSHOP)
- **Title:** ScamAgents: How AI Agents Can Simulate Human-Level Scam Calls
- **Authors:** Sanket Badhe (Rutgers University)
- **Venue / Date:** Preprint — arXiv:2508.06457, submitted 8 Aug 2025, revised 9 Dec 2025; also in the Conference on Applied Machine Learning for Information Security (CAMLIS) 2025 workshop proceedings
- **Key finding:** An autonomous LLM agent ("ScamAgent") with dialogue memory, goal decomposition, and text-to-speech can run realistic multi-turn scam calls — medical-insurance, impersonation, prize/lottery, and government-benefit scams — without human input. Current LLM guardrails, built for single-turn prompts, are ineffective against this kind of attack: splitting the scam across turns with role-play framing and subgoal decomposition bypasses refusals and content filters, producing a fully automated voice-scam pipeline. No numeric success rates are reported in the abstract.
- **Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.06457

### 3. An AI that detects scams live mid-conversation — and baits the scammer (PREPRINT)
- **Title:** AI-in-the-Loop: Privacy Preserving Real-Time Scam Detection and Conversational Scambaiting by Leveraging LLMs and Federated Learning
- **Authors:** Ismail Hossain, Sai Puppala, Md Jahangir Alam, Sajedul Talukder
- **Venue / Date:** Preprint — arXiv:2509.05362, submitted 4 Sep 2025; authors note acceptance at the 26th Privacy Enhancing Technologies Symposium (PETS 2026)
- **Key finding:** Instead of only warning after the fact, this system detects scams live mid-conversation and "scam-baits" — keeping the scammer engaged while protecting the victim. It stayed fluent and engaging (engagement ≈0.80, relevance ≈0.74, perplexity as low as 22.3) while keeping leaked personal information extremely low (PII leakage ≤0.0085). Using federated learning, models kept working across up to 30 training rounds without sharing raw conversation data, and adding differential privacy barely hurt performance. The trade-off the authors flag: stricter safety filters cut personal-data exposure but also shorten the baiting conversation, reducing detection.
- **Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.05362

### 4. Phishing in your own language hits harder — even among highly literate people (PEER-REVIEWED)
- **Title:** Linguistic Hooks: Investigating the Role of Language Triggers in Phishing Emails Targeting African Refugees and Students
- **Authors:** Mythili Menon, Nisha Vinayaga-Sureshkanth, Alec Schon, Kaitlyn Hemberger, Murtuza Jadliwala (Wichita State, UT San Antonio, University of Kansas)
- **Venue / Date:** Peer-reviewed — Proceedings on Privacy Enhancing Technologies (PoPETs) 2026(1), pp. 201–223
- **Key finding:** In a real-world phishing deception study of recently resettled African refugees (n=19), African immigrant students (n=142), and US-born students (n=184), 9.2% of phishing emails (68 of 736) led to link clicks, and data disclosure remained common even among high-literacy groups. Language mattered sharply: Kiswahili emails were read less often (only 25.5% of 568 delivered were opened) but once read, 24.8% (36 of 145) led to clicks — 6.3% of all delivered. Paradoxically, low English literacy partly shielded refugees from email phishing (they often ignored the emails entirely), while trust built through workshops could lead them to share personal information. Digital-literacy training improved awareness but did not eliminate susceptibility.
- **Source:** https://petsymposium.org/popets/2026/popets-2026-0011.pdf

### 5. AI-written phishing warnings protect about as well as expert-written ones (PREPRINT)
- **Title:** Can Large Language Models Automate Phishing Warning Explanations? A Controlled Experiment on Effectiveness and User Perception
- **Authors:** Federico Maria Cau, Giuseppe Desolda, Francesco Greco, Lucio Davide Spano, Luca Viganò
- **Venue / Date:** Preprint — not yet peer-reviewed. arXiv:2507.07916, submitted 10 Jul 2025, revised 15 Dec 2025
- **Key finding:** In a large between-subjects study (N=750), phishing warnings with explanations written by LLMs (Claude 3.5 Sonnet, Llama 3.3 70B) protected users about as well as warnings written by human experts. For real phishing emails, feature-based explanations (pointing at the suspicious features) held click-through to 11.03%, vs. 12.04% for counterfactual-style explanations; Claude's feature-based explanations did best at 7.48%. But not all LLMs helped equally: Llama 3.3's explanations were rated clearer yet did not reduce clicks the way Claude's did — a reminder that a warning that reads well is not necessarily one that changes behavior.
- **Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.07916

## TODAY'S SAFETY TIP

**Set a family safe word — and verify any urgent call or video call through a second channel.** Today's news shows why: North Korea's FaceStealer scheme used AI deepfakes of trusted contacts on real Zoom, Teams and Google Meet calls to steal $400 million, and parents are now getting AI-cloned "kidnapped child" panic calls built from a few seconds of audio. A crying voice that sounds exactly like your child, or a familiar face on a video call, is no longer proof of who is on the other end. Agree on a safe word with your family today — something only you would know — and make it a rule: any urgent request for money or secrecy gets verified by hanging up and calling the person back on a number you already have. The same "verify it yourself" rule applies to the Meta fine: check which apps can access your photo library in your phone's settings, and turn off gallery access for any app that doesn't need it.
