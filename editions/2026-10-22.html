Rhode Island sues Meta over scam ads — the first state case of its kind.

Lead

Rhode Island sues Meta over scam ads — the first state case of its kind. Attorney General Peter Neronha sued Meta on Thursday, alleging the company profits from and helps create scam advertising on Facebook, Instagram and WhatsApp — citing internal documents that Meta displays around 15 billion "higher risk" scam ads a day and earned roughly $16 billion, about 10% of its 2024 revenue, from fraudulent marketing. The same day, a UAE-led operation across 30 countries with INTERPOL, Meta and TikTok said it identified $39 billion in scam-linked funds, the FTC filed two impersonation cases and warned scammers are now impersonating the FTC itself, and Amazon asked courts to stop trademark lawsuits allegedly being used to unlock frozen seller funds.

TOP STORIES (ranked by importance)

1. Rhode Island sues Meta over alleged tolerance for scam advertising

  • Outlet: Reuters
  • Date: October 22, 2026 — verified (Reuters dateline Oct 22; suit filed Thursday, Oct 22, in Providence Superior Court)
  • Summary: Rhode Island Attorney General Peter Neronha sued Meta Platforms in state court, accusing it of unlawfully profiting from and participating in creating scam advertising on Facebook, Instagram and WhatsApp while assuring users it was protecting them. The complaint, citing internal Meta documents first reported by Reuters, alleges Meta displays around 15 billion "higher risk" scam ads a day and earned roughly 10% of its 2024 revenue — about $16 billion — from fraudulent e-commerce, dubious health products and other banned marketing, sometimes charging suspected fraudsters higher ad rates instead of blocking them and using its AI ad tools to help optimise their ads. Examples include more than 2,000 versions of an ad promising to reverse Alzheimer's in 45 days, and ads impersonating a senior Bank of America executive that lured users into WhatsApp stock-trading scams.
  • Why it matters: The first scam-ads case brought against Meta by a U.S. state (following Santa Clara County's May 2026 suit). It seeks civil penalties per violation, restitution, disgorgement and a court order requiring Meta to address rather than profit from scam ads — a direct legal test of platform liability for paid scam advertising, with an internal Meta review quoted in the suit concluding "It is easier to advertise scams on Meta platforms than Google." Meta called the suit "unfounded," said it would fight it, and said it regularly partners with law enforcement.
  • Source: https://www.reuters.com/world/rhode-island-sues-meta-over-alleged-tolerance-scam-advertising-2026-10-22/

2. UAE-led Operation Confrontation Ghost: 30 countries, INTERPOL, Meta and TikTok target AI-powered fraud — $39 billion in scam-linked funds identified

  • Outlet: PR Newswire via Morningstar (UAE Ministry of Interior release)
  • Date: October 22, 2026 — verified (dateline Vienna, Oct 22, 2026; results unveiled Oct 22 at UNTOC COP13 in Vienna. The operation itself ran July 1 – September 30, 2026 — labelled.)
  • Summary: Law enforcement from 30 countries joined INTERPOL, the Global Anti-Scam Alliance and 13 private-sector partners — including Meta, TikTok, Mastercard, Huawei, Crypto.com and Trend Micro — in an operation targeting AI-enabled fraud, digital financial fraud and technology-facilitated sextortion. Over three months it identified more than $39 billion in funds and cryptocurrencies linked to scam activity, over 15,500 victims and more than 3,500 suspects, resulting in more than 960 arrests, raids on 29 fraud centres, shutdown of over 570 social media accounts used to move money and manipulate victims, and identification of more than 4,126 bank accounts linked to fraud.
  • Why it matters: One of the largest quantified cross-border scam operations announced to date, explicitly framed around AI-enabled fraud and sextortion. Note the wording: $39 billion was identified as scam-linked — recovery is described as work in progress, not completed.
  • Source: https://www.morningstar.com/news/pr-newswire/20261022io78858/uae-leads-landmark-global-operation-with-30-countries-and-leading-technology-companies-against-ai-powered-fraud-networks

4. FTC sues GovAssist / Entry over government-impersonation scheme using deceptive Google search ads

6. FTC warns of scammers impersonating the FTC itself — "your phone number will be deleted"

  • Outlet: Popular Science, reporting an FTC consumer alert
  • Date: October 22, 2026 — verified (published Oct 22, 4:15 PM EDT; FTC alert released Oct 22)
  • Summary: The FTC warned that scammers are calling people claiming to be FTC officials, saying criminals in another country have registered a separate phone using the victim's name/number, transferring them to a fake police officer to file a report with fake badge numbers, and threatening loss of their personal phone number if they do not comply. Victims who hand over ID/personal information later get a second call claiming they will be arrested unless they transfer money for a "security deposit." The FTC stressed none of this is true.
  • Why it matters: Government impersonation has turned meta: the impersonated agency is the anti-scam agency itself. The FTC's bright-line rules: real FTC staff will never ask for money, threaten to disconnect a phone number, ask for secrecy, or text a photo of an employee ID.

NEW & EMERGING SCAMS

1. FTC impersonators threaten to "deactivate your phone number" — with a fake police transfer

  • What's new: A caller claiming to be an FTC official says a phone was registered in your name in another country and is being used for scams. They read a fake badge ID, threaten to deactivate your number, then "transfer" you to a fake police officer — or tell you what to search online to find the fake police number yourself, so it feels like you verified it. After collecting ID/personal info for the "report" and demanding secrecy, a follow-up call claims you're under arrest unless you pay a "security deposit."
  • Who's targeted: General consumers by phone.
  • Date/source: FTC Consumer Alert, October 22, 2026; reported same day by Popular Science and CNET — verified.
  • Tip: A real FTC employee will never ask you to send money, threaten to disconnect your phone, tell you to keep a call secret, or text a photo of an employee ID. Hang up and report at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.
  • Sources: https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2026/10/ftc-not-calling-deactivate-your-phone-number ; https://www.popsci.com/technology/ftc-phone-number-scam/

RESEARCH DESK (2025–2026 research; preprints labelled; all six are new to this newsletter)

1. Evaluating AI Models' Capability to Automate Voice Phishing Attacks (PREPRINT)

  • Authors: Fred Heiding, Claudio Mayrink Verdun, Simon Lermen, Andrew Kao, Vitor Albiero, Lauren Deason, Irina-Elena Veliche, Christine Lehane
  • Venue/date: arXiv:2607.09970, submitted July 10, 2026, last revised Aug 20, 2026 (v2) — not peer-reviewed
  • Key finding: AI vishing is dangerous mainly because it is cheap, not superhuman. In a survey experiment with 4,100 US adults, participants heard recordings or read transcripts from six leading voice systems versus human baselines across five scam types. Overall 16.5% said they would or might comply, rising to up to 36% for "relative-in-distress" calls. Caller persuasiveness was the strongest predictor, and one system (Sesame) was rated comparable to — sometimes slightly above — human voices. The economic analysis finds human-operated vishing unprofitable at US wages, while AI-operated vishing appears economically viable for several models.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.09970

2. ScamAgents: How AI Agents Can Simulate Human-Level Scam Calls (PREPRINT)

  • Authors: Sanket Badhe
  • Venue/date: arXiv:2508.06457, submitted Aug 8, 2025, last revised Dec 9, 2025 (v2) — not peer-reviewed
  • Key finding: Safety filters built for single prompts fail against a persistent agent. ScamAgent is an autonomous, multi-turn LLM agent that keeps dialogue memory, adapts to simulated victim replies, and applies persuasion strategies across medical-insurance, impersonation, prize/lottery and government-benefit scenarios. Decomposing a harmful goal into benign-looking subgoals delivered incrementally lets the agent bypass refusal mechanisms that would block a one-shot request, and the scripts can be turned into lifelike voice calls with off-the-shelf text-to-speech.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.06457

3. AI-in-the-Loop: Privacy Preserving Real-Time Scam Detection and Conversational Scambaiting (PREPRINT — accepted to PETS 2026)

  • Authors: Ismail Hossain, Sai Puppala, Md Jahangir Alam, Sajedul Talukder
  • Venue/date: arXiv:2509.05362, submitted Sept 4, 2025, last revised Jan 20, 2026 (v4) — arXiv record states accepted to PETS 2026; preprint version read here
  • Key finding: Put an AI in the live conversation to waste the scammer's time — without pooling victims' private data. Combining instruction-tuned AI with federated learning, the framework produced fluent scambaiting replies (perplexity as low as 22.3, engagement ≈0.80) and in federated tests sustained up to 30 rounds while keeping relevance ≈0.74 and PII leakage ≤0.0085, even with differential privacy. Trade-off: stricter guard models leak less personal information but engage less.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.05362

4. ScamPilot: Simulating Conversations with LLMs to Protect Against Online Scams (PREPRINT)

  • Authors: Owen Hoffman, Kangze Peng, Sajid Kamal, Zehua You, Sukrit Venkatagiri
  • Venue/date: arXiv:2601.22426, submitted Jan 30, 2026 — not peer-reviewed
  • Key finding: Teaching people to advise a victim works better than lecturing them. ScamPilot simulates a scam with two LLM agents — a scammer and a target — and asks the user to coach the target in real time. In a study with 150 participants, blending advice-giving with multiple-choice questions increased scam recognition by 8 percentage points, without making users more suspicious of legitimate conversations. Response efficacy was 9% higher and the change in self-efficacy 19% higher versus comparison conditions. A small, single-study result — promising, not yet a proven standard.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.22426

5. FraudShield: Knowledge Graph Empowered Defense for LLMs against Fraud Attacks (PEER-REVIEWED — ACM Web Conference 2026)

  • Authors: Naen Xu, Jinghuai Zhang, Ping He, Chunyi Zhou, Jun Wang, Zhihui Fu, Tianyu Du, Zhaoxiang Wang, Shouling Ji
  • Venue/date: Proceedings of the ACM Web Conference 2026 (WWW '26), DOI 10.1145/3774904.3792199; arXiv:2601.22485 — peer-reviewed conference paper, preprint version read here
  • Key finding: LLMs used in workflows like contract review and job applications can themselves be fooled by fraudulent content — so give the model a map of fraud tactics first. FraudShield builds a fraud tactic–keyword knowledge graph and augments the input by highlighting suspicious keywords with supporting evidence. Across four mainstream LLMs and five fraud types, the authors report it consistently outperformed state-of-the-art defences, with interpretable clues explaining why a response was steered safer. The abstract reports comparative wins, not a single headline accuracy.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.22485

6. The Deepfakes We Missed: We Built Detectors for a Threat That Didn't Arrive (PREPRINT — position paper)

  • Authors: Shaina Raza (Vector Institute for Artificial Intelligence, Toronto)
  • Venue/date: arXiv:2605.12075, submitted May 12, 2026 — not peer-reviewed; position paper, labelled as such
  • Key finding: A decade of deepfake research aimed at the wrong target. The field organised around 2017–2019 fears of face-swapped public figures swinging elections. Accounting for deepfake incidents in 2022–2026, Raza argues that catastrophe did not materialise in 2024, while the harms that did scale were peer-generated non-consensual intimate imagery, voice-clone scam calls targeting families and finance workers, and emotional-manipulation fraud. The paper proposes three under-defended agendas: real-time voice-clone detection in telecoms, on-device NCII detection, and messaging-layer defences. Read as an evidence-backed argument to rebalance research, not a new detector result.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.12075

TODAY'S SAFETY TIP

Never trust a sponsored search result or an ad at face value — go direct, and treat "higher price to advertise" as a warning sign, not proof of legitimacy. Thursday's Rhode Island suit alleges Meta sometimes charged suspected fraudsters more to advertise instead of blocking them, and the FTC's GovAssist case shows scammers buying the top Google result for "passport renewal online" to impersonate the government. Do this instead: (1) for government services, type the official .gov address yourself — never click the sponsored result; passport forms are free from the State Department; (2) for any ad on Facebook, Instagram or WhatsApp — especially health cures, investment groups, or a "senior executive" offering tips — check the seller/advertiser independently before paying or joining anything; a polished ad, a top search position, and a good review rating can all be bought.
