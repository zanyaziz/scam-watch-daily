1. China-linked Spamouflage accounts pose as U.S. voters on X days before the midterms (Oct 30)

  • Outlet: Graphika blog, as reported by the Associated Press and syndicated coverage
  • Date: October 30, 2026
  • Summary: Graphika reported that accounts likely linked to the long-running China-linked Spamouflage operation are posing as U.S. voters on X ahead of the November 3, 2026 midterms, posting divisive content questioning election integrity and the legitimacy of U.S. democracy. The firm flagged an account that appears to have replaced a previously removed Spamouflage-linked account, four accounts with a Spamouflage history now amplifying MAGA-styled personas, and a further account with more than 200,000 followers presenting as a Philippine culture blog but now posting pro-China and divisive U.S.-focused content. Graphika said it could not determine whether that last account was hijacked, sold, or otherwise transferred.
  • Why it matters: It shows inauthentic accounts on a major platform are again being used to erode trust in voting itself in the final days before an election — and that a familiar persona, not just a new account, can be repurposed.
  • Source: https://political.org/2026/10/30/graphika-finds-china-linked-spamouflage-accounts-posing-as-us-voters-on-x-days-before-midterms/
  • Caveat: This item relies on AP reporting of the Graphika blog as carried by secondary outlets; the original Graphika blog post was not retrieved directly. The Chinese Embassy in Washington denied interfering in U.S. elections, per the same coverage.

2. FBI warns election-season scams are using AI impersonation, fake merch and fake voter-registration pages (Oct 30)

  • Outlet: Detroit Free Press (Susan Tompor column, reporting an FBI October alert)
  • Date: October 30, 2026
  • Summary: The FBI is warning that scammers are exploiting the 2026 election season with several schemes: selling political T-shirts and lawn signs that never ship, impersonating legitimate political action committees or campaign committees and keeping the money, and running a "campaign investment pool" promising a guaranteed return on campaign contributions. Victims are also sent texts or emails claiming they are not registered to vote, with a link to a fraudulent voter-registration page designed to harvest personal information. The FBI notes scammers now use AI tools to generate highly realistic voices, images and written messages impersonating candidates, campaigns, government entities and trusted contacts.
  • Why it matters: Election timing gives old non-delivery and impersonation scams a fresh, urgent hook — and AI-generated impersonation removes the poor-grammar red flags people used to rely on.
  • Source: https://www.freep.com/story/money/personal-finance/susan-tompor/2026/10/30/2026-election-scam-common-prevention/92458255007/

3. Microsoft and Health-ISAC's dismantling of three phishing-as-a-service marketplaces — YAM Store, CoGUI and Iron Man (summarised Oct 30; announced Oct 14)

  • Outlet: PYMNTS (summarising Microsoft's On the Issues blog), corroborated by Healthcare Dive
  • Date: October 30, 2026 (PYMNTS summary); the underlying takedown announcement was October 14, 2026 — labelled accordingly, do not read the takedown itself as Oct 30 news
  • Summary: Microsoft and Health-ISAC, acting under an order from the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of Florida, seized and redirected domains supporting YAM Store, CoGUI and Iron Man, three Chinese-language cybercrime services. Since January 2026 the services collectively enabled more than one billion malicious messages targeting over 75,000 organisations worldwide, including more than 750 million messages aimed at Microsoft customers. CoGUI supplied ready-made impersonation websites and messages; YAM Store sold fraudulent Microsoft and Google accounts and scam tools; Iron Man offered more than 2,300 templates impersonating organisations across 150+ countries. Microsoft disabled more than 20,000 accounts linked to YAM Store and blocked over 100,000 impersonation and fraud domains.
  • Why it matters: It targets the supplier economy behind phishing — the kits, accounts and templates that let low-skill criminals run convincing impersonation campaigns at scale — rather than just individual scam messages.
  • Sources: https://www.pymnts.com/cpi-posts/disrupting-the-service-economy-behind-transnational-cybercrime-operations/ ; https://www.healthcaredive.com/news/phishing-marketplace-takedown-microsoft-health-isac/833215/

4. FTC discloses takedown of 19 fake FTC websites as impersonation losses hit $3.5 billion in 2025 (late-breaking Oct 29)

  • Outlet: U.S. Federal Trade Commission (official press release), via PYMNTS
  • Date: October 29, 2026
  • Summary: The FTC said domain registrars and other providers removed 19 websites impersonating the FTC itself over the past year, after the agency sent notices alleging violations of its Impersonation Rule; it also reported and had removed social media pages impersonating the FTC or its officials. NEW & EMERGING SCAMS

1. AI-cloned executive voice used to seek a $3.3 million transfer — then moved to WhatsApp (Oct 30)

  • How it works: A caller using an AI-generated copy of a company director's voice tried to persuade an employee to transfer about $3.3 million, supposedly for a confidential overseas acquisition. After citing a poor phone connection, the caller moved the conversation to WhatsApp using a profile bearing the director's name and photograph, and the messages reportedly included insider information that made the request harder to spot. The employee became suspicious and internal controls stopped the payment; the company reported the incident to police.
  • Source: https://www.dailynorthern.com/36934/scammers-used-ai-cloned-di-chiefs-voice-to-seek-3-3-million/

2. AI-generated bank calls + card-tap relay drain about $423,114 from 170 victims in three weeks (Oct 30)

  • How it works: Victims received a recorded call warning of suspicious bank-account activity, generated with AI in many different voices, banks, companies and scenarios. Pressing 1 connected them to a fake bank employee who urged fast action to block their card, then told them to download an app and hold their bank card against their phone. Police say the app relayed data from the card's contactless chip to an accomplice at an ATM or payment terminal, who could withdraw money without the victim knowing. Two men were arrested on October 6, and a Rotterdam court ruled on October 20 that both must remain in custody for at least another 90 days.
  • Source: https://dailyhodl.com/2026/10/30/ai-powered-bank-scam-drains-423114-from-170-victims-in-just-three-weeks-report/
  • Note: The page also shows an earlier update stamp of October 28; the report itself is dated October 30.

3. Amazon impersonation wave: fake "suspicious activity" calls, screen-sharing and remote access (Oct 30)

  • How it works: Scammers posing as Amazon contact victims out of the blue claiming suspicious activity on their account, saying unauthorised items have been added to their basket and giving delivery addresses that are not the victim's to make the story seem genuine. Victims are then told the transactions can be stopped only by following instructions that include sharing their screen, downloading money-transfer apps, providing passport or driving-licence documents, or installing remote-access software that lets criminals carry out transactions on their behalf. Report Fraud recorded 110 such reports and £86,837 stolen between 5–14 October 2026, an average of over £9,600 per day; people aged 50–79 accounted for 60% of the reports in the period studied.
  • Source: https://www.ladbible.com/money/amazon-users-warned-of-scam-police-uk-money-698718-20261030

4. Election-season scams supercharged by AI impersonation — fake investment pools, fake PACs, non-delivery merch, fake voter-registration texts (reported Oct 30)

  • How it works: Ahead of the November 3, 2026 U.S. midterms, scammers are exploiting election themes with AI-generated voices, images and written messages impersonating candidates, campaigns, government entities and trusted contacts: a fake campaign investment pool promising a payout if a candidate wins, donations to fake or unaffiliated political action committees, campaign merchandise that is paid for but never shipped, and texts or emails falsely claiming the recipient is not registered to vote, linking to a fraudulent registration page. The defence is to verify any committee on the Federal Election Commission site and check registration only via official state channels.
  • Source: https://www.freep.com/story/money/personal-finance/susan-tompor/2026/10/30/2026-election-scam-common-prevention/92458255007/
  • Note: FBI alert itself dated October 15, 2026, per the article.

5. Invoice fraud now hides prompt-injection commands for AI email agents (campaign Oct 22; blog updated Oct 29)

  • How it works: A novel twist on familiar invoice fraud: alongside a fake invoice (about $88,000, impersonating a North America-based cybersecurity company, with a soon-to-expire 10% discount lure and a fabricated reply chain), the email hides instructions in a 1-pixel HTML span using invisible Unicode Tag characters. A human recipient never sees the instruction, but an AI agent reading the email could — it told the agent to forward the email and PDF unchanged to an accounts-payable-style address, apparently to push the fake invoice into a payment process. Attackers are actively experimenting with this technique as AI agents take on more email actions.
  • Source: https://sublime.security/blog/invoice-fraud-campaign-adds-prompt-injection-aimed-at-email-ai-agents/

6. "Quishing": fake QR codes pasted over parking meters — and in unsolicited packages (Oct 23 roundup; October incidents)

  • How it works: Criminals place fake QR-code stickers over genuine codes in public places, or embed them in emails, texts and posters, directing victims to malicious websites built to steal personal and payment details or install malware. In September 2026 the FTC warned about fake QR stickers over legitimate ones on parking meters; in October 2026 police in Ann Arbor, Michigan, found fake stickers on downtown meters near the University of Michigan bearing phrases such as "Scan to Park!" and "Park AA," sending drivers to fraudulent payment websites. A parallel variant is the brushing scam: an unsolicited package containing a cheap item arrives with a QR code inviting the recipient to identify the sender or arrange a return, but scanning leads to a phishing site or malware.
  • Source: https://itsecurity.blog.fordham.edu/2026/10/23/qr-code-scams-why-you-should-think-before-you-scan/

7. Hyper-realistic AI job scams: "perfectly tailored" dream jobs with stolen recruiter identities (Oct 27)

  • How it works: AI now lets scammers generate polished job adverts, personalised messages and professional-looking recruiter profiles in seconds, removing the old giveaways of poor spelling and awkward language. In the case highlighted, a role was perfectly tailored to the victim's experience, and the scammer stole a real recruiter's LinkedIn profile picture, linked to her account and created a fake email address. The new red flags are a generous salary for little or no experience plus flexible remote work, any upfront fee for training, registration, a background check or a compulsory course, an offer with no formal interview or contract, and an unsolicited contact who immediately tries to move the conversation to WhatsApp — plus email domains or website URLs that do not match the real employer's official site.
  • Source: https://www.uniladtech.com/news/ai/red-flags-ai-job-scams-target-jobseekers-468821-20261020

RESEARCH DESK (2025–2026; peer-reviewed vs preprint labelled)

1. A Large-Scale Study of Personalized Phishing using Large Language Models (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Authors / venue / date: Stefan Czybik (BIFOLD & TU Berlin), Anne Josiane Kouam (Inria & TU Berlin), Peter Heubl (Ruhr University Bochum), Jan Magnus Nold (Ruhr University Bochum), Konrad Rieck (BIFOLD & TU Berlin) — 35th USENIX Security Symposium (USENIX Security '26), Baltimore, August 2026, pp. 1687–1706. Peer-reviewed.
  • Key finding: In a controlled field experiment with 7,741 participants, researchers used only a target's email address to gather personal details via web searches, then had an LLM automatically write a tailored phishing email for each person. LLM-generated personalized ("spear") phishing almost tripled the click rate compared with generic phishing, whether the generic emails were written by humans or by LLMs (BIFOLD's summary puts the personalized-AI vs. generic comparison at a 2.4× click rate). The cost of that automated personalization was only about $0.03 per email — cheap enough to make mass spear-phishing economically viable for small-budget attackers.
  • Source: https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity26/presentation/czybik

2. Understanding the Efficacy of Phishing Training in Practice (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Authors / venue / date: Grant Ho, Ariana Mirian, Elisa Luo, Khang Tong, Euyhyun Lee, Lin Liu, Christopher A. Longhurst, Christian Dameff, Stefan Savage, Geoffrey M. Voelker (UC San Diego / UC San Diego Health / University of Chicago) — 2025 IEEE Symposium on Security and Privacy (SP), San Francisco, May 2025, pp. 37–54; DOI: 10.1109/SP61157.2025.00076. Peer-reviewed.
  • Key finding: In an 8-month randomized controlled experiment sending 10 simulated phishing campaigns to more than 19,500 employees at UC San Diego Health, there was no significant relationship between how recently someone had completed annual mandated cybersecurity training and whether they clicked a phishing link. Embedded training (shown after someone clicked a simulated phish) reduced the likelihood of clicking by only about 2% (about 1.7 percentage points in one secondary summary). Engagement was the weak link: 75% of users spent a minute or less on the embedded training material, and one-third closed the training page immediately without engaging at all. The authors' conclusion: anti-phishing training in its current, commonly deployed forms is unlikely to deliver large practical reductions in phishing risk.
  • Source: https://www.computer.org/csdl/proceedings-article/sp/2025/223600a076/21B7RjYyG9q
  • Note: Venue, authors and DOI confirmed via the UC San Diego publications listing and the DOI record; the IEEE PDF itself was not opened directly in this run.

3. ScamPilot: Simulating Conversations with LLMs to Protect Against Online Scams (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Authors / venue / date: Owen Hoffman, Kangze Peng, Sajid Kamal, Zehua You, Sukrit Venkatagiri (all Swarthmore College) — Proceedings of the 2026 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems (CHI '26), Barcelona, April 13–17, 2026; DOI: 10.1145/3772318.3791313. Peer-reviewed.
  • Key finding: Instead of lecturing users, ScamPilot has them coach a simulated victim through a live, LLM-generated scam conversation, with embedded quizzes and real-time feedback. In a between-subjects study of 150 people, combining advice-giving with multiple-choice questions increased scam recognition by 8% without making people more suspicious of legitimate messages. Response efficacy was 9% higher and self-efficacy 19% higher in that combined condition (the self-efficacy gain was not statistically significant). In short: practicing against a simulated scammer — rather than just reading warnings — measurably sharpened scam-spotting without raising false alarms.
  • Source: https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2601.22426

4. "Account suspension. Verify now": using systemic-functional linguistics to improve phishing text advisories for vulnerable groups (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Authors / venue / date: Desiree Kawabata (Griffith University, Gold Coast, Australia) — Humanities and Social Sciences Communications (Springer Nature); received September 4, 2025; accepted October 8, 2026; published October 22, 2026. Peer-reviewed, open access.
  • Key finding: Comparing 106 real phishing text messages from Australia with legitimate texts from the organisations most often impersonated, the study pinpointed the language tricks that manufacture urgency and authority: particular clause types, time/location/manner markers ("verify now," "within 24 hours"-style framing), high-modality wording ("must," "will be suspended"), and conjunctive phrasing that piles pressure on the reader. Older adults and people from non-English-speaking backgrounds are at particular risk because these linguistic pressures exploit emotional and cognitive load. The practical implication: scam warnings should show concrete examples of these exact phrases, not just generic advice — and the same linguistic markers could feed automated scam-detection systems.
  • Source: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-026-09540-7

5. PP3D: An In-Browser Vision-Based Defense Against Web Behavior Manipulation Attacks (PREPRINT)

  • Authors / venue / date: Spencer King, Irfan Ozen, Karthika Subramani, Saranyan Senthivel, Phani Vadrevu, Roberto Perdisci (University of Georgia, Georgia Tech, DXC Technology, Louisiana State University) — arXiv:2510.18465v1, October 21, 2025. Not peer-reviewed; preprint.
  • Key finding: This is a defense against a different, under-studied scam class: "behavior manipulation" pages that don't steal passwords but trick you into an action — fake tech-support pop-ups, scareware, fake software downloads, bogus sweepstakes. The PP3D browser extension looks at what the page actually shows (screenshots plus on-page text) rather than trusting its web address, so scammers can't dodge it just by moving to a new domain. Tested on 7,149 real-world scam pages across 84 attack campaigns, it caught over 99% of scam pages at a 1% false-positive rate, and still caught over 97% on fresh scam samples collected months after the model was trained — all running locally in the browser to preserve privacy.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.18465

6. SoK: Large Language Model-Generated Textual Phishing Campaigns — End-to-End Analysis of Generation, Characteristics, and Detection (PREPRINT)

  • Authors / venue / date: Fengchao Chen, Tingmin Wu, Van Nguyen, and Carsten Rudolph (Monash University / CSIRO Data61, Melbourne, Australia) — arXiv:2508.21457v1, August 29, 2025. Not peer-reviewed; a systematization-of-knowledge (SoK) survey.
  • Key finding: Synthesizing 44 studies on LLM-driven text phishing (screened from 3,910 references), the authors argue that AI has industrialized phishing into "Phishing-as-a-Service" that can be launched at scale within minutes. Their synthesis cites industry figures (not the survey's own experiments, so treat as reported, not verified, numbers) that about 96% of phishing attempts arrive by email and 82% of phishing emails incorporate LLMs (KnowBe4 2025 report, as cited in the paper). Their Generation-Characterization-Defense framework maps how LLM phishing differs from traditional phishing and where current defences fall short.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.21457

TODAY'S SAFETY TIP

Hang up, look up, call back — and set a family safe word today. October 30's biggest scams all ran on a familiar voice or a trusted name: an AI clone of a company director nearly moved $3.3 million, AI bank calls drained $423,000 from 170 Dutch victims, and election impersonators are faking candidates and campaigns. Do this today: (1) if any urgent call, message or email pressures you to move money, share a code, or "verify" an account, hang up — do not act in the call; (2) find the organisation's real number yourself, from its official app or website (never from the message), and call back; (3) agree a safe word with family now, so a "relative in trouble" voice clone fails the one test a scammer cannot pass. Never send money or a login code to "unlock" a payment, refund, or prize — real ones never ask for that.
