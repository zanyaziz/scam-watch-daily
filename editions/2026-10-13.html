Lead

The official tally lands for the West Africa crackdown — and the FTC halts a sextortion "relief" scheme that allegedly took $60 million from victims. Tuesday brought the verified NCA/DOJ breakdown of the joint disruption first announced by Meta on Monday: 28 arrests, 117 trafficked victims identified, and Meta's removal of more than 3.7 million scam-linked accounts confirmed in an official release dated Oct 13. NEW & EMERGING SCAMS

1. Calendar phishing surges — and a new internal-forward variant

  • What's new: Fake Google Calendar invites can land on a calendar even when the matching email is filtered, because of auto-add settings; lures lead to fake login pages, fake support numbers, or remote-access downloads. A newer variant reported Oct 13: an outsider posing as a prospective customer gets a non-sales employee to forward a meeting-booking link internally; the sales recipient trusts it because it came from a colleague, then hits a Microsoft 365 sign-in prompt.
  • Date: Oct 12, 2026 (ConsumerAffairs, verified); Oct 13, 2026 (KnowBe4, verified)
  • Tip: Set Google Calendar "Add invitations to my calendar" to known senders only; report unfamiliar invites as spam instead of declining, and never sign in from a booking page you didn't navigate to yourself.
  • Sources: https://www.consumeraffairs.com/news/fake-google-calendar-entries-give-phishing-scams-a-new-way-to-reach-consumers-101226.html ; https://blog.knowbe4.com/cyberheistnews-vol-16-41-heads-up-attackers-trick-your-employees-to-share-fake-calendar-invites

2. Quishing escalation: QR lures paired with live session theft (AiTM) — developing, single-source for campaign detail

  • What's new: A campaign tracked as UAT-11985 reportedly used event-themed invites and altered QR codes leading to a Google lookalike flow with an adversary-in-the-middle relay that can capture the live authenticated session — so completing MFA may still hand over access. Separately, Microsoft is rolling out post-delivery QR-code URL checks in Teams (notice Oct 7; rollout early Oct to early Nov).
  • Date: Campaign published Oct 12, 2026 per page metadata (Netcrook); Teams coverage Oct 12, 2026 (CyberPress, verified)
  • Tip: Preview any QR destination and don't sign in from a QR-linked page; prefer passkeys/hardware keys, and revoke active sessions — not just the password — if a scan went wrong.
  • Sources: https://netcrook.com/written_article?slug=qr-phishing-aitm-google-logins&lang=en ; https://cyberpress.org/microsoft-teams-to-detect-malicious-qr-codes/

3. Fake Facebook Marketplace listing puts YOUR name on it

  • What's new: People receive a text/iMessage asking if an item is still available, with a fabricated Marketplace screenshot listing them as the seller — using their name but a photo of a different person with the same name. The apparent goal is simply a reply: even a correction confirms the number is live and can invite follow-on scams. The end goal is unconfirmed.
  • Date: Oct 7, 2026 (ConsumerAffairs, verified; Malwarebytes Oct 6)
  • Tip: Don't reply, click, or call. Open Facebook yourself to check your listings, report the text as junk / forward to 7726, and delete it.
  • Source: https://www.consumeraffairs.com/news/fake-facebook-marketplace-listings-use-your-name-to-draw-you-into-a-scam-100726.html

4. Free Claude Max phishing uses a fake Google window inside the page

  • What's new: A site promising free Claude Max subscriptions (fake scarcity counter, copied branding) pushes visitors to "sign in with Google" — opening a drawn, draggable fake Google sign-in window inside the tab (browser-in-the-browser), complete with padlock and correct-looking address.
  • Date: Reported in KnowBe4 CyberheistNews dated Oct 13, 2026 (verified), summarising a Malwarebytes warning
  • Tip: Never sign in to Google from a window inside another site. Close the tab and go to the provider directly.
  • Source: https://blog.knowbe4.com/cyberheistnews-vol-16-41-heads-up-attackers-trick-your-employees-to-share-fake-calendar-invites

5. "3D phishing": WhatsApp message + AI-cloned voice moves bank money — date unverified

  • What's new: Coverage describes multichannel attacks blending text, voice and video. In a cited Italian bank case, a WhatsApp message appearing to come from a parent-company CEO was followed by a call using an AI-cloned voice of a trusted lawyer, and about €95 million was wired before irregularities were spotted, with a portion still missing. The underlying case was made public in September 2026 per secondary sources.
  • Date: Article surfaced Oct 13–14, 2026; exact publication date not verified — labeled accordingly
  • Tip: No payment or bank-detail change on a voice/video request alone. Hang up, call back on a known number, require dual approval, and agree a family safe word for emergency calls.
  • Source: https://www.cybersecurity-insiders.com/ai-is-rewriting-the-playbook-for-bank-phishing-and-payments-fraud/

6. Scams now start by text — including fake "extra SSI payment" texts (SSI wave: developing)

  • What's new: PYMNTS Intelligence data: first contact by text rose 35% since Sept 2025 to 12% of victims, nearly catching email (13%); 43% of victims paid within an hour of first contact. Riding that shift, fake texts promise an extra SSI payment in October — believable because October genuinely has an unusual double SSI payment schedule — and push recipients to tap a link, confirm bank details, or pay a fee. No official SSA alert naming that exact text was found.
  • Date: Oct 8, 2026 (PYMNTS, verified); SSI guide October 2026, exact day unverified
  • Tip: SSA pays automatically — any text asking you to claim, confirm, or pay to release a benefit is a scam. Check payment dates on ssa.gov directly.
  • Sources: https://www.pymnts.com/cybersecurity/fraud-prevention/2026/scams-move-from-the-inbox-to-the-text-screen/ ; https://govtschemes.org/fake-extra-ssi-payment-texts-scam-alert-whats-real/

7. Job scams tailored to specific groups

  • What's new: Fake offers written for specific circumstances — clearance-type roles for veterans, flexible schedules for parents/caregivers returning to work, even roles aimed at cybersecurity professionals. Red flags: unsolicited outreach, free-email sender domains, early requests for SSN/banking details, fast-track urgency, and any demand to pay for training, equipment or application processing.
  • Date: Oct 8, 2026 (ConsumerAffairs, verified)
  • Tip: Find the listing on the company's own careers page via your own search — if it isn't there, stop. Never pay to be hired.
  • Source: https://www.consumeraffairs.com/news/job-scammers-have-a-type-this-year-heres-whos-on-the-list-100826.html

RESEARCH DESK (2025–2026 research; preprints labeled)

1. A Large-Scale Study of Personalized Phishing using Large Language Models (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Authors: Stefan Czybik, Anne Josiane Kouam, Peter Heubl, Jan Magnus Nold, Konrad Rieck
  • Venue/date: USENIX Security '26, presented August 12, 2026 — peer-reviewed. Verified on USENIX.
  • Key finding: In a field experiment with 7,700 participants, researchers used each target's email address to gather public information and auto-generate a tailored phishing email. LLM-based spear phishing almost tripled the click rate compared with generic phishing, at a personalization cost of about $0.03 per email.
  • Source: https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity26/presentation/czybik

2. Evaluating AI Models' Capability to Automate Voice Phishing Attacks (PREPRINT)

  • Authors: Fred Heiding, Claudio Mayrink Verdun, Simon Lermen, Andrew Kao, Vitor Albiero, Lauren Deason, Irina-Elena Veliche, Christine Lehane
  • Venue/date: arXiv:2607.09970, submitted July 10, 2026; revised August 20, 2026 — not peer-reviewed. Verified on arXiv.
  • Key finding: In a survey experiment with 4,100 US adults, 16.5% said they would or might comply across five scam categories, rising to up to 36% for a "relative-in-distress" scenario. Caller persuasiveness was the strongest predictor of compliance, and one voice model (Sesame) was rated comparable to human voices. The authors conclude human-operated vishing is unprofitable at US wages, while AI-powered vishing appears economically viable.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.09970

3. "It didn't feel right but I needed a job so desperately": Understanding People's Emotions & Help Needs During Financial Scams (PEER-REVIEWED — forthcoming, CHI '26)

  • Authors: Jake Chanenson, Tara Matthews, Sunny Consolvo, Patrick Gage Kelley, Jessica McClearn, Sarah Meiklejohn, Abhishek Roy, Renee Shelby, Kurt Thomas, Amelia Hassoun
  • Venue/date: arXiv:2604.06218, submitted March 20, 2026; to appear in CHI '26 proceedings — labeled peer-reviewed/forthcoming on that basis. Verified on arXiv.
  • Key finding: People often sense something is wrong and continue anyway because need outweighs suspicion. Fear and hope are the central emotions scammers exploit, and financial insecurity and legal precarity elevate risk. Help needs differ by stage of the scam, so the authors argue for stage-specific interventions rather than one generic warning.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.06218v1

4. "It Felt Real" — Victim Perspectives on Platform Design and Longer-Running Scams (PREPRINT)

  • Authors: Jingjia Xiao, Qing Xiao, Hong Shen
  • Venue/date: arXiv:2510.02680, submitted October 3, 2025 — not peer-reviewed. Verified on arXiv.
  • Key finding: In longer scams, the platform itself is part of the con. Interviews with 25 victims in China (including romance fraud and pig-butchering) found scammers systematically use platform affordances — profile verification, algorithmic recommendations, integrated payments, private chat — to stage credibility, build intimacy, and sustain coercion.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.02680

5. PsyScam: A Benchmark for Psychological Techniques in Real-World Scams (PREPRINT)

  • Authors: Shang Ma, Tianyi Ma, Jiahao Liu, Wei Song, Zhenkai Liang, Xusheng Xiao, Yanfang Ye
  • Venue/date: arXiv:2505.15017, submitted May 21, 2025; latest version September 22, 2025 — not peer-reviewed. Verified on arXiv.
  • Key finding: Current models struggle to reason about the psychology inside a scam, not just its surface text. PsyScam collects real-world scam reports from six public platforms, annotated with the psychological techniques used; tested on classification, completion and augmentation tasks, existing models faced significant challenges — manipulation tactics are a blind spot for benchmarks focused only on wording or URLs.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.15017v1

6. What You Read Isn't What You Hear: Linguistic Sensitivity in Deepfake Speech Detection (PREPRINT)

  • Authors: Binh Nguyen, Shuji Shi, Ryan Ofman, Thai Le
  • Venue/date: arXiv:2505.17513, submitted May 23, 2025 — not peer-reviewed. Verified on arXiv.
  • Key finding: Changing the words, not just the voice, can fool a deepfake detector. Minor transcript-level changes produced attack success rates above 60% on several open-source detector–voice pairs, and one commercial system's accuracy fell from 100% to 32%. A case study replicating a Brad Pitt audio deepfake scam completely bypassed commercial detectors.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.17513v1

TODAY'S SAFETY TIP

Help for sextortion is free — never pay for it. Tuesday's FTC action is the lesson: victims of image-based abuse allegedly paid a "relief" company $1,499–$20,000 for removal help that often never came, while the legitimate tools cost nothing. If you or someone you know is being sextorted: (1) do not pay the blackmailer or any "recovery"/"removal" company that charges a fee — paying marks you for more demands and more scams; (2) for adults, use StopNCII.org and for minors NCMEC's Take It Down — both free, both work with major platforms to block the images; (3) preserve evidence (screenshots, usernames, payment handles) without engaging further, report the account in-app, and report to the FBI's IC3 (ic3.gov) or local police. The same rule covers every "recovery" pitch: anyone who finds you after a scam and offers to get your money back for an upfront fee is running the second scam.
