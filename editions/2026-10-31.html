Lead

WhatsApp bans 9,400 "digital arrest" accounts as India's Supreme Court presses banks on victim compensation. In a Supreme Court hearing on October 30, India's cybercrime coordination centre reported that WhatsApp has banned 9,400 accounts linked to "digital arrest" scams since January 2026, with more than 83,668 WhatsApp accounts and 3,962 Skype IDs blocked overall for use in the scam, in which callers impersonating police or court officials frighten victims — often older adults — into paying. Note on the day itself: October 31 was a quiet day for major platform scam news — no story dated October 31 from a major outlet was found — so this edition leads with the October 30 items not covered in yesterday's edition. Two big October 30 stories, the Microsoft takedown of phishing-service marketplaces and the FTC's takedown of 19 fake FTC sites, were covered in the October 30 edition and are not repeated as new items here.

1. WhatsApp bans 9,400 "digital arrest" accounts in India; total blocks top 83,668 WhatsApp accounts (Oct 30)

  • Outlet: Gadgets 360 / Outlook Money (Supreme Court hearing coverage)
  • Date: October 30, 2026
  • Summary: India's Supreme Court heard the suo motu digital-arrest case on October 30, with the Centre reporting via the Indian Cyber Crime Coordination Centre (I4C) that WhatsApp banned 9,400 accounts linked to digital-arrest scams since January 2026 after a dedicated 12-week investigation. The wider government tally reported the same day: over 83,668 WhatsApp accounts and 3,962 Skype IDs blocked for use in digital-arrest scams, in which callers impersonate police, CBI or court officials and frighten victims — often older adults — into paying. The court also pushed the Reserve Bank of India to frame rules under which banks would compensate victims where inadequate due diligence contributed to the fraud.
  • Why it matters: WhatsApp is the delivery channel for one of the fastest-growing elder-fraud formats, and this pairs platform bans with a potential shift in who pays when banks miss red flags — a precedent other regulators will watch.

2. SEC charges $130 million crypto scheme that faked SEC approval to win trust (late-breaking Oct 29)

  • Outlet: U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (press release, via CRWE republication); also Law360, Oct 29
  • Date: October 29, 2026 — late-breaking, not October 31
  • Summary: The SEC sued BG Wealth Sharing Ltd and DSJ Exchange Pty Ltd, foreign-operated but Colorado-incorporated entities, alleging they raised over $130 million from tens of thousands of retail investors in 40+ U.S. states and territories between at least October 2025 and June 2026. The pitch — a "wealth-sharing program" promising extraordinary, supposedly risk-free returns from crypto-trading signals — was spread via social media, encrypted messaging, weekly live videoconferences and a mobile app. The trust trick, per the SEC: false Form ADV and Form D filings that were publicly viewable on SEC systems, shown to investors as supposed proof of licensing, plus fake certificates bearing the SEC seal. The SEC says neither firm was ever registered, losses exceed $100 million, and the filings have since been removed. These are allegations; no court has ruled.
  • Why it matters: It exposes a novel credibility hack — a real filing on a regulator's own site repurposed as a fake license — and a warning that applies far beyond crypto: a filing is not an endorsement. The social and encrypted-messaging distribution is exactly how these schemes now recruit.
  • Source: https://crweworld.com/article/news-provided-by-crwe-press-release/3940426/two-entities-charged-in-130-million-fraud-scheme-that-used-false-sec-filings-to-boost-investor-confidence

NEW & EMERGING SCAMS

1. Amazon impersonation + screen-sharing / remote-access takeover (Oct 30)

  • Date: October 30, 2026 (alert issued that day, covering October 5–14, 2026)
  • How it works: Criminals contact victims out of the blue claiming suspicious activity on their Amazon account, saying unauthorised items have been added to their basket and giving delivery addresses that are not the victim's. They then persuade victims to share their screen, download money-transfer apps, hand over passport or driving-licence details for so-called verification, or install remote-access software — after which they move money or carry out transactions on the victim's device. People aged 50–79 accounted for 60% of the 110 reports in the nine-day period.
  • Why it matters: This is a classic brand-impersonation lure upgraded into full device takeover: once screen-sharing or remote access is granted, the scammer no longer needs the victim to send money themselves. £86,837 was stolen in just nine days.
  • Source: https://apnin.com/report-fraud-alert-more-than-86000-stolen-in-nine-days-by-fraudsters-posing-as-amazon/

2. AI voice cloning and deepfake impersonation (Oct 18–20)

  • Date: October 18, 2026 (INTERPOL campaign announced in Singapore); Better Business Bureau warning reported October 20, 2026
  • How it works: Criminals use generative AI to reproduce a person's voice or appearance from limited material available online, then pose as a colleague requesting a bank transfer, a distressed family member, or a new romantic partner. The BBB notes that a voice can be cloned from a small audio sample and used in calls or voicemails impersonating relatives, employers, or other trusted people, with urgency used to stop the victim verifying first.
  • Why it matters: Familiar trust signals — a known voice, a face on a video call — are no longer reliable proof of identity. INTERPOL's warning signs are unsolicited contact, sudden urgency, emotional pressure, demands for secrecy, and unusual payment requests.
  • Source: https://fincrimecentral.com/interpol-ai-deepfake-scams-fraud-warning-2026/

3. AI-written phishing and smishing targeting election officials and campaigns (Oct 16–19)

  • Date: October 16, 2026 (FBI/CISA joint Public Service Announcement); reported October 19, 2026
  • How it works: Attackers use AI tools to write fluent, native-language phishing emails and smishing texts aimed at election officials, candidates, and political organisations, to steal login credentials and sensitive information. The agencies also warn that AI-generated deepfake audio and video are being used to invent identities, impersonate real people, and make phishing schemes more believable, at greater speed and scale.
  • Why it matters: The old tell — badly written, obviously foreign phishing — is disappearing. The agencies' lead recommendation is phishing-resistant multi-factor authentication for email, VPNs, and accounts tied to critical systems, and verifying unusual requests through a separate channel.
  • Source: https://www.technology.org/2026/10/19/fbi-cisa-ai-phishing-midterms-election-officials/

4. Quishing — fake QR codes on parking meters and in messages (Oct 23)

  • Date: October 23, 2026 (article); incidents cited include an FTC warning in September 2026 and fake stickers found in Ann Arbor, Michigan, in October 2026
  • How it works: Criminals place fake QR-code stickers over genuine codes on parking meters, posters, and signs — Ann Arbor stickers used phrases such as "Scan to Park!" — or embed QR codes in emails and texts claiming account verification or a work service. Scanning sends the victim to a fake payment or login page built to harvest card details, login credentials, or to push malware. A QR code hides its destination, so the victim cannot inspect the link before scanning.
  • Why it matters: Quishing turns a routine real-world action — paying for parking — into credential theft, and it evades the usual "check the link" advice because the link is inside an image. Relatedly, Microsoft announced on October 7, 2026 that Defender for Office 365 would start extracting and checking QR-code URLs in Teams messages.
  • Source: https://itsecurity.blog.fordham.edu/2026/10/23/qr-code-scams-why-you-should-think-before-you-scan/

5. Fake Facebook Marketplace listing that uses YOUR name as the seller — DEVELOPING (date unverified)

  • Date: Date unverified — circulating in October 2026 (a linked post in the article is dated October 13, 2026)
  • How it works: Victims receive an unsolicited iMessage asking if an item is still available, with an image that looks like a Facebook Marketplace listing showing the recipient's own name as the seller, using a profile photo lifted from another Facebook user with the same name. Replying confirms the phone number is active, making it more valuable to criminals, and can lead to follow-on password-reset attempts, Facebook account takeover, or malware links.
  • Why it matters: The lure works precisely because it is personalised — seeing your own name stops you and prompts a reply, which is the foot in the door. Labelled developing because this rests on secondary reporting of Malwarebytes' documentation, not on the primary Malwarebytes post itself.
  • Source: https://theredalertnews.com/scammers-clone-name-hijack-trust/

6. Wrong-number texts that grow into crypto investment (pig-butchering) scams (Oct 27–28)

  • Date: October 28, 2026 (iTechPost article); Engadget article October 27, 2026
  • How it works: A seemingly accidental wrong-number text is the entry point. The sender apologises, sustains a friendly or romantic conversation, then moves it to WhatsApp or Telegram, mentions personal trading success or a relative's expertise, and shows screenshots of crypto gains. The victim is encouraged to register on a trading platform that shows quick gains and allows small early withdrawals, building credibility before larger deposits that can never be withdrawn.
  • Why it matters: Even a polite correction reply signals an active, engageable target. The FBI figures cited in the coverage put crypto investment fraud at $7.2 billion in reported losses in 2025, the largest loss category that year, and found that 78% of 3,780 victims contacted in 2025 had not realised they were being scammed.
  • Source: https://www.itechpost.com/articles/237669/20261028/how-wrong-number-text-scams-turn-random-messages-costly-cryptocurrency-investment-scams.htm

7. Cloned WhatsApp work groups hijacking a business's crypto settlements — DEVELOPING (Oct 16)

  • Date: October 16, 2026 (police disclosure)
  • How it works: After hijacking an employee's messaging account, criminals locked the genuine WhatsApp work group and blacklisted all real clients and colleagues in it, cutting off warnings. They then cloned the group — copying its name, member nicknames, and avatars — and, impersonating client representatives inside the fake group, issued crypto exchange and transfer instructions in normal business language. Staff at a Hong Kong virtual-currency exchange firm transferred crypto to external wallets, losing over HK$4 million (about US$514,000), with suspicion arising only when a fiat deduction failed for insufficient funds.
  • Why it matters: This is account takeover weaponised as business-process compromise: the victim was not tricked by a stranger, but by a perfect replica of their own trusted work channel, with the real channel silenced. Large transfers need out-of-band verification (a real phone call) and enforced multi-factor authentication on messaging accounts. Labelled developing because it rests on secondary reporting of the police briefing, not the primary briefing itself.
  • Source: https://coincamps.ai/article/t34654

RESEARCH DESK

1. AI-personalised phishing almost triples click rates — for about 3 cents an email (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Title: A Large-Scale Study of Personalized Phishing using Large Language Models
  • Authors: Stefan Czybik et al. (BIFOLD/TU Berlin, Inria, Ruhr University Bochum team)
  • Venue / Date: USENIX Security Symposium 2026, Baltimore (presented August 2026)
  • Key finding: When attackers used a target's email address to scrape public web information and auto-generate a personalised phishing email, victims clicked far more often than with generic phishing — the USENIX abstract says LLM-based spear phishing "almost triples the click rate" versus generic strategies, whether the generic emails were human- or LLM-written. The experiment involved about 7,700 participants, and personalisation cost approximately $0.03 per email. Caveat: participants knew they were in a study and had prior security training, so real-world click rates could differ.
  • Source: https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity26/presentation/czybik

2. Standard phishing training barely moves the needle (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Title: Understanding the Efficacy of Phishing Training in Practice
  • Authors: Grant Ho, Ariana Mirian, Elisa Luo, Khang Tong, Euyhyun Lee, Lin Liu, Christopher A. Longhurst, Christian Dameff, Stefan Savage, Geoffrey M. Voelker
  • Venue / Date: 2025 IEEE Symposium on Security and Privacy (SP), San Francisco, May 2025; pp. 37–54, DOI: 10.1109/SP61157.2025.00076
  • Key finding: In an 8-month randomised controlled experiment with over 19,500 employees at UC San Diego Health and 10 simulated phishing campaigns, there was no significant relationship between recently completing annual mandated cybersecurity training and failing a phishing simulation. Embedded training (shown after clicking a test phish) reduced the likelihood of clicking a later phish by only about 2%. Engagement was the bottleneck: 75% of users spent a minute or less on embedded training material, and one-third closed the training page immediately without engaging.
  • Source: https://www.computer.org/csdl/proceedings-article/sp/2025/223600a076/21B7RjYyG9q

3. Scam texts manufacture urgency with grammar, not just threats (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Title: "Account suspension. Verify now": using systemic-functional linguistics to improve phishing text advisories for vulnerable groups
  • Authors: Desiree Kawabata (Griffith University, Gold Coast, Australia)
  • Venue / Date: Humanities and Social Sciences Communications (Nature Portfolio), published 22 October 2026; DOI: 10.1057/s41599-026-09540-7
  • Key finding: Comparing 106 Australian phishing text messages with legitimate texts from the organisations scammers most imitated, the study found a recurring linguistic recipe for pressure: particular clause types, time/location/manner markers, high modality ("must," "now"), and conjunctions that together create urgency and authority. The author flags older adults and people from non-English-speaking backgrounds as especially exposed. Important limit, stated by the study itself: it is exploratory — these features are likely to manipulate recipients through emotional and cognitive pressure, not proven to cause clicks.
  • Source: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-026-09540-7

4. Overconfidence and social pressure predict who gets scammed (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Title: Personal, environmental and behavioral predictors associated with online fraud victimization among adults
  • Authors: Vimala Balakrishnan, Umayma Ahhmed, Faris Basheer (Universiti Malaya)
  • Venue / Date: PLOS ONE, 2025 (20(1): e0317232; PMID: 39879178)
  • Key finding: Across two Malaysian adult survey samples (n = 820 in Jan 2023 and n = 629 in Nov 2023–Jan 2024), two factors significantly predicted online-fraud victimisation: overconfidence (odds ratio 1.453, p = 0.005) and social influence (odds ratio 1.225, p = 0.006). Women showed higher risk in this sample (odds ratio 1.440, p = 0.045). Trust in banks, by contrast, did not significantly predict victimisation — scammers impersonate trusted brands, so trust alone doesn't explain who falls for it. Caveat: self-reported survey data from Malaysia, not a global victim census.
  • Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11778706/

5. AI-rewritten voice-phishing scripts slip past detectors (PREPRINT)

  • Title: Talking Like a Phisher: LLM-Based Attacks on Voice Phishing Classifiers
  • Authors: Wenhao Li, Selvakumar Manickam, Yung-wey Chong, Shankar Karuppayah
  • Venue / Date: arXiv:2507.16291, submitted 22 July 2025 — preprint, not peer-reviewed
  • Key finding: Researchers used four commercial LLMs to rewrite real voice-phishing (vishing) scripts with prompt engineering and semantic obfuscation, then tested them against machine-learning classifiers trained on a real Korean vishing dataset (KorCCViD). Transcripts rewritten by GPT-4o cut classifier accuracy by up to 30.96% while keeping the same deceptive meaning (high BERTScore similarity), and each rewrite took under 9 seconds on average at negligible cost per query. Takeaway: voice-scam detectors trained on known scripts are fragile against AI paraphrasing.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.16291

6. An AI that baits scammers in real time — without sharing victims' data (PREPRINT)

  • Title: AI-in-the-Loop: Privacy Preserving Real-Time Scam Detection and Conversational Scambaiting by Leveraging LLMs and Federated Learning
  • Authors: Ismail Hossain, Sai Puppala, Sajedul Talukder, Md Jahangir Alam
  • Venue / Date: arXiv:2509.05362, submitted 4 September 2025 (v4, 20 January 2026); arXiv record notes acceptance at the 26th Privacy Enhancing Technologies Symposium (PETS 2026) — preprint, not yet peer-reviewed as published
  • Key finding: Instead of only blocking scams after the fact, this system joins the conversation: an instruction-tuned AI detects a live scam chat and keeps the scammer engaged ("scambaiting") while minimising harm. In the authors' evaluations it produced fluent, engaging replies (perplexity as low as 22.3, engagement ≈ 0.80), and under federated learning (models updated without sharing raw chat data) it held up for 30 rounds with engagement ≈ 0.80, relevance ≈ 0.74, and personal-data leakage ≤ 0.0085. The trade-off is explicit: stricter safety moderation leaks less personal information but shortens engagement; looser settings engage longer but raise privacy risk.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.05362v1

TODAY'S SAFETY TIP

Never grant screen-sharing or remote access to an unsolicited caller — no matter which company they claim to be. Today's Amazon-impersonation wave works exactly this way: a caller invents a problem with your account, then asks you to share your screen or install remote-access software so they can "fix" it. Once they are on your device, they don't need you to send money — they can move it themselves. If you get such a call, hang up, open the company's app or website yourself (never via a link or number the caller gives you), and check your account there. No legitimate company will ever ask you to share your screen or hand over ID documents to "verify" an account problem.
