## TOP STORIES (ranked by importance)

### 1. Scammers exploit the global Microsoft 365 outage with fake "restore your emails" phishing
- **Outlet:** Microsoft Threat Intelligence (via X) / Accountancy Daily / Australian Broker News
- **Date:** November 13, 2026
- **Summary:** Within hours of the global Microsoft 365 outage that began November 12 (knocking out Outlook and Teams for roughly 36 hours), scammers launched a credential-phishing campaign impersonating Microsoft service-health notifications. The emails — subject lines following the pattern "Outage – " plus a six-character code — falsely claimed messages held during the outage would be deleted within 24 hours unless recipients clicked to restore them, using sender domains moments-notice.com, planetmaison.com and newbooker.com with SendGrid tracking links redirecting to reallifeaccounting.com. The same day, the UK tax authority HMRC warned that fraudsters were calling taxpayers pretending to be Microsoft and asking for HMRC login details, and Australian banks including ING warned customers to expect fake tech-support calls exploiting the outage.
- **Why it matters:** A textbook case of scammers riding a real, high-profile disruption: the outage gave the phishing emails instant credibility, and the targets were businesses and taxpayers already confused about their email. Microsoft stressed the notifications are fraudulent and urged users to check outage status only via the Microsoft 365 admin centre.
- **Source:** https://www.accountancydaily.co/scammers-target-hmrc-logins-amid-microsoft-outage
- **Corroboration:** https://www.indianwitness.com/news/business/microsoft-urges-users-not-to-trust-unexpected-outage-alerts-asking/ ; https://www.brokernews.com.au/news/breaking-news/banks-warn-scammers-will-exploit-microsoft-friday-13th-outage-290312.aspx
- **Date note:** The phishing emails were detected by Microsoft on Nov 13; Microsoft's public warning circulated Nov 13–14.

### 2. Operation Endgame seizes 235 servers and 257 domains; 6.4 million stolen logins recovered
- **Outlet:** Europol / U.S. Department of Justice (reported Nov 13 by Cybernews, SecurityPointBreak, others)
- **Date:** Europol announcement November 12, 2026; U.S. DOJ release and Have I Been Pwned data load November 13, 2026
- **Summary:** Police in nine countries (Bulgaria, Canada, Denmark, Germany, Moldova, Netherlands, Poland, UK, US), coordinated by Europol and Eurojust, dismantled infrastructure behind three malware families — Gootloader, CastleRAT and StealC — seizing 235 servers and 257 domains and securing about 6.4 million stolen login credentials belonging to more than 40,000 victims. For Gootloader alone, 14 servers and 218 domains were taken down and 681 infected websites cleaned up. The DOJ says Gootloader infected at least 25,000 computers since 2018 and was used in ransomware attacks on at least 44 U.S. victims causing about $28.8 million in losses. On Nov 13, Have I Been Pwned loaded ~179,700 email addresses and 893,000 unique passwords from CastleRAT victims.
- **Why it matters:** Gootloader spread through compromised websites via fake legal documents — a social-engineering lure — and fed ransomware gangs. The seized logins still work until victims change them, so the haul is a warning, not a rescue; readers should check Have I Been Pwned and rotate passwords.
- **Source:** https://cybernews.com/news/europol-disrupts-gootloader-malware-network/
- **Corroboration:** https://securitypointbreak.com/2026/11/13/operation-endgame-castlerat-have-i-been-pwned/

### 3. FTC halts phantom debt-collection operation that took $20M+ for debts people didn't owe
- **Outlet:** Federal Trade Commission (announced Nov 13; covered by Collection Industry News)
- **Date:** November 13, 2026 (temporary restraining order issued November 5, 2026)
- **Summary:** At the FTC's request, a federal court temporarily halted and froze the assets of McDonnell & Associates LLC (also operating as First Capital Group, Maxwell & Bryant Group and Lewis, McDonnell & Associates) and owners Oscar Gerardo Gonzalez Martin and Oscar Ruiz Canela. The complaint alleges the operation falsely claimed to be a law firm or affiliated with attorneys and used high-pressure threats to coerce consumers into paying debts they often did not owe — a practice known as phantom debt collection. Consumers paid more than $20 million to the defendants. The FTC alleges violations of the FTC Act, the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act and the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act.
- **Why it matters:** Phantom debt collection is one of the most feared consumer scams because the callers sound official and threaten legal action. The case is a reminder that a real collector must validate a debt in writing — and that threats of immediate arrest or lawsuits over the phone are a red flag.
- **Source:** https://collectionindustrynews.com/ftc-action-results-in-court-issuing-temporary-restraining-order-halting-phantom-debt-collection-operation/

### 6. Device-code phishing hijacks a Microsoft Teams sign-in
- **Outlet:** Help Net Security (via Wisevoter summary, Nov 13)
- **Date:** November 13, 2026
- **Summary:** Attackers impersonated a senior vice president at another construction firm and lured an employee to a fake Microsoft Teams meeting page via a Google share link. The page displayed a Microsoft device code disguised as a "Booking Reference" with a countdown timer; when the victim approved it on Microsoft's real sign-in page, the attackers obtained a valid session token. The phishing kit was delivered as AES-256-GCM ciphertext with its decryption key on the same page, and restarted a new device-code session when Microsoft returned an error. Managed security provider Huntress's security operations center spotted the incident before the attackers could use the stolen token.
- **Why it matters:** Device-code phishing defeats normal phishing defences because the victim sees Microsoft's genuine sign-in page. Anyone who is ever asked to enter a code shown on a meeting-invite page should stop — legitimate Teams meetings never ask for a device code.
- **Source:** https://wisevoter.com/world/2026/11/13/device-code-phishing-microsoft-teams-sign-in

## NEW & EMERGING SCAMS

### 1. Device-code phishing via fake AI event pages — OpenAI, SpaceX and Anthropic impersonated
- **Date:** Push Security analysis, November 5, 2026 (covered by BleepingComputer the same day)
- **How it works:** A phishing campaign used fake event-registration pages for OpenAI Academy, a SpaceX product briefing, and an Anthropic session at AWS re:Invent to take over Microsoft and Google work accounts. Visitors were promised rewards (three months of ChatGPT Pro, Grok Heavy, or a "SpaceX share") for signing in. On the Microsoft side the trick is device-code phishing: the victim types a short code into Microsoft's real sign-in page, approves their real MFA prompt, and the attacker's session gets the tokens — the URL is real, the MFA prompt is real, and only the requester is fake. The Google side used a reverse proxy sitting between the victim and Google's real sign-in page to steal passwords, MFA responses, and session cookies. Device-code phishing has gone from a nation-state technique to something sold as a service (kits named Kali365, ZoomMock, MSDRoot, SkyW4lker). No victim counts or confirmed losses were reported.
- **Who it targets:** Startup founders and staff, and anyone with a Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace work account — especially people who follow AI-company events.
- **Why it's new:** This campaign defeats ordinary MFA without breaking it: the victim completes the genuine login flow themselves, so push approvals and authenticator codes offer no protection. The fix is structural — block the device-code flow in Microsoft Conditional Access, and move to passkeys or security keys — plus the usual rule of never signing in through a link from an event invite.
- **Source:** https://kunalvohra.com/blog/device-code-phishing-fake-ai-events

### 2. Holiday smishing wave — fake SSA "survey" texts and parcel-delivery texts
- **Date:** SSA OIG scam alert, November 10, 2026; parcel-delivery warnings, November 13, 2026
- **How it works:** Two smishing waves are running into the holiday shopping season. (a) The Social Security Administration's Office of the Inspector General posted a scam alert on November 10: scammers are texting people that their Social Security account was "flagged for an incomplete survey response" and urging them to "verify your information" via a link. The OIG says it has received 48 reports of this scam in just 30 days. (b) Fake USPS/FedEx/DHL "package on hold" texts — claiming an incomplete address or unpaid fee and pushing a link to a look-alike site that harvests card details — are surging as holiday shipping ramps up. The FTC found fake package-delivery alerts were the single most-reported text scam category of 2024, with $470 million lost to text scams that year. USPS does not send unsolicited texts about delivery problems, and messages you initiate yourself never contain a link.
- **Who it targets:** Everyone with a phone — the SSA texts target the general public; the parcel texts exploit anyone expecting holiday deliveries.
- **Why it's new:** The SSA variant is a fresh pretext, flagged by the agency's own watchdog this week; the parcel variant is a seasonal surge of a known scam. In both cases the fix is the same: don't tap the link or reply — delete the text, forward it to 7726, and check the real account by going to the official site or app yourself.
- **Source:** https://dunlap.today/a-social-security-text-about-a-survey-you-never-finished-a-state-penalty-email-sent-to-tennessee-businesses-and-a-10-question-holiday-scam-quiz-most-folks-missed-your-scam-watch/ ; https://newsnationonline.com/international/usps-delivery-text-scam-how-to-spot-report/

### 3. AI-built fake holiday stores — a new one reported every ~3 minutes
- **Date:** Scamwise 2026 Holiday Shopping Scam Report, October 20, 2026; NordVPN announcement, November 4, 2026; Swiss NCSC warning, November 10, 2026
- **How it works:** Scammers no longer fake one product — they fake the entire store, with a full catalog, return policy, and paid ad campaign. Users report a new fake store roughly every 3 minutes (about 500 a day); fake holiday-themed stores grew 5x in 60 days; 85% of fake stores disappear within two weeks; 74–75% use AI-generated images, media, and text; at least 1 in 5 traces back to an ad on a major platform, with Meta and Google the preferred channels. Separately, NordVPN announced on November 4 that it traced more than 10,000 counterfeit shop domains to a single cloned template impersonating 166 fashion and luxury brands across 26 countries — 1,549 were fully working shops on November 3 — and that Perplexity had recommended two of the shops as trustworthy. Switzerland's National Cyber Security Centre warned on November 10 that it is already seeing a surge in fake AI-generated shops and parcel phishing ahead of Black Friday (November 27) and Cyber Monday (November 30).
- **Who it targets:** Holiday shoppers, especially bargain hunters clicking ads on social media and search.
- **Why it's new:** Polish is no longer proof: AI writes flawless shop text, realistic product photos, and fake reviews, and the stores are often hosted on well-known e-commerce platforms (myshopify.com was the most common domain in confirmed cases) and promoted through real ads. The stores vanish fast — most within two weeks — so by the time a victim complains, the shop is gone. Pay by credit card, never by bank transfer, gift card, or crypto, and check the seller and domain before paying, not the look of the site.
- **Source:** https://factash.com/blog/black-friday-scams-2026-fake-online-stores/ ; https://scamdrill.com/blog/fake-store-imposter-ads-black-friday-2026 ; https://www.bacs.admin.ch/en/26w45-en
- **Note:** Report figures are from Scamwise's own user submissions, not a census of all fake stores online.

### 4. Task scams evolve — fake job lures behind a $6.3B crypto theft
- **Date:** Binance report, November 3, 2026; MHA/I4C biometric warning re-flagged November 2026
- **How it works:** India's Ministry of Home Affairs, through I4C's National Cybercrime Threat Analytics Unit, has warned job seekers about a newer twist on the task scam: fake online interviews that harvest face and eye biometrics (reported May 2026, flagged again in November 2026 coverage). Fraudsters pose as recruitment agencies, run a fake interview, and get the victim to complete a face or eye scan on an unknown website — presumably to build deepfake or identity-fraud material. The underlying task scam is unchanged in structure: a WhatsApp/Telegram message offers easy part-time work (liking videos, rating products), pays small amounts at first, then demands "prepaid task" deposits to unlock earnings — victims escalate through sunk-cost pressure and lose large sums (one reported case: ₹720,000). Separately, Binance reported on November 3 that a fake-job approach — an engineer contacted on a job platform, interviewed over a messaging app by a fake CEO, then asked to download a malicious code repository — was the entry point for a crypto theft it estimates at about $6.3 billion, attributed by Binance and the FBI to North Korean hackers.
- **Who it targets:** Job seekers, students, and people looking for side income — especially on WhatsApp and Telegram; the Binance case shows the same lure also targets tech professionals for account and network access.
- **Why it's new:** Two escalations in one familiar scam family: the lure now harvests biometrics that can't be changed like a password, and the same "job interview" pretext has been used as the opening move in one of the largest crypto thefts ever reported. The rule that kills every variant is the same: a real job never asks you to pay to work, deposit money to unlock earnings, or scan your face on an unknown site.
- **Source:** https://www.techyami.in/2026/11/fake-job-offer-scams-in-india-2026-task.html ; https://www.wra.com.br/en/post/fake-job-scam-binance-attack-lessons
- **Note:** The $6.3B figure and North Korea attribution come from Binance's own report and were not independently verified; the MHA/I4C biometric warning dates to May 2026, re-flagged November 2026.

### 5. Zelle "business account upgrade" scam targets Facebook Marketplace sellers
- **Date:** Write-ups published November 10, 2026; same pattern documented in scam libraries reviewed November 3, 2026
- **How it works:** A seller-side Marketplace scam is circulating again this month: a buyer agrees to a sale immediately, claims to have paid by Zelle, and then the seller receives an email that looks like it's from Zelle saying the payment is on hold until the seller "upgrades to a business account" — for a fee (reported amounts around $250–$300). The buyer promises to cover the fee or add it to the price. The emails come from lookalike Gmail addresses (one example: onlinezelleserverllc@gmail.com), not from Zelle. Zelle has no paid business upgrade, and Zelle payments arrive in the recipient's bank app — Zelle never emails to release funds. The only real transfer is the "upgrade fee" or "refund" the seller sends.
- **Who it targets:** People selling on Facebook Marketplace (and similar local marketplaces) who accept payment apps.
- **Why it's new:** This flips the usual Marketplace script — the scammer plays the eager buyer, not the too-cheap seller — and it exploits the holiday selling rush, when sellers are fielding many messages and answering on autopilot. The tell is simple and absolute: you never have to send money to receive money. Confirm payment only by opening your own bank or payment app and seeing the money in your balance.
- **Source:** https://www.scanmyshadow.com/blog/2026/11/zelle-business-account-upgrade-scam-marketplace.html ; https://gmork.com/scams/facebook-marketplace-zelle-buyer-scam

## RESEARCH DESK

### 1. Your Instagram posts can be turned into a phishing email written just for you (PREPRINT)
- **Title:** Context-Aware Spear Phishing: Generative AI-Enabled Attacks Against Individuals via Public Social Media Data
- **Authors:** Elham Pourabbas Vafa, Sayak Saha Roy, Shirin Nilizadeh (University of Texas at Arlington)
- **Venue / Date:** Preprint — not yet peer-reviewed. arXiv:2605.11268, submitted May 11, 2026; revised September 30, 2026
- **Key finding:** AI can turn your public Instagram posts into a phishing email written just for you — no account hack needed. The researchers built profiles from 200 public Instagram accounts and generated more than 18,000 phishing emails with five AI models (GPT-4, Claude 3 Haiku, Gemini 1.5 Flash, Gemma 7B, Llama 3.3); as few as 10 posts per person were enough to personalise an attack. In a 70-person user study, the AI-written messages beat real-world phishing emails on persuasiveness and drew less suspicion. Default settings on three popular AI safety tools (Llama Guard, ShieldGemma, WildGuard) caught only 0–19% of the attack requests — though tuning them for this specific abuse raised detection to as high as 99%. Caveat, per the paper: the study measured perceived suspicion in a controlled setting, not real-world click rates.
- **Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.11268

### 2. Scammers who write in your own language — and ask for help — are far more convincing (PEER-REVIEWED)
- **Title:** Language as Lure: A Naturalistic Study on Pasifika Phishing Susceptibility
- **Authors:** Eric Spero, Isa Seow, Lucas Betts, Eddie Fuatimau, Robert Biddle, Danielle Lottridge, Giovanni Russello (University of Auckland; Biddle also Carleton University)
- **Venue / Date:** Peer-reviewed — Twenty-First Symposium on Usable Privacy and Security (SOUPS 2025), August 11–12, 2025, Seattle, WA
- **Key finding:** In a real-world simulation with 2,000 government employees in a Melanesian country, each person got one simulated phishing email a month for four months, in English or the local language. When the email was a personal request for help written in the local language, people clicked more than when the same email arrived in English. The starkest case: a "church document" request in the local language got a click rate above 30% — more than double the English version's rate. People said they trusted local-language mail more, assuming scammers wouldn't bother using it.
- **Source:** https://www.usenix.org/conference/soups2025/presentation/spero

### 3. An AI that explains smishing in plain words helps people spot the next one (PEER-REVIEWED)
- **Title:** Can You Walk Me Through It? Explainable SMS Phishing Detection using LLM-based Agents
- **Authors:** Yizhu Wang, Haoyu Zhai, Chenkai Wang, Qingying Hao, Nick A. Cohen, Roopa Foulger, Jonathan A. Handler, Gang Wang (University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign; Cohen, Foulger, Handler at OSF Healthcare)
- **Venue / Date:** Peer-reviewed — Twenty-First Symposium on Usable Privacy and Security (SOUPS 2025), August 11–12, 2025, Seattle, WA
- **Key finding:** Text-message scams are hard to spot because the messages are so short — so the researchers built SmishX, an AI system that gathers outside context (who the sender claims to be, where links redirect, what the linked page looks like) and then explains in plain words why a text is dangerous. On real-world SMS datasets it detected phishing texts with 98.8% overall accuracy, beating existing methods. In a user study of 175 people, reading SmishX's explanations significantly improved people's own ability to spot phishing texts, across all age groups — including adults over 60, the group the FBI says files the most fraud complaints.
- **Source:** https://www.usenix.org/conference/soups2025/presentation/wang

### 4. Changing a few words can break a voice-deepfake detector (PREPRINT)
- **Title:** What You Read Isn't What You Hear: Linguistic Sensitivity in Deepfake Speech Detection
- **Authors:** Binh Nguyen, Shuji Shi, Ryan Ofman, Thai Le
- **Venue / Date:** Preprint — not yet peer-reviewed. arXiv:2505.17513, submitted May 23, 2025
- **Key finding:** Voice-deepfake detectors listen to how something sounds — but changing the words can break them. The researchers made small wording changes to the transcript of a fake voice clip and found attack success rates above 60% against several open-source detectors; one commercial detector's accuracy collapsed from 100% on synthetic audio to just 32%. To show the real-world stakes, they replicated the Brad Pitt audio-deepfake scam and used wording tweaks to completely bypass commercial detectors. The lesson: defences that only check acoustics are not enough — the words themselves matter.
- **Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.17513

### 5. A friendly chatbot can be turned into a pickpocket with a clever system prompt (PEER-REVIEWED)
- **Title:** Malicious LLM-Based Conversational AI Makes Users Reveal Personal Information
- **Authors:** Xiao Zhan, Juan-Carlos Carrillo, William Seymour, Jose Such (King's College London; Carrillo and Such also VRAIN, Universitat Politècnica de València)
- **Venue / Date:** Peer-reviewed — 34th USENIX Security Symposium (USENIX Security '25), August 2025, Seattle, WA (arXiv preprint June 13, 2025)
- **Key finding:** In a randomised controlled trial with 502 participants, chatbots instructed to play "investigator" or "detective" roles extracted significantly more personal information than benign chatbots — and people barely noticed the risk. The tactics that leaned on the social nature of privacy (reciprocity, emphasising benefits) worked best while making people feel least at risk. As reported from the study's results: more than 90% of participants chatting with a malicious bot disclosed personal data, versus 24% when asked the same questions on a form — and over 80% answered every single personal-data request, versus 6% on the form.
- **Source:** https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity25/technical-sessions (paper also at https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.11680)

## TODAY'S SAFETY TIP

**Zelle is not a safe way to pay a stranger — and you never have to send money to receive money.** Five of today's seven victim accounts involve Zelle, and the pattern is always the same: a fake payment screenshot, an email saying your payment is "on hold" until you upgrade or send a fee, or a "support" caller telling you to send $500 back to release the funds. Zelle works like a debit card — once the money leaves, there is no buyer protection and no way to get it back. When buying or selling on Marketplace: meet in person, inspect the item, and confirm payment only by opening your own bank app and seeing the money actually in your balance — never trust a screenshot, an email, or a phone call. Cash in hand remains the safest Marketplace payment. And the same "verify it yourself" rule applies to the outage phishing wave today: if an email claims your Microsoft account needs restoring, don't click — open the app or type the address yourself.
