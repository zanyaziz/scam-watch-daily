NEW & EMERGING SCAMS

1. QR-code "quishing" — Navy Federal warning dated October 25, 2026

  • What's new: Navy Federal Credit Union published a dedicated warning on QR-code scams dated October 25, 2026 — the clearest Oct 25-dated emerging-scam item in this run.
  • How it works: Scammers place malicious QR codes — in emails, texts, posters, menus, parking meters and stickers placed over legitimate codes — that lead to fake sites harvesting login, payment or personal data, or prompting a payment to the wrong recipient. Because the URL is hidden inside the code, victims cannot inspect the destination before scanning the way they can with a written link.
  • Who's targeted: Bank and credit-union customers, diners, drivers paying for parking, and anyone scanning codes for payments or account verification.
  • Tip: After scanning, preview the URL and stop if the domain does not exactly match the merchant or service. Check physical stickers for tampering, and confirm payment details inside the official app instead.
  • Source: https://www.navyfederal.org/about-us/security-center/latest-scams/qr-code-scam.html

2. Fake law firm offering to "recover" lost funds installs AnyDesk on victims' computers (DEVELOPING — single aggregator source, Oct 25 timestamp)

  • What's new: A report timestamped October 25, 2026 (08:52 UTC) describes a fake law firm posing as a funds-recovery service. Single aggregator-style source, not independently corroborated — labelled developing.
  • How it works: The supposed firm targets people who have already lost money to scams, offering legal recovery. Victims are directed to install AnyDesk remote-access software, giving the "firm" control of their computer — converting a recovery pitch into a second theft. This is the classic recovery-scam pattern: the second scam feeds on the first loss.
  • Who's targeted: Prior scam victims, initially reported in South Africa with claimed reach beyond it.
  • Tip: No legitimate lawyer or recovery service needs remote access to your computer. Never install AnyDesk, TeamViewer or similar software at a stranger's direction, and treat any unsolicited "we can get your money back" approach as a scam.
  • Source: https://www.blaquiere-talks-no-to-racism.co.za/fake-law-firm-pretending-to-recover-lost-funds-targets-victims-across-south-africa-and-beyond-

3. Fake escrow / deposit diversion in home purchases — one letter changed in the title company's domain (Oct 16 warning, Oct 25 syndication)

  • What's new: The BBB case in Top Story 3, syndicated by CBS Chicago / WBBM on October 25, 2026. (Also Top Story 3.)
  • How it works: Scammers register a copycat domain for a real title/escrow company — sometimes differing by a single letter — build a convincing fake site or email thread, send the buyer a payment link or wiring instructions, and divert the deposit or full closing funds to an account they control. By the time the real title company asks where the money is, the wire is gone.
  • Who's targeted: First-time and other homebuyers in the closing window, when a six-figure wire is expected and urgency is high.
  • Tip: Before wiring anything, call the title company on a number you look up yourself — never the number in the email or on the site that sent the instructions — and read the domain character by character.
  • Sources: https://www.audacy.com/wbbm780/news/local/down-payment-scam-targets-new-homeowner ; https://www.bbb.org/article/news-releases/17198-bbb-warning-scammer-steals-74000-from-illinois-homebuyer-using-fake-title-company-website

4. Gift-card draining — balance emptied before the card is ever used (DEVELOPING — undated/recent explainers; no Oct 25 incident verified)

  • What's new: Fresh explainer coverage of a long-running retail tactic; no specific Oct 25 incident was verified in this run, so this is labelled developing/background rather than today's news.
  • How it works: Criminals record card numbers and PINs from cards on store racks (sometimes resealing the packaging), then monitor balances and drain the card as soon as it is activated at purchase — so the recipient finds a zero balance. Variants swap barcodes or tamper with packaging.
  • Who's targeted: Gift-card buyers and recipients, especially ahead of the holiday season.
  • Tip: Buy cards kept behind the counter where possible, inspect packaging for tampering, keep the receipt, and check the balance promptly after purchase.
  • Sources: https://www.aha.org/news/headline/2025-12-09-hhs-oig-warns-gift-card-draining-scam ; https://scamicide.com/2025/12/08/scam-of-the-day-december-9-2025-gift-card-draining/

5. Wrong-number text → crypto romance/investment ("pig butchering" pipeline) — FTC/IC3 guidance recirculating

  • What's new: Continuing fresh attention to the wrong-number opener as the entry point to long-con crypto fraud; the FTC consumer guidance page itself is undated in this run's check, so treat the tactic summary as verified guidance, not an Oct 25 event.
  • How it works: A "wrong number" text starts a friendly chat that moves to WhatsApp or Telegram, builds a romantic or friendly relationship over weeks, then introduces a crypto or investment "opportunity" on a fake platform. Small withdrawals may be allowed to build trust; large withdrawals are blocked behind "tax" or "unlock" fees, and the platform disappears. The FBI's IC3 public service announcement of September 30, 2025 describes the same wrong-number → social-engineering pattern.
  • Who's targeted: Anyone who replies to an unknown number; lonely and older adults are disproportionately targeted for the long-con phase.
  • Tip: Do not reply to wrong-number texts at all — replying confirms your number is live. Never invest on a platform introduced by someone you have only met by text.
  • Sources: https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2024/02/love-not-money-protect-yourself-romance-scams ; https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2025/PSA250930

6. Fake IT help-desk vishing — a call from "IT" asking for your password or MFA code (Oct 24 instalment)

  • What's new: Scamicide's "Scam of the day — October 24, 2026" instalment on IT help-desk scams (Part 2), published October 23–24, 2026.
  • How it works: A caller claiming to be from the victim's IT department or a vendor's support desk creates urgency — an account lockout, a security incident, a required update — and asks the employee to share a password, approve an MFA prompt, or install remote-access software. The aim is account takeover of workplace systems, often as the first step to business-email compromise or ransomware.
  • Who's targeted: Employees, especially at larger organisations with outsourced or centralised IT support they may not know by voice.
  • Tip: Real IT never asks for your password or MFA code. Hang up and call IT on the internal number you already have; approve only MFA prompts you initiated yourself.
  • Source: https://scamicide.com/2026/10/23/scam-of-the-day-october-24-2026-it-help-desk-scams-part-2/

RESEARCH DESK (2025–2026 research; peer-reviewed vs preprint labelled; all six are new to this newsletter)

1. PhishDebate — multi-agent debate cuts a single LLM detector's errors roughly in half (PREPRINT)

  • Authors / venue / date: Mohammed Aqil Shabeer Ahamadulla et al. — arXiv:2505.19199, submitted May 25, 2025; revised October 10, 2025. Not peer-reviewed.
  • Key finding, in plain language: Instead of one AI model judging a website alone, PhishDebate has several specialised agents argue — one checks the URL, one reads the page text, one inspects the code, one looks for brand impersonation — with a moderator and a judge reaching the verdict. On a balanced set of 794 legitimate and 766 phishing sites it reached 96.44% weighted accuracy, 96.54% precision and 96.46% F1, and — more importantly — cut error rates substantially versus a single model with the same backbone: false positives fell by 52.5% and false negatives by 29.8% relative to a GPT-4o single-agent baseline. Structure, not just a bigger model, did the work.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.19199

2. A current snapshot of LLM phishing detection — which models actually catch phish, and at what cost (PREPRINT)

  • Authors / venue / date: Kacper Leki, Kacper Daniel, Patryk Zięcina, Jan Brzozowski, Daniel Marczak — arXiv:2602.20758, submitted February 24, 2026. Not peer-reviewed.
  • Key finding, in plain language: A standardised benchmark of 977 URLs (713 legitimate, 264 phishing) run in October 2025 across 11 model configurations from 5 providers. Among standard hosted models, Gemini 2.5 Flash scored best (F1 0.923, false-positive rate 1.5%); Claude Sonnet 4.5 was worst (F1 0.774, FPR 13.0%) — provider choice alone moved F1 by about 15 points and false positives by nearly 9x. Reasoning models did not reliably help: turning reasoning on changed detection in only 18.2% of cases, and in one model family it improved F1 while doubling false positives. Latency ranged from 0.94s to 17.98s per URL, so the most accurate setup may be too slow or costly to run on every link.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.20758

3. When the AI shopping agent is the mark — LLM agents phished and scammed in a simulated marketplace (PREPRINT)

  • Authors / venue / date: Pratyush Ranjan Tiwari et al. — arXiv:2606.26291, submitted June 24, 2026. Not peer-reviewed.
  • Key finding, in plain language: Researchers built AgentShroudBench — 111 adversarial scenarios in a simulated shopping environment — and tested 12 widely used LLM agents against seller deception: fake discounts, manipulated reviews, impersonation and bait-and-switch. Every agent was vulnerable to some degree (overall susceptibility 22.7%–87.8%), and the more capable agent was the easier mark in the showcased comparison: Claude Sonnet 4.6 was deceived in 87.8% of runs (88.7% on seller-deception scenarios), while GPT-4o-mini was deceived in 46.4%. All 12 agents showed elevated susceptibility above 70% to at least one attack family. As agents start buying on people's behalf, scam defence has to cover the agent, not just the human.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.26291

4. The language of deception — linguistic style, not surface polish, changes how convincing a phish is (PREPRINT)

  • Authors / venue / date: Eesha Kokje, Ritu Agarwal, Rahul Telang — arXiv:2607.22785, submitted July 24, 2026. Not peer-reviewed.
  • Key finding, in plain language: Two pre-registered online experiments with 2,253 U.S. adults tested high- vs. low-quality phishing emails at two levels: surface quality (spelling, grammar, formatting) and stylistic quality (how the manipulation is linguistically framed). Once emails were competently written, surface polish had only a weak effect — but stylistic sophistication raised susceptibility by 5.45 percentage points in the controlled study and 3.23 points in a more naturalistic webmail simulation. The gap widened among frequent email users, the opposite of the "experience protects you" assumption. Fixing typos is no longer the tell to teach; the framing is.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.22785

5. People beat the machine at spotting deepfake video — 65.6% vs. 46.8% (PREPRINT)

  • Authors / venue / date: Mohammed Ahmed Subahi, Khalifa Al Mahmud Aunok, Abdullah Al Mamun Bulbul — arXiv:2512.04349, submitted December 4, 2025. Not peer-reviewed; modest sample, treat percentages as directional.
  • Key finding, in plain language: A within-subjects study (n=28) had people and a commercial detector (Hive AI) judge the same deepfake videos. Humans reached 65.6% accuracy with a mean response time of 13.3 seconds; the AI detector managed only 46.8% — below a coin flip — and was slower, at 24.6 seconds. The authors suggest hybrid workflows that route suspicious video to human review rather than relying on automated detection alone. Small sample, so the direction matters more than the exact figures.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.04349

6. Can people tell AI-written phish from human-written phish? Barely — and confidence misleads (PREPRINT)

  • Authors / venue / date: Xiyang Chen, Yunpeng Song, Zhongmin Dai — arXiv:2510.01619, submitted October 2, 2025. Not peer-reviewed; small exploratory study.
  • Key finding, in plain language: In two experiments with 36 participants judging human-written vs. LLM-generated phishing messages, average detection accuracy was 51.2% — essentially chance. People relied on surface cues like message length and formatting, which are exactly the cues modern LLMs reproduce well, and participants who felt confident were not more accurate than those who did not. The authors argue for interventions that correct overconfidence, not just more "spot the phish" training built on outdated tells.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.01619

TODAY'S SAFETY TIP

Before you scan a QR code or wire a deposit, verify through a channel you chose — not the one that contacted you. Sunday's two clearest lessons pair up: Navy Federal's Oct 25 quishing warning (codes hide their destination, and a sticker can be pasted over a real code) and the BBB's $74,000 homebuyer loss (a fake title-company site, one letter off, diverted a closing wire). Do this: (1) after scanning any QR code, read the previewed URL character by character and stop if the domain is not exactly the merchant's or bank's real one — and never enter banking credentials on a page a QR code opened; (2) for any wire, deposit or closing payment, call the company on a number you look up yourself and confirm the account details out loud before sending — instructions that arrived by email, text or a payment link are not verification; (3) if a "recovery" service, lawyer or support agent then offers to help and asks you to install AnyDesk or share your screen, hang up — that is the second scam, not the rescue.
