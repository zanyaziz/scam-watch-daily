Dutch gambling industry sues Meta over illegal gambling ads on Facebook and Instagram

Lead

Dutch gambling industry sues Meta over illegal gambling ads on Facebook and Instagram — A Dutch trade association filed suit in Amsterdam's district court on September 28, saying more than 90% of the gambling ads it reviewed on Facebook came from illegal operators and that Meta removes only a small fraction. Meanwhile, U.S. prosecutors moved to seize crypto from a Coinbase account-takeover scam, a Delhi court ordered Meta to pull AI deepfakes pairing a woman with India's prime minister, and scam tech-support ads evaded Google's filters on at least 284 sites.

TOP STORIES (ranked by importance)

1. Dutch gambling industry sues Meta over illegal gambling ads on Facebook and Instagram

  • Outlet: SBC News (trade press), corroborated by AlienWP
  • Date: September 28, 2026 (lawsuit filed; SBC News reported Sept 29)
  • Summary: Dutch trade association VNLOK filed suit against Meta in the Amsterdam District Court, accusing it of letting illegal gambling ads proliferate on Facebook and Instagram. VNLOK's surveys found over 90% of the gambling ads it reviewed on Facebook came from illegal operators, and that Meta removed only a small fraction (11% in May, 15% in June), often too late. The group alleges Meta breached duties under the EU Digital Services Act and seeks a ruling forcing pre-emptive blocking of illegal ads. A Dutch parliamentary motion that could let the Gaming Authority fine platforms for failing to remove illegal ads was up for a vote on September 29.
  • Why it matters: A direct test of platform liability for scam- and fraud-linked advertising under the DSA — the same platform-accountability theme the FTC is pushing in the U.S. If VNLOK wins, it sets a precedent for pre-emptive ad-policing duties for Meta, Google, TikTok, and others.
  • Source: https://sbcnews.co.uk/marketing/2026/09/29/meta-vnlok-gambling-ads/

4. FTC's move to hold platforms liable for impersonation scam ads draws legal analysis

  • Outlet: IPWatchdog (legal publication)
  • Date: September 28, 2026 (analysis; the underlying FTC ANPRM issued September 24, 2026)
  • Summary: In its September 24 Advance Notice of Proposed Rulemaking, the FTC asked for public input on whether to expand its Rule on Impersonation of Government and Businesses to cover platforms whose ad-optimization tools help impersonation scams reach consumers. The agency cited over 1 million imposter-scam reports in 2025 with nearly $3.5 billion in losses — about 30% of victims first contacted via social media, at $2.1 billion in losses — and argued platforms "internalize the revenue but externalize the risk." IPWatchdog's analysis notes the proposed rules could force platforms to vet advertisers and monitor AI-driven ad targeting.
  • Why it matters: The FTC is shifting enforcement from individual scammers to the ad infrastructure of Facebook, Google, and others — the most consequential U.S. regulatory development for scam ads in years, and it pairs directly with the Dutch lawsuit against Meta in item 1.
  • Source: https://ipwatchdog.com/2026/09/28/ftc-seeks-comment-on-proposed-rule-to-crack-down-on-platform-facilitation-of-impersonation-scam-ads/

5. Google Ads carried tech-support scareware campaign hitting Windows and Mac users

  • Outlet: SC World (SC Media brief), Novknown, NeoTeo
  • Date: September 28, 2026 (underlying research from Netskope, Sept 24; Ars Technica Sept 25, with Google's statement)
  • Summary: Over 250 Google Ads campaign IDs across at least 284 legitimate publisher sites delivered fake security warnings that made browsers appear locked — fullscreen mode, hidden cursor, disabled exit keys — pushing victims to call fraudulent support numbers. Netskope observed clicks from 619 customer organizations (62% U.S.) between August 31 and September 14. The pages imitated Microsoft Defender on Windows and Apple alerts on Mac. Google told Ars Technica on September 25 it was investigating: "We have zero tolerance for scams."
  • Why it matters: Tech-support scams remain the most abused malicious-ad category, and this campaign evaded Google's filters despite the company's 2018 ban on third-party tech-support ads — fresh ammunition for the FTC's platform-accountability argument.
  • Source: https://www.scworld.com/brief/google-ads-deliver-sophisticated-tech-support-scam-targeting-windows-and-mac-users

NEW & EMERGING SCAMS

1. Hay-sale "triangle" scam hits buyers and sellers in one loop

  • What's new: A scammer orders hay from a farmer using stolen payment credentials, then relists that hay on Facebook Marketplace at below-market price. The buyer pays the scammer (often via Zelle); the scammer gives the farmer the buyer's address for delivery. The original payment is later reversed, leaving the farmer unpaid and the buyer with no recourse.
  • Who flagged it: Adams County (Colorado) Sheriff's Office — official alert issued Sept 27, 2026; reported by The Badger.
  • Why it matters: Classic triangle fraud layered with stolen-credential payments and Marketplace relisting. Red flag: the person you pay is not the person delivering the goods.
  • Source: https://denver.thebadger.news/articles/2026-09-27-adams-county-sheriff-hay-payment-scam-warning

2. Fake GIWA mainnet drains ~766 ETH — developing

  • What's new: Scammers spun up a fake "GIWA mainnet" (the Upbit-backed Ethereum Layer 2) re-using the real Chain ID 9134, making counterfeit RPCs and bridges look authentic. About 1,335 wallet addresses bridged real ETH (reported ~767.65 ETH total, ~766.25 ETH drained) before funds disappeared. GIWA clarified Sept 27 that its mainnet has not launched; "DYOR compensation" of over 200 ETH has reportedly been distributed to victims.
  • Status: DEVELOPING. Single crypto-press source; GIWA's own statement confirms the "not live" fact, but the drained-amount figures are thinly sourced. Treat precise ETH numbers as unverified pending broader coverage.
  • Source: https://cryptocompass.com/articles/crypto-scam-alert-giwa-mainnet-not-live-fake-chain-drained-766-eth

3. Active two-wave Zoom + DocuSign phishing campaign (Kaspersky)

  • What's new: Kaspersky flagged an ongoing corporate campaign: wave 1 impersonated official DocuSign emails to corporate accounts across the Middle East, Latin America, Western Europe, Russia, Armenia and Azerbaijan; wave 2 (active now) poses as Zoom "your account is about to be disabled" notices, using credential-stealing links and embedded forms that harvest personal and credit-card details. More than 1,000 phishing emails detected.
  • Who flagged it: Kaspersky (Andrey Kovtun, Email Threats Protection Group Manager), Sept 28–29, 2026.
  • Why it matters: Deliberately low-tech — scammers are winning by mimicking ubiquitous workplace brands so busy employees miss the signs.
  • Source: https://www.kaspersky.co.za/about/press-releases/kaspersky-identifies-ongoing-phishing-email-campaign-mimicking-zoom-and-docusign

4. Fake HR/payroll desktop apps install ScreenConnect remote access

  • What's new: Scammers advertise fake Windows desktop clients for three U.S.-based HR and payroll platforms — none of which actually offers a desktop app — and the downloads silently install ConnectWise ScreenConnect, giving attackers persistent remote access. The scam sites were built with the AI app builder Lovable, hosted on Vercel, hidden behind a bot-challenge page, and the malicious installers are hosted on GitHub Releases pages so downloads point to a trusted domain.
  • Who flagged it: Allure Security, reported by The Register, Sept 25, 2026.
  • Why it matters: A novel social-engineering blend: AI-built websites plus trusted hosting plus legitimate remote-admin-tool abuse to own HR machines and their payroll data.
  • Source: https://www.theregister.com/security/2026/09/25/crooks-use-fake-desktop-apps-to-fool-hr-staff-into-giving-them-remote-access/5299226

5. Fake recruiter / task-job scam impersonating Blue Whale Apps

  • What's new: Scammers impersonating the real company Blue Whale Apps recruit via WhatsApp and social media for fake remote work, send victims to lookalike websites, then demand "investments," security deposits, or cryptocurrency to keep earning or unlock commissions. Blue Whale posted a fraud alert Sept 28 after multiple victim reports and reported the activity to the FBI's IC3. Pattern includes unusually high pay for simple online tasks and fake staffing-agency fronts.
  • Why it matters: A real company's own alert confirms the task-scam model is still evolving and name-squatting legitimate employers.
  • Source: https://www.phoneya.com/blog/fake-recruiter-task-job-scam

6. Parking-meter QR-code stickers — FTC warning recirculating

  • What's new: Scammers paste fake QR-code stickers over legitimate QR codes on parking meters; scans route drivers to cloned payment pages that steal card numbers, login credentials, or install malware. The FTC issued a consumer alert Sept 3, 2026 (coverage recirculating now): preview the URL before opening, inspect the sticker for signs of tampering, and verify via the official parking operator's app. Related wave: "brushing" scam packages (often Amazon/eBay-branded) containing QR codes that lead to phishing sites — the BBB says these are increasingly common.
  • Who flagged it: Federal Trade Commission (consumer alert, Sept 3, 2026); recent explainers via MalwareTips; brushing angle via BBB (Sept 15, 2026).
  • Sources: https://malwaretips.com/blogs/fake-parking-qr-code-scam/ and https://gmg-kprc-prod.cdn.arcpublishing.com/news/local/2026/09/15/brushing-scam-warning-what-to-do-if-you-receive-a-package-you-never-ordered/

7. Fake e-vite (Punchbowl-style) phishing wave — developing

  • What's new: A viral Instagram reel (AdviceWithErin, ~2.3M followers, posted Sept 24) flagged a phishing wave disguised as a party e-vite "from someone you know": the sender address is slightly off, many recipients are BCC'd, and the "view invitation" link opens a fake Gmail login page — entering credentials hands scammers SSO-linked access to bank accounts and social media. Dozens of comments say they received the same "Exclusive Digital Invitation" email, often from a compromised real contact.
  • Status: DEVELOPING. Social-media-sourced, not a security-firm report; independent technical verification is lacking.
  • Source: https://www.instagram.com/reel/DdrOPUzupZ9/

RESEARCH DESK (2025–2026 peer-reviewed research; preprints labeled)

1. First qualitative study of the pig-butchering scam lifecycle (peer-reviewed)

  • Title: "'Hello, is this Anna?': Unpacking the Lifecycle of Pig-Butchering Scams"
  • Authors: Rajvardhan Oak, Zubair Shafiq (UC Davis)
  • Venue/date: USENIX SOUPS 2025 (Aug 2025); also arXiv:2503.20821
  • Key finding: Based on in-depth interviews with 26 victims, the first qualitative analysis of pig butchering maps an organized lifecycle: staged trust-building, fraudulent investment platforms with fabricated returns, high-pressure extraction, then persistent re-engagement to amplify losses. Victims report lasting psychological and financial harm and heightened vulnerability to secondary "recovery" scams. Proposes concrete intervention points for social media and financial platforms.
  • Source: https://www.usenix.org/conference/soups2025/presentation/oak-butchering

2. How Reddit functions as scam education, scambaiting, and victim support (peer-reviewed)

  • Title: "Victims, Vigilantes, and Advice Givers: An Analysis of Scam-Related Discourse on Reddit"
  • Authors: Rajvardhan Oak, Zubair Shafiq (UC Davis)
  • Venue/date: USENIX SOUPS 2025 (Aug 2025)
  • Key finding: Thematic analysis of 2,435 scam-related Reddit posts shows dual functions: preventive communities (education, awareness) versus performative engagement (scambaiting as counteraction). The platform also works as a victim support system offering emotional validation and recovery strategies — grassroots knowledge-sharing that shapes digital-security behavior.
  • Source: https://www.usenix.org/conference/soups2025/accepted-papers

3. LLM-personalized spear phishing nearly triples click rates — at ~3 cents per email (peer-reviewed)

  • Title: "A Large-Scale Study of Personalized Phishing using Large Language Models"
  • Authors: Stefan Czybik et al. (Berlin Institute for the Foundations of Learning and Data)
  • Venue/date: USENIX Security '26 (Aug 2026)
  • Key finding: Field experiment with 7,700 participants: personal info harvested via web search, emails auto-generated by LLMs. Personalized spear phishing almost tripled the click rate versus generic phishing (whether generic emails were human- or LLM-written), at a personalization cost of only ~$0.03 per email. Authors call for reducing publicly linkable personal data and adding personalized attacks to awareness training.
  • Source: https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity26/presentation/czybik

4. Structured prompting lifts phishing-webpage detection to F1 0.93 (peer-reviewed workshop)

  • Title: "Adaptive Linguistic Prompting (ALP) Enhances Phishing Webpage Detection in Multimodal Large Language Models"
  • Authors: Atharva Bhargude, Ishan Gonehal, Chandler Haney, Dave Yoon, Kevin Zhu, Aaron Sandoval, Sean O'Brien, Kaustubh Vinnakota (Algoverse AI Research)
  • Venue/date: ACL 2025 Student Research Workshop (peer-reviewed workshop); also arXiv:2507.13357 (Jul 2025)
  • Key finding: A structured semantic-reasoning prompting method — linguistic pattern breakdown, urgency cues, manipulative diction — applied to multimodal LLMs (GPT-4o, Gemini 1.5 Pro) achieved an F1-score of 0.93 on phishing webpage detection, outperforming traditional approaches.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.13357v1

5. Production LLMs can be nudged into generating scam URLs and malicious code (PREPRINT — not peer-reviewed)

  • Title: "Scam2Prompt: A Scalable Framework for Auditing Malicious Scam Endpoints in Production LLMs"
  • Authors: Zhiyang Chen, Tara Saba, Xun Deng, Xujie Si, Fan Long (University of Toronto)
  • Venue/date: arXiv:2509.02372 (Oct 2, 2025) — preprint, no peer-review venue claimed
  • Key finding: Innocuous developer-style prompts triggered malicious URL/code generation in 4.24% of cases across 4 production LLMs (GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini, Llama-4-Scout, DeepSeek-V3). On a benchmark of 1,559 such prompts tested against 7 additional 2025 LLMs, malicious code generation rates ranged from 12.7% to 43.8%, and state-of-the-art guardrails detected under 0.3% of cases.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.02372v2

6. Commercial LLMs identify phishing-brand impersonation at 93–95% accuracy (PREPRINT — not peer-reviewed)

  • Title: "How Can We Effectively Use LLMs for Phishing Detection? Evaluating the Effectiveness of Large Language Model-based Phishing Detection Models"
  • Authors: Fujiao Ji, Doowon Kim (University of Tennessee, Knoxville)
  • Venue/date: arXiv:2511.09606 (Nov 25, 2025) — preprint, no peer-review venue claimed
  • Key finding: Evaluated 7 LLMs plus 2 deep-learning baselines on 19,131 real-world phishing sites and 243 benign sites. Commercial LLMs with screenshot input at zero temperature reached 93–95% brand-identification accuracy (Qwen up to 92% among open-source); stacking input modalities or one-shot prompting did not consistently help and could degrade results.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.09606v2

TODAY'S SAFETY TIP

Never pay a stranger upfront on Facebook Marketplace — especially via Zelle or Cash App. Five of today's seven victim accounts hinge on one mistake: sending non-reversible money before receiving the goods. Zelle and Cash App payments are essentially instant cash — no buyer protection, no chargeback. The rule: inspect the item in person, pay cash on delivery (or a payment method with buyer protection), and walk away from any seller who demands full payment upfront or refuses cash on delivery. That single rule would have stopped today's firewood scams, the patio-set scam, and the hay-triangle scam — and it pairs with the day's FTC warning: preview any QR-code link before scanning it.
