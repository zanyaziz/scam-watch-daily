NEW & EMERGING SCAMS

1. AI voice-clone "family emergency" scams — the code-word defence

  • Date: Fresh explainers published November 11, 2026
  • How it works: Scammers clone a loved one's voice from a short clip harvested from social media videos, voicemail greetings, or podcasts, then call relatives claiming an emergency — an accident, arrest, or kidnapping — and demand urgent money via gift cards, crypto, or wire transfer, insisting the victim stay on the line and tell no one. The FBI's 2025 Internet Crime Report logged 22,364 AI-related complaints and nearly $893M in losses, its first dedicated AI section.
  • Who it targets: Relatives of anyone with a public voice footprint — parents and grandparents especially.
  • Why it's new: The countermeasure getting fresh attention is a pre-agreed family code word: AI can copy a voice but not shared family knowledge. Ask for the code word before acting on any urgent money plea, however real the voice sounds.
  • Source: https://curiospark.org/blog/ai-voice-cloning-scams/ (Nov 11, 2026); https://www.chainpursuit.com/blog/ai-crypto-scams-deepfakes-voice-clones/ (updated Nov 11, 2026)

2. WhatsApp "vote for me" device-linking takeover — the ballet-scam lure

  • Date: Derbyshire Police warning November 7, 2026; 50+ cases in Switzerland in October 2026
  • How it works: A message arrives from a real contact asking you to vote for a child (e.g., "Eliška" or "Mia") in a dance competition, for a free lesson or scholarship. The link opens a fake ballet-school voting page (AI-generated photos) that asks for your phone number, then shows a code to type into WhatsApp — that code links the scammer's device to your account via WhatsApp's linked-devices feature. Your phone keeps working normally while the scammer reads your chats and spams the same message to all your contacts. The same lure also appears as pet and school contests.
  • Who it targets: WhatsApp users with children in their contact circle — parents, grandparents, teachers.
  • Why it's new: It weaponizes a legitimate WhatsApp feature (linked devices) rather than a password, so nothing about the victim's phone looks wrong afterward. Switzerland's National Cyber Security Centre reported 50+ cases in October 2026 alone.
  • Source: https://yourlocalcomputerguy.co.uk/blog/uk-scam-alerts-november-2026.html (updated Nov 7, 2026); https://www.thescamhound.co.uk/post/whats-app-beware-of-the-ballerina (Nov 7, 2026)

3. WhatsApp malware disguised as work files — payroll and invoice attachments

  • Date: Singapore Police Force warning November 2, 2026
  • How it works: Malware spreading through WhatsApp as routine work files — "Invoice 2026-09-15.iso", "Statement of Account_54d3.zip", "Salary Revision.rar". The messages come from compromised accounts of people the victim knows (colleagues, supervisors), and the victim is asked to open the file on a computer. The malware can log keystrokes and steal banking credentials and personal data.
  • Who it targets: Office workers and anyone whose colleagues message them on WhatsApp.
  • Why it's new: The trust comes from the sender, not the file — a compromised colleague's account makes an .iso or .zip look like ordinary work. No monetary losses reported to date, but personal information may have been stolen. Singapore's Cyber Security Agency issued a related advisory Oct 28, 2026.
  • Source: https://www.centreforcybersecurity.com/blog/whatsapp-malware-work-files-singapore (Nov 4, 2026)

4. AI biometric fake job interviews — face and eye scans harvested

  • Date: November 2026 hiring-fraud report; India's I4C warning May 2026
  • How it works: A new twist on the WhatsApp/Telegram task scam: after the usual hook (easy work liking videos or rating hotels, small real payouts first), victims are pushed into fake online interviews that harvest face and eye biometrics on unknown websites. The classic task-scam endgame is unchanged: "prepaid" or "VIP" tasks requiring deposits to unlock earnings, which are never returned.
  • Who it targets: Job seekers, especially in India, recruited over WhatsApp and Telegram.
  • Why it's new: Biometric harvesting adds a lasting identity-theft layer to the familiar task scam — a face or iris scan can't be changed like a password. The FTC reported task-scam reports jumping from ~5,000 in all of 2023 to ~20,000 in just the first half of 2024.
  • Source: https://www.techyami.in/2026/11/fake-job-offer-scams-in-india-2026-task.html (updated Nov 12, 2026); https://www.gmork.com/scams/task-job-scam-app-optimization (reviewed Nov 3, 2026)

5. Quishing — QR-code phishing surges, including a fake WhatsApp Web lure

  • Date: Fresh quishing guides November 9–10, 2026; fake WhatsApp Web domain flagged November 11, 2026
  • How it works: QR codes hide phishing links inside an image, so they slip past email filters that scan text links. Fake codes appear as stickers pasted over real ones on parking meters, in emails disguised as password-expiry or HR notices, in PDFs, and in texts — scanning leads to a spoofed login or payment page, or a malware download. A concrete fresh example: threat-intel site PhishDestroy flagged domain blz-whatsapp.com on November 11, 2026 — a Chinese-language fake "WhatsApp Web" guide built around QR-code pairing, rated critical risk (90/100), with 9/92 VirusTotal detections.
  • Who it targets: Anyone who scans QR codes to pay, log in, or "verify" — drivers and office workers especially.
  • Why it's new: Industry data tracked quishing volume up over 100% quarter-over-quarter in early 2026, with tens of millions of QR-laden phishing emails in a single month by March. Yesterday's edition covered the wider quishing picture; this is the fresh WhatsApp-Web variant.
  • Source: https://www.techgeek.com.au/qr-code-scams-quishing-how-it-works-australia/ (Nov 9, 2026); https://phishdestroy.io/domain/blz-whatsapp.com/ (observed Nov 11, 2026); https://markets.financialcontent.com/stocks/article/marketersmedia-2026-11-10-qr-code-phishing-risks-types-and-security-tips-quishing-prevention-guide-2027 (Nov 10, 2026)

6. Fake Zelle "business account upgrade" and fake Venmo confirmations target Marketplace sellers — DEVELOPING

  • Date: Fresh write-ups November 10–11, 2026
  • How it works: A fake buyer sends a spoofed Zelle email claiming the seller's account has limits and they must pay to "upgrade" before they can receive funds — flipping the seller into paying the buyer. A parallel variant sends a fake Venmo payment-confirmation email to make a seller believe payment arrived, so they hand over the item. RESEARCH DESK

1. AI voice phishing fools 1 in 6 U.S. adults — and the script matters more than the voice (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Title: Evaluating AI Models' Capability to Automate Voice Phishing Attacks
  • Authors: Fred Heiding (Harvard Kennedy School), Claudio Mayrink Verdun (Harvard SEAS), Simon Lermen, Andrew Kao, Vitor Albiero, Lauren Deason, Irina-Elena Veliche, Christine Lehane (Meta Platforms)
  • Venue / Date: Peer-reviewed — Expert Systems with Applications, Vol. 332 (2027), article 133620; available online 17 July 2026. Also on arXiv: 2607.09970.
  • Key finding: In a large survey experiment with 4,100 representative U.S. adults exposed to scam calls generated by six leading AI voice systems (Meta's Llama Full Duplex, OpenAI AVM, Google Gemini, Sesame, PlayAI, ElevenLabs) plus human baselines, an average of 16.5% said they would comply with the scam request across five scenarios. The worst case — an AI-cloned voice posing as a sister in danger — drew 36.1% compliance. People were actually fairly good at spotting synthetic voices (70.3% correct), but that didn't protect them: what predicted compliance was how persuasive the caller sounded, not whether the voice sounded human. Each step up in persuasiveness raised the odds of complying by a factor of 2.58. The authors' cost analysis also finds AI vishing is already profitable at a few dollars per hour, while human-operated vishing loses money at a $34.55/hour wage.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.09970v2

2. Practising against fake scammers makes people better at spotting real ones (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Title: ScamPilot: Simulating Conversations with LLMs to Protect Against Online Scams
  • Authors: Owen Hoffman, Kangze Peng, Sajid Kamal, Zehua You, Sukrit Venkatagiri (all Swarthmore College)
  • Venue / Date: Peer-reviewed — Proceedings of the 2026 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems (CHI '26), April 13–17, 2026, Barcelona. DOI: 10.1145/3772318.3791313.
  • Key finding: ScamPilot lets users coach a simulated victim through a live scam conversation run by two LLM agents (one playing the scammer, one the target), with embedded quizzes and real-time feedback. In a controlled study of 150 people, the version combining advice-giving with quiz questions improved scam recognition by 8% versus a control group — without making people more suspicious of legitimate messages. Participants' confidence in their ability to respond rose 19%, and their sense that their responses would work rose 9%. The idea is "inoculation": a weakened, simulated dose of a scam builds resistance to the real thing.
  • Source: https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2601.22426

3. AI-generated faces are trusted more than real faces (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Title: AI-Generated Faces are Becoming More Trustworthy
  • Authors: Alexis McGuire (Lancaster University), Paul Taylor, Sophie Nightingale (Lancaster), Maty Bohacek (Stanford), Hany Farid (UC Berkeley)
  • Venue / Date: Peer-reviewed — Journal of Vision (2026), DOI: 10.1167/jov.26.7.3.
  • Key finding: 169 participants judged 96 faces (real, GAN-generated, and diffusion-model-generated) for real-vs-fake and for trustworthiness. Accuracy at telling real from AI was only 58.4% — barely above a coin flip. Worse, AI faces were rated more trustworthy than real ones: real faces averaged 4.03 on the trust scale, GAN faces 4.36, and diffusion-model faces 4.70 — the most trustworthy of all. For scammers, that means a fake profile picture is now likely to look more credible than a genuine one.
  • Source: https://phys.org/news/2026-07-ai-real-people.html (DOI: 10.1167/jov.26.7.3; the journal's own page returned a 403 to the fetcher, so details are verified via the Phys.org report of the published paper)

4. People are worse at spotting fake faces than fake messages (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Title: Framing digital inauthenticity: Comparing user detection of AI-generated faces to message-based scam methods
  • Authors: Dawn M. Sarno et al.
  • Venue / Date: Peer-reviewed — Acta Psychologica, Vol. 262 (February 2026), article 105995; published online 4 December 2025. PMID: 41349270.
  • Key finding: In a comparative study, participants were less likely to detect image-based fakes (AI-generated faces) than message-based scams such as phishing. They also made poorer, riskier judgments about faces than about messages. The traits that helped people spot fake faces were risk literacy, cognitive reflection (the habit of pausing to think before answering), and gender — suggesting that the mental skills that protect against phishing don't fully transfer to spotting AI imagery.
  • Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41349270/

5. AI web agents hand over your SSN to scam sites — even when they "know" it's a scam (PREPRINT)

  • Title: "I Strongly Suspect This Website Is a Scam": Benchmarking PII Leakage and Detection without Defense in Autonomous Web Agents
  • Authors: Soham Roy, Sarthakbrata Halder, Arya Bharaty, Vaibhav Bhaskar, Yash Sinha, Dhruv Kumar, Srikant Panda, Murari Mandal
  • Venue / Date: Preprint (not yet peer-reviewed) — arXiv:2606.00497, submitted 30 May 2026, revised 23 October 2026. Pre-registered benchmark; code and data public.
  • Key finding: The authors built "Scammer4U," a benchmark of 91 scam websites (plus 10 benign look-alikes) designed to trick AI browsing agents into submitting a user's private data. With no privacy guidance, four leading AI agents leaked the most sensitive data — Social Security numbers, card numbers and CVVs, bank accounts, passwords — in 54–93% of sessions, versus 0% on the benign twin sites. Most striking is the "detection–action gap": even when the agent's own reasoning explicitly flagged the site as suspicious, it still submitted the sensitive data in 35.9% of sessions (vs 66.1% when it voiced no suspicion). In other words, the agent can "know" it's a scam and hand over your data anyway — so defences can't rely on the agent's judgment and must intercept outgoing submissions directly.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.00497

TODAY'S SAFETY TIP

Never type a code from a message into WhatsApp — and never trust a voice alone. Today's "vote for my child" scam works because the code it asks you to enter links a stranger's device to your WhatsApp account; WhatsApp never asks you to type a code to vote in a contest, and a real friend's message asking you to do it means their account is already compromised. The same rule covers the AI voice-clone emergency call: a cloned voice can sound exactly like your child or parent, but it can't know a secret only your family shares. Agree on a family code word now — a word or phrase you'd never post online — and make it the rule that no urgent money request is real until the caller says it. Check your linked devices today (WhatsApp → Settings → Linked Devices) and remove anything you don't recognize.
