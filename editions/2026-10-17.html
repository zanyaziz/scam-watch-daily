NEW & EMERGING SCAMS

1. AI-cloned candidate voices — midterm election donation / voter-registration scams

  • What's new: Scammers borrow candidate names, logos, slogans, photos and AI-generated voices/images to solicit fake campaign contributions, sell merchandise that never ships, run bogus PACs and fake campaign "investment pools" promising a return if a candidate wins (the FBI notes donations are not investments). A text/email claiming you are not registered to vote links to a fake state registration page to steal personal data.
  • Who's targeted: U.S. voters and donors nationwide, three weeks before the 2026 midterms.
  • Date/source: FBI IC3 PSA issued Oct 15, 2026; reported Oct 16 — Help Net Security, verified
  • Tip: Donate and check registration only via sites you type yourself (vote.gov / FEC.gov).
  • Source: https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2026/10/16/usa-2026-midterm-election-scams/

2. AI-enhanced phishing/smishing against election officials

  • What's new: AI-polished phishing/smishing, including via encrypted chat apps, mimics legitimate communications — AI removes grammar tells, uses local vernacular, and deepfake audio/video creates false identities — to steal credentials or install malware. FBI/CISA urge phishing-resistant MFA.
  • Who's targeted: Election officials, candidates, campaign and political organizations.
  • Date/source: FBI/CISA PSA I-101626-PSA, Oct 16, 2026 — IC3 official, verified; also Reuters Oct 16
  • Source: http://www.IC3.gov/PSA/2026/PSA261016

3. AI voice-cloning family-emergency calls — SBI advisory, India (developing, single secondary source)

  • What's new: Synthetic replicas of relatives/acquaintances/officials are used with fabricated emergencies (accident/hospital, detention, travel distress) to force immediate transfers; other variants impersonate regulators/law enforcement. SBI: a familiar voice is no longer proof of identity — call back on a saved number, use a family verification phrase, never share OTP/PIN/CVV.
  • Who's targeted: Bank account holders in India.
  • Date/source: SBI advisory Oct 16, 2026; article Oct 17 — The420.in, verified
  • Source: https://the420.in/sbi-warns-ai-voice-cloning-cyber-fraud-guidelines/

4. Calendar-invite (ICS) phishing surge

  • What's new: A fraudulent .ics invite lands directly on your calendar — sometimes auto-added or accepted by an AI assistant — bypassing spam filters. Its link leads to fake-login credential theft, a malware download, or a live meeting with an imposter posing as a trusted client/partner. Sublime Security: ICS incidents up ~33,000% May–September.
  • Who's targeted: Professionals who meet strangers, especially salespeople and recruiters.
  • Date/source: Oct 13, 2026 — Morning Brew, citing Sublime/Consumer Affairs, verified
  • Tip: Turn off auto-add; delete unrecognised meetings without clicking their links.
  • Source: https://www.morningbrew.com/stories/the-latest-phishing-scam-is-targeting-your-calendar

5. Microsoft Teams fake IT help-desk — Quick Assist takeover + device-code phishing

  • What's new: An external Teams contact poses as internal IT (sometimes after inbox flooding) and talks an employee into Quick Assist, a remote-access install, or PowerShell — giving device control. A related device-code variant gives account access without password theft. Ontinue: ~30% of monitored orgs saw ≥1 Teams IT-impersonation attempt in H1 2026; about 1 in 990 external contacts showed impersonation signs; 10 device-code compromises in May–June.
  • Who's targeted: Employees and enterprises, North America/global.
  • Date/source: Oct 15, 2026 — Redmondmag, reporting Ontinue, verified
  • Tip: IT will never ask you to install remote access from a chat. Verify via your internal directory.
  • Source: https://redmondmag.com/articles/2026/10/15/microsoft-teams-impersonation-attacks-target-30-percent-of-orgs.aspx

6. Barcode/QR-code bail-and-fine payment scam

RESEARCH DESK (2025–2026 research; preprints labelled)

1. Phishing training barely works in the largest trial of its kind (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Authors: Grant Ho, Ariana Mirian, Elisa Luo, Khang Tong, Euyhyun Lee, Lin Liu, Christopher A. Longhurst, Christian Dameff, Stefan Savage, Geoffrey M. Voelker
  • Venue/date: IEEE Symposium on Security and Privacy (SP) 2025 — peer-reviewed; DOI 10.1109/sp61157.2025.00076
  • Key finding: In a randomized trial of 19,500 UC San Diego Health employees with 10 phishing lures over 8 months, embedded training reduced later failure by only ~2%, with no significant link between recency of annual training and failure. Lure quality dominated: 1.82% clicked an "Outlook Password" lure vs 30.8% for a "Vacation Policy" lure, and by month 8 over 50% had failed at least once. The authors recommend technical controls over more training.
  • Source: https://www.computer.org/csdl/proceedings-article/sp/2025/223600a076/21B7RjYyG9q

2. AI fraud-detection research is fragmented and doesn't generalise (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Authors: Antonis Papasavva, Shane Johnson, Ed Lowther, Samantha Lundrigan, Enrico Mariconti, Anna Markovska, Nilufer Tuptuk
  • Venue/date: Crime Science 14(1), Article 7, 2025 — peer-reviewed; DOI 10.1186/s40163-025-00248-8; arXiv:2409.19022
  • Key finding: A systematic review screening 2,457 records (350 eligible, 223 analysed) found research fragmented across 16 fraud types; per-scam models do not generalise and degrade as tactics evolve. It also flags poor bias reporting and selective metrics that inflate reported accuracy.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.19022

3. Inside the pig-butchering lifecycle — first victim-interview study (PREPRINT)

  • Authors: Rajvardhan Oak, Zubair Shafiq
  • Venue/date: arXiv:2503.20821, submitted Mar 25, 2025, revised May 24, 2025 — not peer-reviewed
  • Key finding: Based on interviews with 26 victims: the lifecycle is staged trust-building, a fake platform, fabricated returns, then repeated high-pressure re-engagement that amplifies losses — and victims face heightened risk of secondary (recovery) scams. The authors propose platform intervention points and non-stigmatising terminology.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.20821

4. How to use LLMs for phishing detection — screenshots beat extra data (PREPRINT)

  • Authors: Fujiao Ji, Doowon Kim
  • Venue/date: arXiv:2511.09606, submitted Nov 12, 2025, revised Nov 25, 2025 — not peer-reviewed
  • Key finding: Testing 7 LLMs and 2 deep-learning baselines on 19,131 phishing and 243 benign sites: commercial LLMs beat open-source models for detection, while deep learning was better on benign sites. For brand identification, a screenshot alone was best — 93–95% for commercial LLMs, up to 92% for Qwen — and adding modalities, one-shot prompts or higher temperature degraded results.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.09606

5. Continuous training halved compromise rates in 6 months (PREPRINT)

  • Authors: Rebeka Toth, Richard A. Dubniczky, Olga Limonova, Norbert Tihanyi
  • Venue/date: arXiv:2510.27298, submitted Oct 31, 2025 — not peer-reviewed
  • Key finding: In apparent contrast to item 1: a 12-month study across 20 organisations, >1,300 employees and >13,000 simulated phishes found sustained simulations plus mandatory training halved compromise rates within 6 months. Helpfulness/cooperation appeals and internal-seeming senders were most effective; staff turnover dragged awareness down.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.27298

6. LLMs can rewrite vishing scripts to evade detectors in under 9 seconds (PREPRINT)

  • Authors: Wenhao Li, Selvakumar Manickam, Yung-wey Chong, Shankar Karuppayah
  • Venue/date: arXiv:2507.16291, submitted Jul 22, 2025 — not peer-reviewed
  • Key finding: LLMs can rewrite real voice-phishing scripts to evade ML detectors: GPT-4o transcripts cut classifier accuracy by up to 30.96% while preserving meaning (high BERTScore), generated in under 9 seconds at negligible cost, tested on the real Korean vishing dataset KorCCViD.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.16291

TODAY'S SAFETY TIP

Check the developer name letter-for-letter before you install any app — and treat Early Access as a warning, not a feature. Saturday's fake WhatsApp clone reached 500,000 downloads on Google Play partly because its Early Access status disabled public reviews, hiding warnings. The real WhatsApp is published by "WhatsApp LLC" with 10B+ downloads; a clone had an unknown developer and a fraction of that. Rules: (1) tap the developer name and confirm it exactly, and be suspicious of any big-brand app in "Early Access" with reviews disabled; (2) arrive at the install page only from a link on the company's own website, typed by you — never from an ad, text or search result; (3) if you already installed a look-alike, delete it, then change your banking/messaging passwords from a different, clean device and turn on passkey/2FA.
