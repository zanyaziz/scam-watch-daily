FBI blames North Korea for the record $6.3 billion Binance theft.

Lead

FBI blames North Korea for the record $6.3 billion Binance theft. In a late-breaking October 31 announcement, the FBI attributed the theft of more than $6 billion in crypto from Binance to North Korea's TraderTraitor group — the largest crypto heist on record — and published 628 wallet addresses for exchanges to block. Note on the day itself: November 1 was a quiet day for fresh platform announcements — no new enforcement action, takedown, or policy change from Meta, Google/YouTube, Apple, Microsoft, TikTok, X, Snapchat, Telegram, or Discord was found in major outlets for November 1 itself — so this edition leads with the October 31 FBI attribution plus November 1 lawsuit coverage and a major regulation taking effect that day.

TOP STORIES (ranked by importance)

1. FBI blames North Korea for the record $6.3 billion Binance theft (late-breaking Oct 31)

  • Outlet: Associated Press (via Netscape News); corroborated by Bloomberg via TradingView
  • Date: October 31, 2026 — late-breaking, not November 1
  • Summary: The FBI said that North Korea's TraderTraitor group was responsible for the theft of more than $6 billion in crypto from Binance on or about October 29, 2026, the largest crypto heist on record. Working with the Secret Service and IRS Criminal Investigation, the FBI said the attackers were converting stolen assets mainly into ether and expected further laundering through DeFi and instant-swap services, and it published 628 wallet addresses (608 Ethereum, 19 Bitcoin, 1 Solana) for exchanges and services to block. Binance said it had set aside enough to cover about 90% of the roughly $6.3 billion and promised customers would be fully reimbursed.
  • Why it matters: This is the first official U.S. law-enforcement attribution of the theft, turning private analytics firms' suspicions into an actionable blocklist — and it shows state-backed actors now operating at a scale dwarfing the $1.5 billion Bybit hack attributed to the same group in 2025.
  • Sources: https://webcenters.netscape.compuserve.com/news/story/0001/20261031/883f18140c1e21ce0c38781e72cbe315 ; https://www.tradingview.com/news/bloomingbit:1a9cec122094b:0-fbi-says-north-korea-was-behind-binance-s-6-3-billion-hack-sees-signs-of-money-laundering/

2. Rhode Island alleges Meta profited billions from scam ads on Facebook, Instagram and WhatsApp (Nov 1)

  • Outlet: Forbes (via Scoop aggregator listing), November 1, 2026; lawsuit detail from Biometric Update
  • Date: November 1, 2026 (Forbes report); the underlying Rhode Island lawsuit was already covered October 28, 2026, so this is November 1 coverage of the suit, not a confirmed November 1 filing date
  • Summary: A lawsuit claims Meta earned billions from scam advertising across Facebook, Instagram and WhatsApp while using AI tools to help target victims. Rhode Island Attorney General Peter Neronha filed under the state's Deceptive Trade Practices Act, alleging Meta's "sophisticated, algorithmically driven system" targeted users by behavior, interests and vulnerabilities, and that internal projections put about 10% of its 2024 revenue — about $16 billion — from ads for restricted goods or ads seeking to defraud consumers. Meta responded that the AG refused to engage on its scam protections and that it fights scams because they are bad for its business and users.
  • Why it matters: It reframes platform scam ads from a moderation failure into an alleged profit center, citing Meta's own internal figures — if the "10% of revenue" claim survives in court, it raises the stakes for advertiser-verification rules everywhere, including Thailand's rule taking effect the same day (item 3).
  • Sources: https://scoopfeeds.com/article/69fc0b3a-07f6-587a-9c08-44bf20f953ec ; https://www.biometricupdate.com/202610/meta-faces-growing-scrutiny-over-scam-ads-in-us-europe-and-asia

3. Thailand's "No ID, No Ads" rule takes effect — social platforms must verify advertisers before ads run (Nov 1)

  • Outlet: Baker McKenzie (law-firm analysis of the regulation); corroborated by Biometric Update and Power Ads
  • Date: November 1, 2026 (effective date; regulation issued May 5, 2026)
  • Summary: From November 1, social media providers receiving payment for ads shown in Thailand must verify the advertiser's identity before the ad goes live, unless the advertiser was verified within the previous year — via government ID matched by facial comparison or an approved digital-ID system. Platforms must retain advertiser names, ID/company documents and contact details (plus third-party payer details) for at least 90 days after the advertising relationship ends. Meta told Thailand's Digital Economy and Society Ministry on October 23 that it is ready to comply; TikTok and Google gave a House committee no such assurance at an October 22 hearing, according to IDTechWire.
  • Why it matters: This is the first major market to make advertiser identity verification a precondition for running social ads, explicitly aimed at scam ads — and non-compliance risks loss of safe-harbor protection and joint liability for tech-crime damages, a model other regulators are watching.
  • Sources: https://www.bakermckenzie.com/en/insight/publications/2026/08/thailand-upcoming-social-media-advertisers-verification-requirements ; https://powerads.group/news/thailand-no-id-no-ads-advertiser-verification-november-1/

4. BTCPay Server tells every operator to shut down after bitcoin hot wallets are swept (Nov 1)

  • Outlet: CoinDesk; corroborated by Bitcoin.com News and Decrypt
  • Date: November 1, 2026 (X alert ~5:25 PM ET November 1; CoinDesk write-up published November 2)
  • Summary: BTCPay Server, the open-source tool merchants use to accept bitcoin without a payment intermediary, urged all operators to shut down their servers after reports that attackers were sweeping internet-connected hot wallets. Founder Nicolas Dorier later said the reports appeared to involve releases older than version 2.4.2, with attackers logging in with administrator access, and that the project's own servers running 2.4.7 were not attacked — but no loss total, affected-server count, or confirmed attack vector had been disclosed. Because BTCPay is self-hosted, there is no central operator that can switch instances off, so each merchant must act itself.
  • Why it matters: A blanket "shut everything down" order from a widely used payment tool, days after its October 29 emergency 2.4.7 release, is a warning to any small business running unpatched self-hosted software — and the still-unknown entry point means even updated operators cannot yet be sure they are safe.
  • Sources: https://www.coindesk.com/tech/2026/11/02/btcpay-a-bitcoin-payment-tool-tells-users-to-switch-off-their-servers-after-reports-of-drained-wallets ; https://news.bitcoin.com/security/bitcoin-payment-processor-btcpay-hit-by-hot-wallet-theft-reports/

5. Binance-hack launderers move onto Solana after draining ~$500 million through Tron; Binance reopens withdrawals (Nov 1)

  • Outlet: The Defiant (Chainalysis findings); Binance reopening figures via CryptoMediaZone's November 1 live account
  • Date: November 1, 2026 (Defiant published November 1, 17:08 UTC)
  • Summary: Chainalysis said on November 1 that the Binance-hack launderers had drained about $500 million from Tron staging wallets — spanning more than 1,000 addresses and over 3,000 deposits — and had begun moving funds onto Solana via Chainflip, Relay, THORChain, NEAR and other routes. One independent investigator cautioned the $500 million counts repeated internal transfers, with only about $242 million actually leaving the Tron holding wallets. The same day, Binance reopened withdrawals on all 15 networks in its first phase and reported $4.649 billion in withdrawals against $1.01 billion in deposits between 06:00–15:30 UTC November 1, a net outflow of about $3.64 billion.
  • Why it matters: The laundering is automated and moving faster than freezes can follow, jumping chains within days of the theft — a live test of whether the FBI's October 31 blocklist (item 1) can actually choke off the proceeds, and whether Binance's customer exodus stabilizes.
  • Sources: https://thedefiant.io/news/hacks/binance-hack-launderers-reach-solana-after-draining-500-million-on-tron-chainalysis-says ; https://cryptomediazone.com/hack-database/binance-hack-day-five/

6. Fake Amazon account alerts stole £86,837 in nine days, UK fraud service warns (Nov 1 roundup)

  • Outlet: UK Scam Alerts roundup (November 1, 2026) recapping a Report Fraud warning dated October 30, 2026; figures corroborated by Essex Trading Standards / Neighbourhood Watch
  • Date: November 1, 2026 (roundup post); underlying Report Fraud alert October 30, 2026, covering October 5–14, 2026 — label the figures as October 5–14, not November 1
  • Summary: Fraudsters cold-contact victims claiming suspicious activity on their Amazon account, saying unauthorised items were added to the basket for delivery to an unfamiliar address and someone is trying to pay with the victim's bank details. Victims were then told to share their screen, download a money-transfer app, install remote-access software, or hand over passport/driving-licence details. Report Fraud received 110 reports naming Amazon impersonation in the nine days October 5–14, with £86,837 stolen — over £9,600 a day — and 60% of reports from people aged 50–79.
  • Why it matters: Amazon impersonation remains one of the most effective impersonation scripts because it exploits a routine, trusted notification (an order problem) and then escalates to full device control; Amazon never asks customers to share their screen, install software, or move money to "protect" an account.
  • Sources: https://yourlocalcomputerguy.co.uk/blog/uk-scam-alerts-november-2026.html ; https://www.ourwatchmember.org.uk/Alerts/A/518305

NEW & EMERGING SCAMS

1. Fake "SKR Claim" app planted in an official app store — seed-phrase theft (Nov 1)

  • Date: November 1, 2026, verified — live for about one hour before removal; disclosed by Solana Mobile on X on November 1
  • How it works: A malicious app called "SKR Claim" surfaced in the Solana dApp Store's App Spotlight after unauthorized access to its dApp Publishing Portal. It asked users to enter their wallet seed phrases, supposedly to claim the SKR token. Solana Mobile patched a bug that allowed unpublished apps to be linked into curated sections and began removing the app from devices where it had been installed.
  • Why it matters: The scam did not just imitate a store listing — it appeared in the curated Spotlight section of Solana Mobile's own dApp Store, the place users are trained to trust. A seed phrase rebuilds a wallet on any device, so anyone who typed it in should treat that wallet as compromised and move funds to a new wallet. No install, victim, or loss figures had been disclosed as of this edition.
  • Source: https://www.ccn.com/news/crypto/solana-dapp-store-breach-fake-skr-claim-app/

2. Hijacked third-party website script drains logged-in trading accounts — no password needed (Oct 29, fresh reporting Nov 1–2)

  • Date: October 29, 2026, verified — theft between about 17:10–18:02 UTC; refunds reported October 30; detailed reporting November 1–2
  • How it works: A malicious script replaced DebugBear's real-user-monitoring script loaded by Polymarket's international site, after DebugBear's DNS records were compromised through its domain registrar. The code reused signed-in traders' sessions, API keys, and Polymarket's own relayer to sell positions and transfer funds — about $2.64–2.7 million to the attackers' wallet. Polymarket paused trading, removed the script within the hour, and said it would refund 726 affected wallets plus $1.19 million to about 5,500 wallets affected by the pause, about $3.9 million in total.
  • Why it matters: Attackers did not phish passwords or break smart contracts — they compromised a page-speed vendor's delivery path and turned victims' own logged-in browser sessions into a theft tool. A supply-chain account-takeover variant: a trusted third-party script on a site you trust can act as you.
  • Source: https://thehackernews.com/2026/11/hackers-steal-about-27-million-from.html

3. Phishing emails sent through Google's own infrastructure (Oct 29–30)

  • Date: October 29, 2026, verified — flagged on X by Casa's Jameson Lopp on October 29; reported October 30
  • How it works: A new flavor of Google phishing abuses Google's own infrastructure to send the email, with a link to a fake login page designed to steal passwords and session/2FA data. It follows a May 2026 variant abusing Google's account-recovery form, and a December 2025 Check Point case in which 9,394 emails to about 3,200 organizations came from a real Google address via Google Cloud's Application Integration tool. Google told researchers it had blocked those campaigns and attributed them to misuse of an automation tool, not a break-in.
  • Why it matters: The emails pass normal trust checks because they genuinely come via Google systems, so "check the sender domain" fails. Crypto holders and wallet/exchange users are singled out as highest risk, but any account holder can be targeted — the theft happens on the fake login page after the click.
  • Source: https://www.cryptopolitan.com/jameson-lopp-flags-google-phishing/

4. "Contagious Interview" crypto job scam — new private-repo / package-install variant — DEVELOPING (Oct 21)

  • Date: Disclosed October 21, 2026, verified as disclosure date — described on the October 21 episode of The OpenSourceMalware Show; reported October 23. Labelled developing: no victim count, affected companies, or confirmed crypto losses tied to this specific variant were disclosed.
  • How it works: North Korea-linked actors approach crypto job seekers on LinkedIn, invite them into a private repository, and ask them to download the project and install its packages as a technical assessment. Researchers describe a malicious .npmrc configuration and hidden software in the task that can steal passwords, crypto-wallet data, and access tokens if run. This builds on Atlassian's September 21, 2026 report on convincing recruiter profiles, company sites, and coding projects in the same campaign.
  • Why it matters: The familiar fake-recruiter job lure now routes victims into a private code repository and a rigged coding task, where installing the project's packages can plant malware — shifting the trap from a fake interview call to a developer's own toolchain.
  • Source: https://crypto2community.com/crypto-news/north-korean-hackers-target-crypto-job-seekers-with-new-malware-attack

5. Quishing — fake QR codes on parking meters and in scam barcodes (October 2026 wave)

  • Date: October 2026 wave, verified in part — Ann Arbor, Michigan fake meter stickers reported in October 2026; FTC parking-meter warning September 2026; SSA Office of Inspector General barcode/QR alert dated September 28, 2026
  • How it works: In Ann Arbor, police found fake stickers on downtown meters near the University of Michigan reading "Scan to Park!" and "Park AA," sending drivers to fraudulent payment sites built to steal personal and financial information; city staff were sent to inspect and remove them. Separately, the SSA OIG warned that impersonators posing as police, sheriffs, or federal agents text or email barcodes/QR codes and instruct victims to load money onto them at a big-name retailer, then read the code back — with some victims losing thousands of dollars without ever sharing a Social Security number.
  • Why it matters: Quishing turns a routine real-world action — paying for parking — into credential theft, and it evades the usual "check the link" advice because the link is inside an image. A peelable sticker over a printed/laminated code is the key physical red flag; legitimate agencies never demand immediate payment via a barcode or QR code.
  • Sources: https://itsecurity.blog.fordham.edu/2026/10/23/qr-code-scams-why-you-should-think-before-you-scan/ ; https://oig.ssa.gov/~assets/uploads/2026-september-barcodes.pdf

6. Biometric-harvesting video calls — stealing your face to beat video KYC — DEVELOPING (advisory date unverified)

  • Date: Advisory date unverified — India's I4C National Cybercrime Threat Analytics Unit (NCTAU) advisory is described as fresh in recent coverage and was summarized in a widely shared post on November 2, 2026; do not date the advisory itself to a specific day on this evidence.
  • How it works: Fraudsters pose as bank/telecom staff warning that eKYC has expired or an account will be blocked, then steer victims onto a WhatsApp/Telegram video call and direct them through facial exercises — blink, turn left/right, smile, read aloud. Fake remote-job interviews are a second harvest route, with long video interviews and unusual camera exercises. The captured footage is fed into AI tools to create a replica with blinking, head movement, and voice, which can then be used for fake bank accounts, SIM swaps, loan fraud, money laundering, and impersonation — often discovered months later.
  • Why it matters: Instead of stealing a password, scammers harvest high-quality facial movements to build a deepfake that can later pass video-KYC, liveness checks, and account recovery. A face, unlike a password, cannot be reset. The defence cited by I4C coverage is to never do KYC from an unsolicited call, hang up and contact the institution directly, and lock Aadhaar biometrics via the mAadhaar app / UIDAI portal when not needed.
  • Sources: https://www.varindia.com/public/news/how-ai-fraud-is-breaking-india-s-kyc-infrastructure ; https://www.linkedin.com/posts/satish-kulkarni-4a260039_cybersecurity-fraudalert-deepfake-activity-7522869714490957824-nEEG

RESEARCH DESK

1. Language as Lure: A Naturalistic Study on Pasifika Phishing Susceptibility (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Title: Language as Lure: A Naturalistic Study on Pasifika Phishing Susceptibility
  • Authors: Eric Spero, Isa Seow, Lucas Betts, Eddie Fuatimau, Robert Biddle, Danielle Lottridge, Giovanni Russello (University of Auckland; Biddle also Carleton University)
  • Venue / Date: Twenty-First Symposium on Usable Privacy and Security (SOUPS 2025), August 2025, Seattle, WA, pp. 19–35
  • Key finding: Language and culture change who clicks. In a real-world simulation with 2,000 government employees over four months — each receiving one simulated phishing email per month in either English or their local language, with all participants fluent in both — emails in the local language that made a personal request for help drew more clicks than the English versions. The standout was a local-language "church agenda" email: more than 30% clicked its link, more than double the rate for the English version of the same email. The authors point to stronger emotional responses in a first language, a cultural emphasis on helping, and weaker spam filtering for low-resource languages.
  • Source: https://www.usenix.org/conference/soups2025/presentation/spero

2. ScamFerret: Detecting Scam Websites Autonomously with Large Language Models (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Title: ScamFerret: Detecting Scam Websites Autonomously with Large Language Models
  • Authors: Hiroki Nakano, Takashi Koide, Daiki Chiba (NTT Security Holdings Corporation & NTT Corporation)
  • Venue / Date: Proceedings of the 22nd Conference on Detection of Intrusions and Malware and Vulnerability Assessment (DIMVA 2025); arXiv version February 2025 (arXiv:2502.10110)
  • Key finding: An LLM agent that autonomously gathers evidence about a URL — page content, DNS records, user reviews — and explains its verdict can spot scam sites without scam-specific training. With GPT-4 it reached 0.972 mean accuracy across four scam types in English (fake online shopping, technical-support scams, cryptocurrency scams, and investment scams) and 0.993 accuracy for fake online-shopping sites across three languages (English, German, and Japanese), outperforming conventional machine-learning detectors and simpler LLM approaches in the authors' tests.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.10110

3. Jailbreaking Generative AI: Multivector Phishing Threats and Transformer-Based Defenses (PREPRINT)

  • Title: Jailbreaking Generative AI: Multivector Phishing Threats and Transformer-Based Defenses
  • Authors: Rina Mishra, Gaurav Varshney (Indian Institute of Technology Jammu)
  • Venue / Date: arXiv:2507.12185 — submitted 16 July 2025, revised 1 April 2026 — preprint, not peer-reviewed
  • Key finding: Novices using a jailbroken ChatGPT-4o-Mini could build working phishing attacks across email, web, SMS, and voice. In the authors' studies, GenAI assistance raised novices' perceived phishing competence by 240%, improved task-completion rates by 400%, and cut implementation time by 57% versus using traditional internet resources alone (lab experiment n=30; survey n=90). In a controlled simulation, AI-generated phishing content compromised 25% of security-aware participants. On defence, the authors' transformer classifier for malicious prompts reached an F1-score of 0.9864 (XLNet) on a 21,000-prompt dataset.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.12185

4. Talking Like a Phisher: LLM-Based Attacks on Voice Phishing Classifiers (PREPRINT)

  • Title: Talking Like a Phisher: LLM-Based Attacks on Voice Phishing Classifiers
  • Authors: Wenhao Li, Selvakumar Manickam, Yung-Wey Chong, Shankar Karuppayah (Universiti Sains Malaysia)
  • Venue / Date: arXiv:2507.16291 — 22 July 2025 — preprint, not peer-reviewed
  • Key finding: Rewording real scam-call scripts with commercial LLMs can slip past automated voice-phishing detectors while keeping the scam intact. Tested against classifiers trained on a real-world Korean vishing dataset (KorCCViD), transcripts rewritten by GPT-4o cut classifier accuracy by up to 30.96% while preserving meaning (measured by BERTScore). Generation took under 9 seconds per transcript on average, at negligible cost per query — meaning evasion is cheap and fast, not just theoretically possible.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.16291

5. SoK: Large Language Model-Generated Textual Phishing Campaigns — End-to-End Analysis of Generation, Characteristics, and Detection (PREPRINT)

  • Title: SoK: Large Language Model-Generated Textual Phishing Campaigns — End-to-End Analysis of Generation, Characteristics, and Detection
  • Authors: Fengchao Chen, Tingmin Wu, Van Nguyen, Carsten Rudolph (Monash University / CSIRO Data61)
  • Venue / Date: arXiv:2508.21457 — 29 August 2025 — preprint, not peer-reviewed
  • Key finding: This is a systematization of the whole field, not a single experiment: after screening 3,910 references, the authors reviewed 44 studies in depth and built a Generation–Characterization–Defense framework. Their synthesis finds LLM phishing differs from old phishing mainly through personalization, style imitation, and multilingual reach at scale. Figures they cite from industry/research sources underline the scale: about 96% of phishing attempts arrive by email, 82% of phishing emails in a 2025 industry report incorporated LLMs, and a mid-2025 evaluation found LLM-generated spear-phishing emails exceeding 30% click-through — higher than human-crafted attempts in that evaluation.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.21457

6. Exemplifying Emerging Phishing: QR-based Browser-in-The-Browser (BiTB) Attack (PREPRINT)

  • Title: Exemplifying Emerging Phishing: QR-based Browser-in-The-Browser (BiTB) Attack
  • Authors: Muhammad Wahid Akram, Keshav Sood, Muneeb Ul Hassan (Deakin University), Basant Subba (IIT Ropar)
  • Venue / Date: arXiv:2505.18944 — 25 May 2025 — preprint, not peer-reviewed
  • Key finding: A proof-of-concept combining two tricks — a QR code that hides the malicious link, and a fake browser window inside a real-looking page — makes a credential-stealing attack simple to build with public tools. In the authors' case study, a victim scanning a QR code for a fake "YouTube Ads Free Access" offer was shown a fake Google login popup generated with Google Gemini; entered credentials were saved to the attacker's database, and the victim was then redirected to the real YouTube site to mask the theft. Note: this is a working demonstration, not a measured victim click-rate study, so treat its impact as demonstrated feasibility rather than a prevalence figure.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.18944

TODAY'S SAFETY TIP

Never type your wallet seed phrase into any app — even one in an official, curated app store. Today's fake "SKR Claim" app reached Solana Mobile's own dApp Store Spotlight and asked users for their seed phrase to "claim" a token. No legitimate claim, airdrop, support agent, or wallet app ever needs your seed phrase: it rebuilds your entire wallet on any device. If an app, site, or message asks for it, stop, delete the app, and go to the project's official site or app yourself to check — and if you already entered it, move your funds to a brand-new wallet immediately, because that wallet is compromised.
