Lead

INTERPOL warns deepfakes have gone mainstream — ordinary people, not celebrities, are now the target. Sunday was a quiet day for brand-new platform announcements, so this edition leads with INTERPOL's October 18 public campaign warning that cloned voices and fake faces built from a small amount of online content are now used against everyday people for investment fraud, impersonation, romance scams and sextortion. Also genuinely dated Oct 18: Singapore Police warned of fake Facebook investment ads impersonating popular investors, and Bloomberg's long-form on the Brooklyn crypto-scam takedown (241 websites, 112 servers seized) was published. The week's dominant story — Meta's removal of 3.7 million West Africa scam accounts, with 28 arrests and 117 trafficking victims rescued — is carried over and clearly labelled (announced Oct 13).

  • Date: Announcement October 13, 2026; CoinDesk coverage Oct 14 — labelled carryover, NOT a new Oct 18 announcement (the CoinDesk page carries conflicting Oct 14 / Oct 18 stamps; treat the operation as Oct 13–14)

2. The cryptocurrency cop who took down a global scam network from Brooklyn — 241 websites, 112 servers seized

  • Outlet: Bloomberg, via BusinessMirror
  • Date: Published October 18, 2026 — verified; underlying operation began Oct 6, Brooklyn DA announcement Oct 14, labelled
  • Summary: Brooklyn DA Eric Gonzalez's Virtual Currency Unit, working with the FBI, seized/disrupted 241–242 crypto-fraud websites and 112 servers in Eastern Europe and the US after a three-year investigation. Victims — disproportionately Russian-speaking residents in Brooklyn and across the US — were lured by Russian-language Facebook ads, coached by fake advisers to send crypto to fake trading platforms, locked out on withdrawal, then re-targeted with fake "recovery" offers. Losses are described as tens of millions of dollars; about $300,000 was in the process of being returned. No arrests were announced; the breakthrough was targeting servers/back-end, not just domains, to stop instant clones.
  • Why it matters: The Facebook-ads-to-fake-platform pipeline is the core pig-butchering funnel, and shifting from domain seizures to server seizures is a meaningful enforcement evolution.
  • Source: https://businessmirror.com.ph/2026/10/18/the-cryptocurrency-cop-who-took-down-a-global-scam-network-from-brooklyn/

NEW & EMERGING SCAMS

1. Deepfakes go fully mainstream — INTERPOL says ordinary people are now the target

  • What's new: INTERPOL launched a public awareness campaign on October 18, 2026 warning that cloned voices and fake faces built from "just a small amount of content found online" are now used for investment fraud, impersonation, romance scams, sextortion and disinformation against everyday people — e.g. a video call from a "colleague" asking for a transfer, or a panicked call from a "family member" in trouble. Campaign line: "wait, validate and don't take the bait." INTERPOL notes AI is wiping out classic tells like spelling mistakes and clumsy formatting.
  • Who's targeted: Everyone — families (voice-clone emergency calls), employees/finance staff (fake colleague video calls), online daters.
  • Date/source: Campaign announced Oct 18, 2026 (INTERPOL, as cited); article published Oct 19 — verified
  • Tip: Set a family safe word now; hang up and call back on a number you already have before sending money, even if the voice/face look right.
  • Source: https://aiparabellum.com/interpol-ai-deepfake-scams-campaign/

2. Fake Claude / ChatGPT tools — "InstallFix" paste-a-command lures steal sessions, keys and code

  • What's new: ANY.RUN research logged 1,258 public sandbox analyses from April 15–Oct 5, 2026 tied to counterfeit AI tools; Claude impersonation was the largest share, then ChatGPT/Codex, plus Copilot and Gemini fakes. About 45% told the victim to paste a command into Terminal/PowerShell. A variant, "LLMshare," hosts the malicious install instructions inside real shared chats on chatgpt.com / claude.ai, so the link itself is on a trusted domain. Payloads are infostealers/RATs that export browser cookies for session theft without a new MFA prompt.
  • Who's targeted: Developers (source code, SSH keys, CI/CD tokens, cloud/AI API keys), marketers and ad buyers.
  • Date/source: Dark Reading, October 16, 2026 — verified; corroborated by ANY.RUN and Oct 17 coverage
  • Tip: Install AI tools only from the vendor site or official package manager. Never paste a terminal command copied from an ad, search result, shared chat or third-party doc.
  • Source: https://Www.Darkreading.com/application-security/fake-claude-chatgpt-attacks-developers-risk

3. Calendar phishing (ICS) surge — fake meetings auto-land on your calendar

  • What's new: Malicious .ics calendar invites were up ~33,000% between May and September (Sublime Security). Invites can appear in Google Calendar without the victim opening the email, bypassing spam filters; links lead to fake logins, malware, or a live meeting with an impersonator. Newer wrinkles: AI assistants are auto-accepting invites on workers' behalf, and a Fortra-tracked variant has an attacker ask a non-sales employee to forward a booking link to Sales, so the malicious link arrives from a trusted colleague.
  • Who's targeted: Professionals who meet strangers — sales, recruiters, client-facing staff — plus anyone with auto-add calendar settings.
  • Date/source: Morning Brew, October 13, 2026 — verified; Guardian/Consumer Affairs report the same figures
  • Tip: Turn off "automatically add invitations"; verify any unrecognised meeting with the organiser on a separate channel before clicking.
  • Source: https://www.morningbrew.com/stories/the-latest-phishing-scam-is-targeting-your-calendar

4. Teams impersonation industrialises — fake IT helpdesk in ~30% of orgs; 5-day-old fake Teams login domain flagged Oct 18 (developing)

  • What's new: Ontinue's 1H 2026 report found ~30% of monitored organisations saw at least one fake IT-support impersonation attempt over Microsoft Teams, across 393,433 external contacts — about 1 in 990 showed impersonation signs. The playbook: a message/call from "IT" persuading the employee to grant remote access (AnyDesk, Quick Assist, ScreenConnect). Developing: researcher Steven Lim flagged teams-cloud[.]live on October 18, 2026 when the domain was five days old, copying the full Teams login screen; no victim count or operator has been established — treat as a block-now warning, not a confirmed breach.
  • Who's targeted: Workplace Teams users, especially staff who can grant remote access or approve payments.
  • Date/source: SecureWorld, October 14, 2026 (Ontinue); Cyber Security News on the Oct 18 domain flag — both verified
  • Tip: Real IT will not contact you from an external Teams account to ask for remote access. Only sign in on microsoft.com domains.
  • Sources: https://www.secureworld.io/industry-news/fake-it-support-on-teams ; https://cybersecuritynews.com/teams-domain-login-page/

5. QR-code + adversary-in-the-middle session theft — you pass MFA and still hand over the account (developing)

  • What's new: A campaign tracked as UAT-11985 paired event-themed invitations built from public event information with altered QR codes and a Google lookalike flow fronted by an AiTM relay. The relay forwards the real login in real time, so completing sign-in and the second-factor step hands the attacker the live authenticated session token — code-based MFA is bypassed in practice. Separately, Microsoft announced Teams will now extract URLs hidden in QR-code images in messages and warn/block on malicious destinations, rolling out globally by early November.
  • Who's targeted: In this campaign, people affiliated with Taiwanese research organisations; the technique generalises to any Google/Microsoft account holder who scans an invite/poster QR code.
  • Date/source: Netcrook analysis published Oct 12, 2026, recirculated Oct 19 — single-source technical reporting, labelled developing
  • Tip: Preview the URL after scanning; don't sign in from a QR code on an invite/poster. Use passkeys/hardware keys for high-value accounts, and revoke active sessions if you suspect a scan-and-sign-in.
  • Source: https://netcrook.com/written_article?slug=qr-phishing-aitm-google-logins&lang=en

6. Election-season AI phishing & smishing — FBI/CISA warn the typo tell is dead

  • What's new: Back-to-back PSAs ahead of the November midterms: on October 16, 2026 the FBI and CISA warned actors are using AI tools to write fluent, native-language phishing/smishing and deepfake audio/video to create fake identities — spelling/grammar errors are no longer a reliable tell. A companion FBI alert on Oct 15 named four voter schemes using candidates' names, images, logos and AI-cloned voices: fake campaign "investment pools," fake PACs, merchandise that never ships, and fake voter-registration messages.
  • Who's targeted: Election officials, candidates and campaigns; voters and donors.
  • Date/source: IC3 PSA261016, October 16, 2026 — primary source, verified
  • Tip: Use phishing-resistant MFA on email/VPN/critical accounts. Donate or register only by typing the official address yourself.
  • Source: https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2026/PSA261016

7. Marketplace "that's your name on the listing" reply lure — the reply itself is the payload

  • What's new: Malwarebytes documented texts/iMessages asking "Is this still available for purchase?" attached to a fake Facebook Marketplace listing image where the seller name is the recipient's own name (with a profile photo lifted from a different Facebook user with the same name, likely built from breached name+phone data). There is no link and no money ask — the goal is to provoke a corrective reply ("That's not me"), confirming the number is live and human for follow-on scam waves. The campaign's ultimate objective remains unconfirmed.
  • Who's targeted: Anyone whose name+mobile number appeared in breached/brokered data.
  • Date/source: Malwarebytes Oct 6, 2026; Consumer Affairs Oct 7 — verified across multiple outlets
  • Tip: Don't reply — not even to correct it. Open the real Facebook app yourself and check your Marketplace activity there; block and report the number.
  • Source: https://www.robinspost.com/news/consumer-news/consumer-daily-reports/2271177-consumer-news-fake-facebook-marketplace-listings-use-your-name-to-draw-you-into-a-scam?utm_source=dlvr.it&utm_medium=facebook

Also tracking: Investment/crypto texts are now the #1 scam text in 47 states, funneling to WhatsApp groups (McAfee Labs via Money, Oct 17, 2026; FTC figures cited: >$7.9B reported lost to investment scams in 2025, median loss >$10,000). Source: https://money.com/investment-scam-texts-most-common-states/?apxa1=homepage

RESEARCH DESK (2025–2026 research; preprints labelled)

1. Filter-then-Verify: social-engineering detection in email networks (PREPRINT)

  • Authors: Barsat Khadka, Prasant Koirala, Kshitiz Neupane, Nick Rahimi
  • Venue/date: arXiv:2605.17201, submitted May 17, 2026 — not peer-reviewed
  • Key finding: A two-stage system first uses a Graph Neural Network to flag anomalous sender–receiver patterns (86% recall in structural filtering), then a ModernBERT model reads message context to cut false positives, reaching over 92% precision. Tested on Enron data augmented with synthetic campaigns, it detected both external attacks and insider-threat cases — combining who talks to whom with what they say beats either alone.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.17201

2. What are brands telling you about smishing? (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Authors: Dev Vikesh Doshi, Mehjabeen Tasnim, Fernando Landeros, Chinthagumpala Muni Venkatesh, Daniel Timko, Muhammad Lutfor Rahman
  • Venue/date: Symposium on Usable Security and Privacy (USEC) 2026, Feb 27, 2026, DOI 10.14722/usec.2026.23067; arXiv:2601.20999 — peer-reviewed
  • Key finding: Brand guidance on SMS phishing is patchy. Of 149 well-known brands across 25 categories, only 46% even defined smishing, less than 1% offered a video tutorial, and only 50% told customers how to report a smishing text. Terminology and advice varied widely, and some brands recommended potentially ineffective tactics like simply "ignoring suspicious messages." The authors call for standardised guidance.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.20999

3. Quishing and LLM-generated phishing against organisations (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Authors: Marie Weinz, Nicola Zannone, Luca Allodi, Giovanni Apruzzese
  • Venue/date: ACM AsiaCCS '25; arXiv:2505.12104 — peer-reviewed (reused from Oct 14 edition, still directly relevant)
  • Key finding: In three real-world simulations sending over 71,000 emails, QR-code phishing was just as effective as traditional link phishing at luring employees to a fake landing page — but much harder for operational detectors to spot. In one company, over 30% of opened LLM-written phishes led to a landing-page visit. Higher self-reported phishing awareness correlated with greater organisational resilience.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.12104

4. PreScam: predicting scam progression from early conversations (PREPRINT)

  • Authors: Weixiang Sun, Shang Ma, Yiyang Li, Tianyi Ma, Zehong Wang, Colby Nelson, Xusheng Xiao, Yanfang Ye
  • Venue/date: arXiv:2605.12243, submitted May 12, 2026 — not peer-reviewed
  • Key finding: PreScam turns 177,989 raw user-submitted scam reports into 11,573 structured conversational scam instances across 20 categories, annotated by "kill chain" stage and the scammer's psychological actions. Supervised encoders substantially outperform zero-shot LLMs at predicting in real time whether a conversation is approaching its money-loss stage, while predicting the scammer's next action remains only moderately successful even for strong LLMs.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.12243

5. MemoPhishAgent: memory-augmented phishing URL detection (PREPRINT)

  • Authors: Xuan Chen, Hao Liu, Tao Yuan, Mehran Kafai, Piotr Habas, Xiangyu Zhang
  • Venue/date: arXiv:2602.21394, submitted Feb 24, 2026, revised Apr 21, 2026 — not peer-reviewed
  • Key finding: Remembering past phishing cases helps an AI detector catch new ones. This multi-modal LLM agent with episodic memory improved recall by 13.6% over three state-of-the-art baselines on public datasets, and by 20% on real suspicious URLs crawled from five social media platforms; episodic memory alone contributed up to a 27% recall gain. In production it processes 60,000 high-risk URLs weekly at 91.44% recall.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21394

6. The deepfakes we missed: detectors built for a threat that didn't arrive (PREPRINT — position paper)

  • Authors: Shaina Raza (Vector Institute)
  • Venue/date: arXiv:2605.12075, submitted May 12, 2026 — not peer-reviewed; evidence-based position paper, not an experimental study (reused from Sep 29 edition)
  • Key finding: Nearly a decade of deepfake-detection research was built for a 2017–2019 threat model — face-swapped public figures and election misinformation — that did not materialise in the 2024 global information environment. An accounting of 2022–2026 incidents shows the dominant real harms are peer-generated non-consensual intimate imagery, voice-clone scam calls targeting families and finance workers, and emotional-manipulation fraud. The paper argues the misalignment between research/benchmarks and actual harms — not model capability — is now the main bottleneck. Directly relevant to today's INTERPOL lead.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.12075

TODAY'S SAFETY TIP

Set a family safe word today — a familiar voice is no longer proof of identity. Sunday's INTERPOL campaign (Oct 18) warned that cloned voices and fake faces built from a small amount of online content are now aimed at ordinary families: a panicked call from a "relative" in trouble, or a video call from a "colleague" asking for a transfer. Do this now: (1) agree a short, unguessable family safe word that is never posted or texted anywhere, and treat any emergency money request that can't produce it as a scam; (2) hang up and call back on a number you already have saved — never a number the caller gives you; (3) slow down on purpose: INTERPOL's line is "wait, validate and don't take the bait," and urgency is the scammer's main tool.
