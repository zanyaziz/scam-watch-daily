WIRED exposes 400+ cloaked AI apps hiding explicit deepfake generators — promoted through Meta ads, sold via Apple and Google Pay.

Lead

WIRED exposes 400+ cloaked AI apps hiding explicit deepfake generators — promoted through Meta ads, sold via Apple and Google Pay. A WIRED investigation published November 2 traced a network of more than 400 apps and domains posing as pet-photo editors, language tutors and workout planners that could load a hidden web interface for generating sexually explicit and violent AI imagery from uploaded photos. Apple removed 19 apps and Meta acted against 11,600 ad accounts — but similar ads and apps kept surfacing as reporting ended. Note: the Amazon impersonation figures republished in UK outlets on November 2 (£86,837 over nine days) were already covered in the November 1 edition, so they are not repeated here.

1. WIRED exposes 400+ cloaked AI apps hiding explicit deepfake tools; Apple and Meta act

  • Outlet: WIRED investigation, as reported by Silicon Report (corroborated by Analytics Insight / AI Weekly)
  • Date: November 2, 2026 (verified — Silicon Report dated Nov 2, 2026, stating the WIRED investigation was "published on Monday")
  • Summary: WIRED traced more than 400 connected apps and domains posing as pet-photo editors, language tutors and workout planners that could load a hidden web interface for generating sexually explicit and violent AI imagery from uploaded photos. Researchers at AI Forensics and Infoblox linked the cluster to Chinese-language codebases, Chinese hosting, and Hong Kong-registered KeLing Technology Limited, which did not respond to requests for comment. Apple removed 19 apps, including pet-photo app Viefur; Meta acted against 11,600 ad accounts, about 4,700 business pages and 15,000 pages, and blocked 209 apps from advertising.

2. Forged tax letters in France use QR codes to steal crypto wallet recovery phrases

  • Date: November 2, 2026 (campaign traces to at least August 2026, with rotating domains; similar DAC8 phishing reported in Germany and the Netherlands)
  • How it works: Fraudsters mail letters forged as France's tax authority (DGFiP), citing the EU's DAC8 crypto-reporting rules and threatening fines of €750/€1,500 plus 40–80% surcharges. A QR code supposedly to "register" holdings leads to a counterfeit government-style site that harvests tax logins, then asks for the victim's crypto wallet recovery phrase.
  • Why it matters: A novel offline-to-online "quishing" twist weaponising a real rule (DAC8 effective January 1, 2026) — a paper letter feels official, and the QR code hides the malicious link.
  • Source: https://the420.in/france-fake-dac8-crypto-tax-scam/

RESEARCH DESK

1. "Account suspension. Verify now": using systemic-functional linguistics to improve phishing text advisories for vulnerable groups (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Title: "Account suspension. Verify now": using systemic-functional linguistics to improve phishing text advisories for vulnerable groups
  • Authors: Desiree Kawabata (Griffith University, Gold Coast, Australia)
  • Venue / Date: Humanities and Social Sciences Communications (Nature Portfolio), published October 22, 2026. DOI: 10.1057/s41599-026-09540-7
  • Key finding: Comparing 106 Australian phishing texts with legitimate texts from the organisations scammers most imitate, scam messages systematically use urgency-and-authority language — particular clause types, time/location/manner markers, high modality ("must", "now") and conjunctions — to pressure fast compliance. Exploratory by the author's own framing: it identifies features likely to manipulate recipients, not proven causal effects. Older adults and people from non-English-speaking backgrounds are singled out as most at risk.
  • Source: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-026-09540-7

2. Device and risk-avoidance behavior in the context of cybersecurity phishing attacks (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Title: Device and risk-avoidance behavior in the context of cybersecurity phishing attacks
  • Authors: Naama Ilany-Tzur (lead, Carnegie Mellon Heinz College) et al., with Ben-Gurion University of the Negev researchers
  • Venue / Date: International Journal of Information Management, 2025. DOI: 10.1016/j.ijinfomgt.2025.102919
  • Key finding: Device changes phishing behaviour. In experiments (250+ MTurk workers each), mobile users were 2.67× more likely than PC users to show risk-avoidant behaviour in experiment 1, and 4.43× more likely in experiment 2 — but only for lower-risk URLs; for obviously high-risk URLs both groups behaved similarly. The authors' interpretation: mobile users don't judge risk better, they dodge the decision, because small screens and on-the-go cognitive load make assessing a link harder. Caveat: lab pop-ups can't fully mimic a real attack. Observational arm: ~500,000 URL requests from 30 US networks over one week.
  • Source: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijinfomgt.2025.102919

3. Talking Like a Phisher: LLM-Based Attacks on Voice Phishing Classifiers (PREPRINT)

  • Title: Talking Like a Phisher: LLM-Based Attacks on Voice Phishing Classifiers
  • Authors: Wenhao Li, Selvakumar Manickam, Yung-Wey Chong, Shankar Karuppayah (Universiti Sains Malaysia)
  • Venue / Date: arXiv:2507.16291 — submitted July 22, 2025 — preprint, not peer-reviewed
  • Key finding: Rewording real scam-call scripts with commercial LLMs can slip past automated voice-phishing detectors while keeping the scam intact. Tested against classifiers trained on a real-world Korean vishing dataset (KorCCViD), transcripts rewritten by GPT-4o cut classifier accuracy by up to 30.96% while preserving meaning (measured by BERTScore). Generation took under 9 seconds per transcript on average, at negligible cost per query — meaning evasion is cheap and fast, not just theoretically possible.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.16291

4. AI-in-the-Loop: Privacy Preserving Real-Time Scam Detection and Conversational Scambaiting by Leveraging LLMs and Federated Learning (PREPRINT)

  • Title: AI-in-the-Loop: Privacy Preserving Real-Time Scam Detection and Conversational Scambaiting by Leveraging LLMs and Federated Learning
  • Authors: Ismail Hossain, Sai Puppala, Md Jahangir Alam, Sajedul Talukder
  • Venue / Date: arXiv:2509.05362 — submitted September 4, 2025 (revised November 25, 2025; latest version January 20, 2026) — preprint, not peer-reviewed (a search listing described it as accepted at PETS 2026, but that note did not appear on the verified arXiv abstract page, so it is reported as a preprint)
  • Key finding: A live "scambaiter" AI that keeps a scammer talking while detecting the scam, learning across devices via federated learning without sharing raw conversations. Tests: fluent replies (perplexity as low as 22.3, engagement ≈ 0.80); over up to 30 federated rounds, engagement ≈ 0.80, relevance ≈ 0.74, personal-data leakage ≤ 0.0085, even with differential privacy on. Trade-off: stricter moderation leaks less data but shortens conversations; looser settings catch more but risk more exposure.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.05362

5. FIST: A Structured Threat Modeling Framework for Fraud Incidents (PREPRINT)

  • Title: FIST: A Structured Threat Modeling Framework for Fraud Incidents
  • Authors: Yu-Chen Dai, Lu-An Chen, Sy-Jye Her, Yu-Xian Jiang
  • Venue / Date: arXiv:2506.05740 — submitted June 6, 2025 — preprint, not peer-reviewed
  • Key finding: Fraud lacks a shared playbook like cybersecurity's MITRE ATT&CK, so FIST builds one: 4 phases (preparation, promotion, engagement, concealment), 9 major tactics, 93 detailed techniques, 58 detection patterns and 12 mitigations, blending technical tricks with psychological manipulation. Validated on real cases (e.g. fake-expert investment groups that build trust, escalate money requests, then block withdrawals) — aiming for a common language so banks, platforms and researchers can share fraud intelligence and automate detection.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.05740

TODAY'S SAFETY TIP

Before you install an AI photo app, check who really made it — and never upload face photos to an app you haven't vetted. Today's lead story shows how apps that look like harmless pet-photo editors or workout planners can hide a deepfake generator that switches on after install, promoted through ads on Facebook and Instagram. Check the developer name, read recent reviews (not just the rating), and look at what permissions the app wants before uploading a single photo of yourself or your family. If a "fun photo" app asks for selfies or full photo-library access and you can't verify the developer, skip it — your face is the raw material for the next deepfake, and unlike a password, you can't reset it.
