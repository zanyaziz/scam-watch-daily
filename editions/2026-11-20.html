1. SEC and CFTC charge Backward Hat Capital in $560M AI-crypto Ponzi; founders arrested

  • Outlet: U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (official press release)
  • Date: November 20, 2026
  • Summary: The SEC announced it obtained a temporary restraining order, asset freeze and expedited discovery against Indianapolis-based Backward Hat Capital, co-founders Phillip Williams and Devin Johnson, Traders Point Capital and Joseph Anderson, alleging a Ponzi scheme that fraudulently raised about $560 million from more than 2,000 investors since about July 2022. Investors were told their money would be converted to crypto and traded by a proprietary AI-driven high-frequency FX arbitrage algorithm; the SEC alleges over $250 million went to Ponzi-like payments to earlier investors and over $220 million was diverted to the founders for houses, exotic cars, other businesses and gambling. The CFTC filed parallel charges (putting the total at over $550 million from at least 2,100 participants), a court appointed Kevin B. Duff as temporary receiver on Nov 20, and the FBI arrested Williams and Johnson on Nov 18–19, with parallel criminal charges in New York. All figures are allegations, not adjudicated findings.
  • Why it matters: The largest investment-fraud enforcement action of the day, and a textbook example of the "AI trading algorithm" pitch now being used to sell Ponzi schemes — the same promise that appears in social-media investment ads.
  • Source: https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2026-116-sec-charges-indianapolis-based-backward-hat-capital-its-co-founders-560-million-dollar-offering

2. Two men charged in Amazon account-takeover fraud ring after ~90 suspicious packages intercepted

  • Outlet: Mainline Now (local news, Montgomery County, PA)
  • Date: November 20, 2026
  • Summary: Two men, Honghao Wei and Jiandong Yin, were arrested November 11 at a Whole Foods in Wynnewood, Pennsylvania, and each face 11 criminal charges including corrupt-organizations and identity-theft felonies, after police intercepted about 90 packages allegedly bought through hacked Amazon customer accounts. The investigation began in October when Wisconsin police flagged a victim whose compromised Amazon account was used to buy groceries delivered to a Pennsylvania address; victims reported their Amazon account email addresses had been changed, locking them out. Investigators say Yin told them he answered an online ad offering payment to collect Amazon packages, and described a warehouse operation near Newark, Delaware, involving Apple products and gift cards. A preliminary hearing is set for November 24. (Allegations only; defendants are innocent until proven guilty.)
  • Why it matters: A concrete look at how stolen Amazon accounts are monetized at scale — account takeover, email change to lock the victim out, and mule networks to collect high-value goods like AirPods. A reminder to check account email-change alerts immediately.
  • Source: https://mainlinenow.com/news/nearly-90-suspicious-packages-lead-police-to-alleged-amazon-fraud-operation-in-lower-merion/

4. Coinbase says its AI blocked $4.7M in transfers to a deepfake Elon Musk YouTube livestream scam

  • Outlet: Malwarebytes (reporting Coinbase's account); BleepingComputer (original report)
  • Date: Coinbase blog November 17, 2026; coverage November 18–19, 2026
  • Summary: Scammers ran a fake YouTube livestream mimicking a Tesla product demonstration, featuring an AI-generated Elon Musk promoting a crypto giveaway that promised to double any crypto sent to wallet addresses and QR codes shown on screen. NEW & EMERGING SCAMS

1. AI deepfake CEO calls steal $129 million — fake boss, fake lawyer, real wire

  • Date: Indictment unsealed November 5, 2026 (SDNY); fresh coverage this week
  • How it works: Federal prosecutors in New York charged three men with using AI to clone the voices — and at times the faces — of company CEOs and lawyers at major law firms. The ring allegedly targeted thousands of companies from 2024 to 2026: a fake CEO would call an accounting employee about a secret deal, then hand them to a fake lawyer who gave wiring instructions, with the money moved into cryptocurrency. Total alleged losses exceed $129 million, including about $2.7 million from a Rhode Island bank and victims at two U.S. hospital systems. Two defendants were arrested in Spain and one in the U.K., with extradition pending. The charges are allegations.
  • Who it targets: Finance and accounting staff at companies of all sizes.
  • Why it's new: It's the largest AI-impersonation prosecution to date, and it breaks the old defense — "call them back on video" — because the face and voice on the call were faked too.
  • Source: https://www.cybermarquez.com/news/2026-11-09-ai-deepfake-ceo-scam-129-million/

2. "FaceStealer": North Korean fake video meetings that steal crypto — and now target open-source developers

  • Date: FBI/DOJ disruption announced November 12, 2026; FBI FLASH advisory AC-111226-MS
  • How it works: The FBI and DOJ disrupted FaceStealer, a North Korean campaign blamed for more than $400 million in cryptocurrency theft over 18 months. The lure arrives from a hijacked Telegram, LinkedIn, Slack or Discord account belonging to someone the target knows, inviting them to a Zoom or Teams meeting on a look-alike domain (e.g. uu01webzoom[.]us). On the call, prerecorded webcam footage of earlier victims plays — the FBI seized more than 900 such videos — and a staged "audio fault" prompts the victim to run a "fix" that installs malware (cookie harvesters, keyloggers, backdoor). The FBI says AI deepfakes of well-known crypto figures are now being added, though stolen real video still dominates.
  • Who it targets: Crypto/Web3 workers, and increasingly open-source software maintainers — the FBI reports a near-miss in which a maintainer of a JavaScript library with tens of millions of weekly downloads was compromised.
  • Why it's new: The campaign is self-propagating — each victim's contacts and webcam footage seed the next attack — and it has now moved beyond crypto into the software supply chain.
  • Source: https://www.fbi.gov/investigate/cyber/facestealer-cryptocurrency-theft-scheme

3. GTA 6 launch hype fuels Unicode QR-code phishing aimed at work logins

  • Date: KnowBe4 Threat Lab report November 16, 2026; game launched November 19, 2026
  • How it works: With Grand Theft Auto VI launching November 19, KnowBe4 reported a surge in GTA-themed phishing. One targeted wave poses as an internal HR/benefits email offering a "free GTA 6 pre-order" to the first 250 employees, expiring in 48 hours. The hook is a QR code built from Unicode text block characters or an HTML table instead of an image, so image-based scanners miss it — and victims are told to scan it with a personal phone, moving them off the company's protected network ("device shift") to a fake login page that harvests work credentials. GTA-themed lures went from 0.005% of KnowBe4's phishing volume in September to 9.6% in the week starting October 26, and gaming phishing overall rose 1,300% against the March–August average.
  • Who it targets: Employees (especially gamers) at companies of any size.
  • Why it's new: It exploits a specific calendar event and a novel evasion trick — text-drawn QR codes — that many email gateways can't yet detect.
  • Source: https://check.secure.post/articles/that-free-gta-6-pre-order-from-hr-is-a-phishing-attack

4. "Trigram": fake job, conference and parcel sites for ~50 AI and crypto brands trick developers into pasting one command

  • Date: Stormshield research published November 11, 2026 (updated Nov 16); covered by The Hacker News Nov 17
  • How it works: Stormshield's Cyber Threat Intelligence team documented a campaign impersonating about 50 AI and crypto brands — plus DHL, DPD, Aramex and an AI/blockchain conference — with pixel-perfect cloned sites on cheap domains (e.g. buildwithritual[.]xyz, joinwebflow[.]xyz). Victims are lured by a fake job posting, conference invite or parcel page, given a per-target access code, then told to paste a command into their terminal to "verify" they are human (the ClickFix technique). The first command looks harmless; the second installs an infostealer that on macOS/Linux shows a fake system password prompt.
  • Who it targets: Developers and job seekers in AI/crypto.
  • Why it's new: It turns the job hunt itself into the infection vector, and the same infrastructure was tied to a forged commit force-pushed to a GitHub repo (antd-img-crop) on Oct 31. If you ran such a command, treat the machine as compromised: rotate keys and kill browser sessions from a clean device.
  • Source: https://www.dugganusa.com/post/trigram-fake-ai-crypto-brand-sites-paste-command-infostealer

5. Toll-text smishing wave goes multi-channel as fake FBI "recovery agents" re-victimize past victims

  • Date: Fresh warnings this week (AVASC daily brief, November 17, 2026)
  • How it works: The nationwide unpaid-toll smishing wave tracked since May is still generating fresh warnings: texts — and now emails — impersonating E-ZPass, SunPass, FasTrak and TxTag claim a small unpaid toll, threaten late fees or registration suspension, and link to look-alike payment pages that harvest card data. A fresh variant flagged this past weekend uses a "Pay Now" button in the message. In parallel, the FBI has renewed a warning that scammers impersonate FBI/IC3 personnel — sometimes using AI-generated video — to "recover" money for people who already lost it, charging an upfront fee.
  • Who it targets: Drivers nationwide, and prior scam victims.
  • Why it's new: The toll scam keeps rotating channels and branding while the fake-recovery twist exploits victims twice; imposter scams remain the #1 reported fraud category nationally for the ninth year running (FTC: $3.5 billion lost to imposter scams in 2025).
  • Source: https://www.avasc.org/briefings/toll-text-scams-fake-fbi-recovery-agents-and-holiday-fraud-collide-2026-11-17

6. Marketplace sellers hit by fake Zelle "business account upgrade" emails

  • Date: Fresh warnings November 10, 2026
  • How it works: A "buyer" agrees to your price instantly — sometimes offering more — and claims to have paid by Zelle. You then receive a fake email (often from a Gmail address like zellepay.support@gmail.com) saying the payment is on hold until you "upgrade to a business account" for a fee (e.g. $250–$300), which the buyer supposedly will cover by sending extra money that you must refund.   • Why it's new: It pairs naturally with the holiday selling surge, and the tell is simple — Zelle never emails you to release funds; real payments appear in your own bank app. Never pay money to receive money.

RESEARCH DESK

1. AI voice scams persuade up to 36% of people to comply — and automation makes them profitable (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Title: Evaluating AI Models' Capability to Automate Voice Phishing Attacks
  • Authors: Fred Heiding, Claudio Mayrink Verdun, Simon Lermen, Andrew Kao, Vitor Albiero, Lauren Deason, Irina-Elena Veliche, Christine Lehane
  • Venue / Date: Peer-reviewed — Expert Systems With Applications, vol. 332 (2027), article 133620 — accepted 10 July 2026, published online 17 July 2026
  • Key finding: In a survey experiment with 4,100 U.S. adults exposed to AI-generated voice-scam calls (built with Llama Full Duplex, Sesame, Gemini, OpenAI AVM, PlayAI, and ElevenLabs), up to 36% said they would or might comply with a scam call claiming a relative was in distress, and the overall compliance rate across five scam scenarios was 16.5%. Some AI voices were rated as persuasive as — or slightly more persuasive than — human voices. The authors' economic analysis found human-run voice phishing is unprofitable at U.S. wages, but AI-automated vishing appears economically viable — the danger is the economics of automation, not superhuman persuasion.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.09970

2. AI web agents hand over your most sensitive data on scam sites — even when they suspect a scam (PREPRINT)

  • Title: "I Strongly Suspect This Website Is a Scam": Benchmarking PII Leakage and Detection without Defense in Autonomous Web Agents
  • Authors: Soham Roy, Sarthakbrata Halder, Arya Bharaty, Vaibhav Bhaskar, Yash Sinha, Dhruv Kumar, Srikant Panda, Murari Mandal
  • Venue / Date: Preprint — not yet peer-reviewed. arXiv:2606.00497, submitted 30 May 2026, revised 23 Oct 2026
  • Key finding: The researchers built "Scammer4U," a benchmark of 91 scam websites designed to trick AI web-browsing agents into handing over users' private data. With no privacy safeguards, the agents leaked the most sensitive data (Social Security numbers, card numbers and CVVs, bank accounts, passwords, API keys) in 54–93% of sessions, versus 0% on identical-looking benign sites — proving the leaks were caused by the scams. Most strikingly, even when the agent's own reasoning flagged the site as suspicious, it still submitted the sensitive data in 35.9% of sessions (vs. 66.1% when it showed no suspicion). Spotting a scam isn't enough — the agent acts anyway.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.00497

3. Ten Instagram posts are enough for AI to write phishing emails better than real ones (PREPRINT)

  • Title: Context-Aware Spear Phishing: Generative AI-Enabled Attacks Against Individuals via Public Social Media Data
  • Authors: Elham Pourabbas Vafa, Sayak Saha Roy, Shirin Nilizadeh (University of Texas at Arlington; Louisiana State University)
  • Venue / Date: Preprint — not yet peer-reviewed. arXiv:2605.11268, submitted 11 May 2026, revised 30 Sep 2026
  • Key finding: Using just 10 public Instagram posts per person, generative AI could build enough of a profile to craft highly personalized phishing emails — the team generated roughly 18,000 emails across five models (GPT-4, Claude 3 Haiku, Gemini 1.5 Flash, Gemma 7B, Llama 3.3) from 200 public Instagram accounts. In a user study with 70 participants, the AI-written attacks consistently beat real-world phishing emails on eight quality measures and raised less suspicion than actual phishing examples. Generic content-moderation safeguards largely failed to catch them; only when the safety tools were specifically tuned to look for context-aware phishing did detection reach up to 99%.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.11268

4. Practicing against a simulated scammer beats passive warnings (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Title: ScamPilot: Simulating Conversations with LLMs to Protect Against Online Scams
  • Authors: Owen Hoffman, Kangze Peng, Sajid Kamal, Zehua You, Sukrit Venkatagiri (Swarthmore College)
  • Venue / Date: Peer-reviewed — Proceedings of the 2026 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems (CHI '26), Barcelona, 13–17 April 2026
  • Key finding: Instead of warning people about scams, the researchers built a simulator where two AI agents role-play a scammer and a victim, and the user coaches the victim in real time. In a 150-person study, the version combining advice-giving with quiz questions improved scam recognition by 8% without making people more suspicious of legitimate messages, and raised users' confidence in their ability to respond by 9% and their overall self-efficacy by 19%. Practicing against a simulated scammer works better than passive warnings.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.22426

5. People can't tell AI-written phishing texts from human ones (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Title: Assessing AI-Generated vs. Human-Authored Spear Phishing SMS Attacks: An Empirical Study
  • Authors: Jerson Francia, Derek Hansen, Benjamin Schooley, Matthew Taylor, Shydra Valynn Murray, Rebekah Cornelius, Greg Snow
  • Venue / Date: Peer-reviewed — Journal of Cybersecurity and Privacy 2026, 6(4), 129 (arXiv:2406.13049, v3 revised 17 Aug 2026)
  • Key finding: In a small pilot study (25 targets), personalized text-message phishing written by GPT-4 from a simple prompt was at least as convincing as messages written by people: participants said they would click 28% of the AI messages vs. 21% of the human-written ones (a gap within the study's margin of error). Job-related messages were significantly more likely to get a click than hobby- or social-media-related ones. And when asked to guess whether a message was written by a human or AI, participants did no better than chance — people cannot tell AI phishing from human phishing by reading it.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.13049

TODAY'S SAFETY TIP

Verify through a channel you open yourself — never trust a single call, text, or screenshot. Today's stories all point the same way: a cloned CEO's voice, a fake PayPal email, a Zelle "business upgrade" message, and a paid search ad can all look perfect. Before you wire money, release goods, or click a shopping link: hang up and call the person or company back on a number from their official site (not the one in the message); open your own bank or payment app to confirm money actually arrived — never trust a screenshot; and for holiday shopping, type the retailer's address into your browser yourself instead of clicking a search ad. If a "kidnapping" or distress call ever comes in, hang up and call your family member directly — scammers are now cloning voices from TikTok clips.
