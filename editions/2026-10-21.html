Meta ordered to pay $25 billion to New Mexico.

Lead

Meta ordered to pay $25 billion to New Mexico. A state judge on Wednesday ordered Meta to pay $25 billion after a jury found the company misled consumers about data privacy — one of the largest state penalties ever against a tech platform, and Meta's second New Mexico jury loss in six months. The same day, the White House said the DOJ's Scam Center Strike Force has frozen nearly $1 billion in scam-linked crypto, the EU opened a Digital Services Act investigation into Booking.com over fake listings and phishing, YouTube launched an anti-hijack "Temporary Feature Pause," and Malwarebytes exposed a fake Amazon Music × YouTube deal harvesting creators' Google logins. Oct 21 was not a quiet day.

1. Meta ordered to pay $25 billion to New Mexico after jury finds company misled consumers about data privacy

  • Outlet: Reuters
  • Date: October 21, 2026 — verified (Reuters URL dated 2026-10-21; judge ordered payment "on Wednesday"). The jury verdict itself was Sept 25, 2026; the $25B penalty order is the Oct 21 news.
  • Summary: A New Mexico state judge, Francis Mathew, ordered Meta Platforms to pay $25 billion after a jury found 26 of 29 company/leadership statements misleading and more than 43 million violations of New Mexico consumer-protection law. The case, sued in 2021 by AG Raúl Torrez and linked to Cambridge Analytica harvesting data from up to 87 million Facebook users, accused Meta of lying about who had access to user data and about handling hate speech/misinformation. Mathew said a penalty of more than $219 billion was warranted under state law (up to $5,000 per violation) but reduced it to $25 billion. Meta argued for a $3.45B cap and is expected to appeal.
  • Why it matters: One of the largest state penalties ever against a tech platform, and the second New Mexico jury loss for Meta in six months (March verdict: $375M, plus $567M teen mental-health fund). It reframes platform deception — misleading privacy/safety statements — as per-user consumer-protection violations at massive scale, a theory other states can copy.

4. YouTube launches "Temporary Feature Pause" to block hijacked channels from streaming and uploading

  • Outlet: Dexerto (reporting YouTube's Oct 21 announcement)
  • Date: October 21, 2026 — verified (Dexerto published 2026-10-21; "in an October 21 announcement, YouTube revealed…")
  • Summary: YouTube announced Temporary Feature Pause, which automatically restricts a channel when it detects unusual/unauthorized activity — no uploads or edits to videos/Shorts, no livestreams, no playlist creation, no custom thumbnails, and no switching private videos to unlisted/public — typically for about 24 hours while security reviews. RESEARCH DESK (2025–2026 research; preprints labelled; all six are new to this newsletter)

1. Phishing 2.0: exploring the capabilities and risks of agentic AI-enabled attacks (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Authors: Pallavi Chandre, Pallavi Bhujbal, R. Kerketta, J. Nandimath, B. Shendkar, R. Bhosale
  • Venue/date: Frontiers in Computer Science, Vol. 8, Article 1795045, DOI 10.3389/fcomp.2026.1795045 — published March 25, 2026 — peer-reviewed journal survey/review (not a new experiment)
  • Key finding: Phishing is moving from human-written lures and AI-assisted drafts to fully autonomous, goal-driven campaigns. This survey argues agentic AI adds planning, contextual personalisation, multi-modal content (text, image, voice/deepfake) and adaptive feedback loops across email, social media, chat and voice — so a campaign can refine itself mid-attack and evade conventional signature/heuristic detection. The authors are explicit that fully autonomous closed-loop campaigns remain largely experimental/emerging rather than widely deployed. Takeaway: defences built for static templates will not hold.
  • Source: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/computer-science/articles/10.3389/fcomp.2026.1795045/full

2. DeepFake Detection in Dyadic Video Calls using Point of Gaze Tracking (PREPRINT)

  • Authors: Odin Kohler, Rahul Vijaykumar, Masudul H. Imtiaz
  • Venue/date: arXiv:2509.25503, submitted Sept 29, 2025, last revised Jan 10, 2026 (v2) — not peer-reviewed
  • Key finding: A deepfake on a video call may get the face right but not the eye movements. In a one-to-one call the system can estimate where the person on screen is looking; real gaze follows subtle conversational, non-verbal patterns that current real-time deepfakes do not mimic. Built on explainable gaze features and tested on a novel dataset created by the authors, the method achieved 82% accuracy — presented as the first reported use of point-of-gaze tracking for deepfake detection. Directly relevant to real-time video-meeting phishing / CEO-fraud calls. Caveat: authors' own dataset, proof-of-concept stage.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.25503

3. Phishing Email Detection Using Large Language Models — the LLM-PEA framework (PREPRINT)

  • Authors: Najmul Hasan, Prashanth BusiReddyGari, Haitao Zhao, Yihao Ren, Jinsheng Xu, Shaohu Zhang
  • Venue/date: arXiv:2512.10104, submitted Dec 10, 2025, last revised Dec 14, 2025 (v2) — not peer-reviewed
  • Key finding: LLMs can spot phishing — and can themselves be phished. Evaluating three frontier models (GPT-4o, Claude Sonnet 4, and Grok-3) under the LLM-PEA framework across prompt injection, text-refinement and multilingual attacks, the paper reports LLMs detecting phishing emails at over 90% accuracy, while also showing LLM-based detectors remain exploitable by adversarial refinement, prompt injection and cross-lingual attacks, with attack success rates of 10–40% reported in the full-text evaluation. Practical point: an LLM filter is not a finished defence — it needs hardening before deployment.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.10104

4. TLS Certificate and Domain Feature Analysis of Phishing Domains in the Danish .dk Namespace (PREPRINT)

  • Authors: Athanasios P. Pelekoudas, Epameinondas Bolis, Jasmin Lindner, Prodromos Kyriakidis, Mathias Davidsen, Johannes T. E. Hansen, Christian H. Reichkendler, Sajad Homayoun
  • Venue/date: arXiv:2603.21652, submitted March 23, 2026, last revised May 18, 2026 (v2) — not peer-reviewed
  • Key finding: The padlock proves almost nothing on its own. Combining Punktum dk registry data with phishing reports, popularity rankings, Netlas TLS data, DNS and lexical features, the study compared phishing, popular and less-visited .dk domains. Several features differed between phishing and highly popular domains, but phishing domains closely resembled ordinary less-popular domains, with substantial overlap — so no individual certificate or domain feature was a reliable standalone indicator of phishing. Combined signals may help; any single "check the cert" rule will not.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.21652

5. ALFA: A Safe-by-Design Approach to Mitigate Quishing Attacks Launched via Fancy QR Codes (PREPRINT)

  • Authors: Muhammad Wahid Akram, Keshav Sood, Muneeb Ul Hassan, Dhananjay Thiruvady
  • Venue/date: arXiv:2601.06768, submitted Jan 11, 2026 — not peer-reviewed
  • Key finding: Colourful, branded "fancy" QR codes evade visual deep-learning detectors that work on plain black-and-white codes. ALFA first converts a fancy code back to a binary grid, recovers erroneous modules with its "FAST" method, then extracts structural features and predicts legitimacy before the harmful payload is opened. On a synthetic dataset of diverse fancy QR variations it reported a false-negative rate of only 0.06%, and the team built a mobile app and compared it with real-world QR readers. Caveat: headline result is on synthetic data, not a field trial.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.06768

6. CIC-Trap4Phish: A Unified Multi-Format Dataset for Phishing and Quishing Attachment Detection (PREPRINT)

  • Authors: Fatemeh Nejati, Mahdi Rabbani, Morteza Eskandarian, Mansur Mirani, Gunjan Piya, Igor Opushnyev, Ali A. Ghorbani, Sajjad Dadkhah
  • Venue/date: arXiv:2602.09015, submitted Feb 9, 2026, last revised Feb 11, 2026 (v3) — not peer-reviewed
  • Key finding: Most phishing datasets cover email text or URLs — attackers hide payloads in attachments. CIC-Trap4Phish is a unified malicious/benign dataset across five formats used in campaigns: Word documents, Excel spreadsheets, PDFs, HTML pages, and QR-code images. For the first four, the authors propose an execution-free static pipeline (structural, lexical and metadata features, with SHAP + feature-importance selection) so files are judged without opening them; lightweight models (Random Forest, XGBoost, Decision Tree) are reported in the abstract as achieving high detection accuracy across formats (no single headline accuracy is given in the abstract). For QR images they combine CNN image detection with lexical analysis of the decoded URL.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.09015

TODAY'S SAFETY TIP

If a "deal," a caller, or a pop-up wants your login, your cash, or your screen — stop and verify through a channel you chose. Wednesday's news hit the same nerve three ways: a fake Amazon Music × YouTube portal harvested creators' Google logins and MFA codes behind a fake "verified" success screen; a fake Apple pop-up talked a victim into 90 minutes of screen-sharing and an SSN request; and bank impostors sent a rideshare to collect withdrawn cash at the door. Do this instead: (1) never sign in, share your screen, or enter an MFA code from a link, pop-up, or call you didn't initiate — close it and go to the official app or site you typed yourself; (2) hang up on any "bank" or "Apple" caller and call back on the number on your card or in the official app; (3) never withdraw cash or hand money to a courier or rideshare a caller sent — a real bank will never ask for that.
