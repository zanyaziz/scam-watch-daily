NEW & EMERGING SCAMS

1. Calendar-invite phishing — malicious meetings land on your calendar without an attachment, then a fake Drive link or an AI-deepfake video call does the work

  • What's new: Fresh attention Oct 24, 2026 — LLRX's "Pete Recommends" weekly roundup dated Oct 24 leads with Business Insider's calendar-invite investigation. Invites are processed differently from email attachments, so a malicious link can land on a calendar without the victim opening an attachment. Sublime data cited in earlier coverage: calendar-based phishing rose more than 10x between July and August 2026; ICS (.ics) phishing up ~33,000% May–September.
  • How it works: Two forms. (1) An invite from a stranger contains a link that looks like a Google Drive project brief — one recipient rated a fake Drive page "9.5 out of 10" for convincingness and stopped only after checking the URL. (2) The victim joins a seemingly legitimate video call with someone using a real staffer's name, a fake email address, and AI-generated audio/video. Other invites carry fake missed-call, unpaid-invoice or subscription-renewal lures. AI assistants that auto-accept invites can add them on the worker's behalf.
  • Who's targeted: Workers who routinely meet strangers — sales, recruiting, consulting and other client-facing roles.
  • Tip: Turn off auto-add for incoming invites, check the URL before opening any Drive/doc link in an invite, and verify an unexpected meeting out-of-band before joining a call that asks for money or credentials.
  • Sources: https://www.llrx.com/2026/10/pete-recommends-weekly-highlights-on-cyber-security-issues-october-24-2026/ ; https://www.pcworld.com/article/3259572/calendar-invite-scams-jumped-10x-in-a-month-heres-how-to-avoid-them.html

2. Fake "iPhone Duo" Apple stores — 25% discount, 30-minute countdown, and a "manager will call you" checkout that harvests your details

Checkout collects contact and shipping details, then claims a manager will contact the buyer later to confirm delivery and payment — exposing the buyer to follow-on phishing/social-engineering even if no purchase completes. A Portuguese-speaking variant asks for an email address and WhatsApp-linked phone number just to "check availability."
  • Who's targeted: Shoppers hunting the new foldable in India, plus Portuguese-speaking buyers targeted by the availability-check variant.
  • Tip: Buy only via apple.com or an authorised retailer you navigate to yourself; a countdown plus a callback-to-pay checkout is the tell.

RESEARCH DESK (2025–2026 research; peer-reviewed vs preprint labelled; all six are new to this newsletter)

1. "I can't recognize (yet): Delayed Rendering to Defeat Visual Phishing Detectors" (PEER-REVIEWED — accepted to IEEE EuroS&P '26; preprint version verified)

  • Authors / venue / date: Ying Yuan, Cristiano Alex Rado, Giovanni Apruzzese, Mauro Conti, Luigi Vincenzo Mancini — arXiv:2605.00183, submitted Apr 30, 2026; arXiv record states Accepted to IEEE EuroS&P '26.
  • Key finding, in plain language: The most advanced phishing-page detectors compare a screenshot of a suspect page against the real brand page. This paper shows that check has a timing hole: if the attacker simply delays rendering key elements — e.g. the logo — until after the snapshot is taken, the detector sees an incomplete page and classifies the phish as benign. With easy "curtain effect" techniques, state-of-the-art detectors were completely defeated, with detection rate dropping from 100% to 0%. In a user study, end users also could not reliably spot the trick (p < .05), so humans do not catch what the delay hides either.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.00183

2. "Can AI Models be Jailbroken to Phish Elderly Victims? An End-to-End Evaluation" (PREPRINT — not peer-reviewed)

  • Authors / date: Fred Heiding, Simon Lermen — arXiv:2511.11759, submitted Nov 13, 2025.
  • Key finding, in plain language: An end-to-end test of the whole attack chain — jailbreak an LLM, generate phishing, deploy it against real people. Across six frontier LLMs spanning four attack categories, several models showed near-complete susceptibility to certain jailbreak vectors. In the human validation study with 108 senior volunteers, the AI-generated phishing emails successfully compromised 11% of participants. The authors argue LLMs also remove language barriers and enable multi-turn trust-building at scale, and that providers' voluntary counter-abuse efforts remain insufficient.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.11759

3. "Clouding the Mirror: Stealthy Prompt Injection Attacks Targeting LLM-based Phishing Detection" (PREPRINT — not peer-reviewed)

  • Authors / date: Takashi Koide, Hiroki Nakano, Daiki Chiba — arXiv:2602.05484, submitted Feb 5, 2026; revised Oct 5, 2026 (v2).
  • Key finding, in plain language: LLM phishing detectors read the suspect page — and the attacker controls the page. Attackers can hide instructions a human visitor never notices but the detector LLM still parses, manipulating its verdict. The paper presents the first comprehensive evaluation of prompt injection against multimodal LLM-based phishing detection and shows that even state-of-the-art models such as GPT-5 remain vulnerable, in controlled settings and on real phishing sites. The proposed defence, InjectDefuser, significantly reduced attack success rates across multiple models (the abstract reports this qualitatively, so no single percentage is quoted).
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05484

4. "Agentic Knowledge Distillation: Autonomous Training of Small Language Models for SMS Threat Detection" (PREPRINT — not peer-reviewed)

  • Authors / date: Adel ElZemity, Joshua Sylvester, Budi Arief, Rogério De Lemos — arXiv:2602.10869, submitted Feb 11, 2026.
  • Key finding, in plain language: Labelled smishing data goes stale fast, so the authors let a large LLM act as an autonomous teacher: it generates synthetic threat data and iteratively fine-tunes a small on-device student model until performance plateaus, with no human in the loop. Comparing four teacher LLMs training two student models, the best configuration reached 94.31% accuracy and 96.25% recall. Against a Direct Preference Optimisation baseline using the same synthetic data, the agentic loop won decisively — 86–94% vs. 50–80% accuracy — showing the closed-loop feedback, not just the synthetic data, does the work.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10869

5. "Robustness, Cost, and Attack-Surface Concentration in Phishing Detection" (PREPRINT — not peer-reviewed)

  • Authors / date: Julian Allagan, Mohamed Elbakary, Zohreh Safari, Weizheng Gao, Gabrielle Morgan, Essence Morgan, Vladimir Deriglazov — arXiv:2603.19204, submitted Mar 19, 2026.
  • Key finding, in plain language: Lab accuracy is not deployment security. On the UCI Phishing Websites benchmark (11,055 instances, 30 features), Logistic Regression, Random Forests, Gradient Boosted Trees and XGBoost all scored AUC ≥ 0.979 under static evaluation. But under a cost-aware evasion model, the median minimal evasion cost was just 2 feature edits, and over 80% of successful minimal-cost evasions concentrated on only three low-cost surface features. The authors' conclusion: robustness is governed by feature economics, not model complexity — stop shopping for a fancier classifier and fix the cheap features attackers flip first.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19204

6. "Intergenerational Support for Deepfake Scams Targeting Older Adults" (PREPRINT — not peer-reviewed; qualitative study)

  • Authors / date: Karina LaRubbio, Alyssa Lanter, Seihyun Lee, Mahima Ramesh, Diana Freed — arXiv:2508.11579, submitted Aug 15, 2025.
  • Key finding, in plain language: AI voice/video clones now impersonate grandchildren in fabricated legal or medical emergencies, undermining the cues older adults traditionally use to spot fraud. In focus groups with 37 older adults (ages 65+), participants frequently relied on trusted relationships — checking with family — to detect scams and build protective practices, rather than on technical signals. The paper argues for deliberately engaging youth (whose identities are themselves being cloned) as active partners in older relatives' online safety. This is a small, qualitative study — design direction for family-based defences, not an efficacy measurement.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.11579

TODAY'S SAFETY TIP

You never have to pay — or "upgrade" — to receive money. Saturday's clearest victim lesson came from Marketplace itself: a chair seller was emailed a fake Venmo notice saying she had to upgrade to a business account to receive a buyer's payment, a step that would have withdrawn over $800 from her own bank; a couch seller lost her bank balance to the same "$800 minimum" story paired with a fake PayPal support screen-share. Venmo, PayPal, Zelle and Cash App never require you to send money, upgrade an account, or share your screen to release a payment someone sent you. If a buyer says a payment is "on hold" until you pay a fee or call a support number they gave you, stop, close the chat, and check the payment only inside the official app — if it isn't there, it was never sent.
