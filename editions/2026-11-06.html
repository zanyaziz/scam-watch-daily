Treasury moves to cut crypto exchange MEXC off from U.S. banks over $16.7 billion in illicit flows.

Lead

Treasury moves to cut crypto exchange MEXC off from U.S. banks over $16.7 billion in illicit flows. FinCEN barred U.S. financial institutions from processing transfers to or from MEXC Global, saying the exchange facilitated at least $16.7 billion in likely illicit activity — the biggest financial-system action yet against a crypto off-ramp used by scammers. The same day, the FTC disclosed new details in its Amazon ad-pricing suit, and a former FBI agent pleaded guilty to stealing $2.47 million in crypto from the very investigations he worked.

TOP STORIES (ranked by importance)

1. FinCEN moves to cut crypto exchange MEXC Global off from the U.S. financial system

  • Outlet: U.S. Treasury / FinCEN (press releases); reported by FinanceFeeds, Bitcoin Insider, Nov 6–7
  • Date: November 6, 2026 (verified)
  • Summary: FinCEN issued an order barring U.S. financial institutions from processing funds transfers to or from MEXC Global, a Seychelles-incorporated crypto exchange, finding it a financial institution of primary money-laundering concern. Treasury says MEXC facilitated at least $16.7 billion in likely illicit activity between May 2021 and May 2026, including transactions tied to Russian and Iranian sanctions evasion, North Korean money launderers, ransomware and scams. The ban takes effect 30 days after publication in the Federal Register (not yet published as of Nov 6), and MEXC said its trading, deposit and withdrawal services remain operational. The same day, OFAC designated eight Canadian money-services businesses accused of facilitating transfers for the Iranian regime, as part of "Operation Economic Outcast."
  • Why it matters: Cutting a top-20 global exchange off from U.S. banks is one of the largest financial-system actions ever taken against a crypto platform over scam and sanctions-evasion flows — it targets the off-ramp scammers use to cash out, not just the scammers themselves.
  • Source: https://financefeeds.com/mexc-faces-us-financial-ban-over-16-billion-cybercrime-links/

2. FTC reveals new details in Amazon ad-pricing lawsuit — charges "as much as 250%" above auction results

  • Outlet: FoodNavigator (reporting FTC disclosures); FTC + 22 states
  • Date: November 6, 2026 (article; newly unredacted complaint portions released Nov 5)
  • Summary: The FTC and 22 states disclosed new details in their lawsuit accusing Amazon of secretly inflating advertising prices, alleging Amazon raised Sponsored Ad rates by as much as 50% over auction results on ordinary days and up to 250% on major shopping days like Prime Day or Black Friday. The suit, filed August 31, 2026, alleges that for more than seven years Amazon manipulated its ad auctions — including by submitting fake second-place bids — and "has likely extracted tens of billions of dollars from unwitting advertising customers," with one large food-and-beverage advertiser allegedly overcharged by more than 100% during 2023 Prime Day. Amazon denies the accusations, says advertisers never pay more than their bid, and calls the lawsuit "misguided."
  • Why it matters: It is the FTC's biggest active deception case against a platform in the newsletter's coverage set, and it alleges the manipulation was deliberate and hidden — advertisers had no way to verify fair pricing. If proven, the overcharges flowed into higher prices for consumers on essentials like food and groceries.
  • Source: https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2026/11/06/amazon-ad-lawsuit-ftc-details-alleged-overcharges/

3. FTC and DOJ: fraud against older adults may have cost $119.5 billion in 2025

  • Outlet: Mondaq (summary of FTC/DOJ reports to Congress)
  • Date: November 6, 2026 (article date — see caveat)
  • Summary: The FTC told Congress that the overall cost of fraud targeting adults 60 and over could have been as high as $119.5 billion in 2025, with reported investment-scam losses in that age group up 42% to more than $1 billion. The Justice Department's parallel report said that between July 1, 2025 and June 30, 2026 it pursued almost 350 criminal and civil enforcement actions involving fraud targeting older adults, charging 712 defendants with stealing or allegedly stealing $4.84 billion from 711,353 victims; the FBI notified 10,143 victims, 77% of whom did not know they were being scammed, and FBI intervention prevented an estimated $646 million in losses.
  • Why it matters: It quantifies the scale of the problem this newsletter covers — older adults are the prime target of the AI-deepfake and impersonation scams in today's other stories — and shows most victims don't even realize they've been scammed until law enforcement tells them.
  • Source: https://alpha.mondaq.com/unitedstates/consumer-law/1865430/ftc-and-doj-report-on-older-adult-consumer-fraud
  • Date caveat: The underlying FTC report ("Protecting Older Consumers 2025–2026") is dated October 19, 2026; the Nov 6 date is the Mondaq summary, not the report itself. Do not present the $119.5B figure as a Nov 6 release.

4. Former FBI agent pleads guilty to stealing $2.47 million in crypto from 35 investigations

  • Outlet: U.S. Department of Justice (announcement Nov 5); reported Nov 6 by crypto.news and others
  • Date: November 5, 2026 (plea; reported Nov 6)
  • Summary: Former FBI counterintelligence supervisor Patrick Steven Yaroch, 37, of Ashburn, Virginia, pleaded guilty in federal court in Alexandria to wire fraud and money laundering after admitting he stole about $2,474,380.66 in cryptocurrency using wallet credentials collected during FBI investigations. Prosecutors say he made roughly 100 fraudulent transfers using credentials from 35 separate investigations, draining wallets belonging to investigation subjects, adversarial nation-state actors, crime victims, and at least one wallet the FBI itself created for an operation. He faces up to 20 years in prison; sentencing is set for March 4, 2027.
  • Why it matters: The theft hit the very wallets the FBI was investigating — including crime victims' funds — and the DOJ says it compromised investigations, sources and methods. It is a stark reminder that crypto-crime enforcement itself can be a target, and that "recovered" crypto is not always safe.
  • Source: https://crypto.news/former-fbi-agent-pleads-guilty-247m-crypto-theft/

5. Workers' comp hearing scams arrive by WhatsApp: fake judges demand payment to "release" benefits

  • Outlet: Milwaukee Journal Sentinel (Nov 6), via Hawks Quindel summary
  • Date: November 6, 2026 (verified via the law-firm summary quoting the Journal Sentinel story; the paywalled original was not opened)
  • Summary: Scammers are targeting Wisconsin residents — mainly Spanish-speaking injured workers — with fake workers' compensation hearings staged on WhatsApp, Zoom or Microsoft Teams, according to the Wisconsin Department of Workforce Development. Victims get an email from an official-sounding group (e.g. "Workers' Compensation Board," often from a .org address) inviting them to an online "hearing" where impostors play a judge, bailiff and attorneys, tell the worker they have won their case, and then demand money to release the benefits. Real hearings are scheduled only by written letter, and a real judge will never ask for payment to release benefits.
  • Why it matters: It is a fresh, platform-specific impersonation variant — the scam's credibility comes from the familiar WhatsApp/Zoom setting — and it targets a vulnerable group (injured, non-native-English-speaking workers) with a government-impersonation hook.
  • Source: https://www.wislawnow.com/2026/11/hawks-quindel-attorney-gabriel-manzano-quoted-in-milwaukee-journal-sentinel-on-workers-comp-hearing-scams/

6. Meta pauses planned ad-terms changes; Big Tech sues California over kids'-feed law

  • Outlet: Reuters (Meta terms); Malwarebytes / IT Security News (California suits)
  • Date: November 6, 2026 (Meta terms pause); California lawsuits filed November 4, 2026

2. WhatsApp "Vote for Me" account takeover spreading via friends' accounts

  • Date: November 5–7, 2026 (UK alerts roundup updated Nov 7; Instagram warning reel posted Nov 5, Facebook reel Nov 6)
  • How it works: A message arrives from a contact you know asking you to vote for someone — e.g., a girl called Eliška, or "Mia," a friend's daughter — in a dance competition, via a link to a site made to look like a ballet school in Prague (e.g., danceaward.cfd). To "vote," the site asks for your mobile number and then displays a code to enter in WhatsApp under Settings > Linked Devices. That code links your WhatsApp account to the scammer's computer, and the same message then goes out to all your contacts. It looks real because the sender is real — their account was taken over minutes earlier. The same trick appears as pet contests and school competitions.
  • Who it targets / how it works: This is a self-propagating takeover: each victim becomes the trusted sender for the next wave, defeating the usual "check the sender" advice. Social posts show it actively circulating this week, and the Linked Devices mechanism means victims hand over full account control without sharing a password.
  • Sources: https://yourlocalcomputerguy.co.uk/blog/uk-scam-alerts-november-2026.html ; https://www.instagram.com/reel/DfXQ-2oA80Y/

3. PlayStation Network account thefts: stolen accounts sold on Telegram and Instagram

4. Facebook Marketplace sellers hit by fake Venmo "payment on hold" scam — DEVELOPING

  • Date: Published/updated November 6–7, 2026; date of the underlying incident not independently verified — label as developing / single-source

RESEARCH DESK

Note: ScamPilot (CHI '26) was covered in the November 3 edition and is not repeated here.

1. "Love, Lies, and Language Models: Investigating AI's Role in Romance-Baiting Scams" (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Title: Love, Lies, and Language Models: Investigating AI's Role in Romance-Baiting Scams
  • Authors: Gilad Gressel, Rahul Pankajakshan, Shir Rozenfeld, Ling Li, Ivan Franceschini, Krishnashree Achuthan, Yisroel Mirsky
  • Venue / Date: USENIX Security Symposium 2026 — peer-reviewed; arXiv:2512.16280, submitted Dec 18, 2025; revised Apr 18, 2026
  • Key finding: In a week-long blinded experiment with 22 participants, an AI chatbot posing as a romantic interest got people to comply with its requests 46% of the time — versus only 18% for a human scammer — and participants actually trusted the bot more (p = 0.007). Interviews with 145 scam-operation insiders found 87% of scam labor is routine conversation that AI can already automate. Worst of all, commercial safety filters caught 0.0% of the romance-baiting dialogues tested. In short: AI is already better than humans at this scam, and today's filters are blind to it.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.16280

2. "A Large-Scale Study of Personalized Phishing using Large Language Models" (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Title: A Large-Scale Study of Personalized Phishing using Large Language Models
  • Authors: Stefan Czybik, Anne Josiane Kouam, Peter Heubl, Jan Magnus Nold, Konrad Rieck
  • Venue / Date: USENIX Security Symposium 2026 — peer-reviewed; August 2026
  • Key finding: In a real-world experiment with 7,700 participants, phishing emails personalized by an LLM — built from details scraped from the web using just the target's email address — almost tripled the click rate compared with generic phishing, whether the generic emails were written by humans or by AI. Personalization cost about $0.03 per email (roughly $150 to target 3,310 people). The takeaway: personalized spear-phishing is now cheap, automated, and dramatically more effective than the generic spam most defenses are tuned for.
  • Source: https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity26/presentation/czybik

3. "Hello, is this Anna?": Unpacking the Lifecycle of Pig-Butchering Scams (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Title: "Hello, is this Anna?": Unpacking the Lifecycle of Pig-Butchering Scams
  • Authors: Rajvardhan Oak, Zubair Shafiq (UC Davis)
  • Venue / Date: USENIX Symposium on Usable Privacy and Security (SOUPS) 2025 — peer-reviewed; arXiv:2503.20821, submitted Mar 25, 2025; revised May 24, 2025
  • Key finding: The first in-depth study built from interviews with 26 pig-butchering victims maps the scam as an organized, multi-stage lifecycle: staged trust-building over weeks, fake investment platforms showing fabricated profits, then repeated high-pressure demands for more money — followed by persistent re-engagement even after victims lose money. Victims also reported lasting psychological and financial harm, including becoming more vulnerable to follow-up scams. The authors propose concrete intervention points for social media and payment platforms, and argue the demeaning "pig-butchering" label itself discourages victims from reporting.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.20821

4. VeriSpoof: Beyond Real or Fake with One Audio LLM (PREPRINT)

  • Title: VeriSpoof: Beyond Real or Fake with One Audio LLM
  • Authors: Yang Xiao, Ting Dang
  • Venue / Date: arXiv preprint — NOT peer-reviewed (preprint); arXiv:2611.04634, submitted Nov 3, 2026
  • Key finding: A single audio AI model that doesn't just say "real or fake" — it also identifies which system generated a voice clone, whether two recordings came from the same source, and explains its reasoning in checkable statements. Across 14 test sets it hit a 2.14% pooled equal error rate, ranking 3rd of 28 systems tested and 1st among open-weight models. Its explanations also caught partially faked audio that one-step answers missed (99.6% vs. 78.4% detection). Relevant because voice-clone scams are surging, and this points toward detectors that can also serve as forensic evidence.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.04634

TODAY'S SAFETY TIP

Check your WhatsApp linked devices — and never type a code from a website into WhatsApp. Today's "Vote for Me" scam works because victims enter a code shown on a scam website into WhatsApp's Linked Devices screen, handing their whole account to a stranger — who then scams all their contacts. Take 30 seconds now: open WhatsApp → Settings → Linked Devices, and remove any device or browser you don't recognize. And make it a hard rule: no website, contest, or "vote" link ever gets a code from your messaging app. If a friend's account sends you a voting link, message them on a different channel first — their account may already be taken over.
