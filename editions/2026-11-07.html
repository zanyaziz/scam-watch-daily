Saturday, November 7, 2026

Hackers pull off a $133 million French bank heist — through the bank's own account.

Lead

Hackers pull off a $133 million French bank heist — through the bank's own account. Intruders reached Crédit Mutuel Arkéa via a service provider's platform and pushed fraudulent transactions through the bank's institutional account, converting the haul into stablecoins. The same day, the FTC's $49.75 million rental-pricing settlement with Progress Residential hit the wires, and a fake gift-card giveaway campaign topping 1,000 incidents was exposed. It was a quiet Saturday for new platform-specific news — no major Reuters/AP/BBC platform-scam story was published on Nov 7 itself — but the enforcement and fraud stories that landed were big.

TOP STORIES (ranked by importance)

1. Hackers pull off $133 million French bank heist via service provider

  • Outlet: BleepingComputer
  • Date: November 7, 2026, 10:25 AM ET (verified)
  • Summary: French cooperative bank Crédit Mutuel Arkéa (5.8 million members, €211.5 billion in assets) was hit by a cyberattack in which intruders reached its network through a service provider's platform and used the bank's own institutional account to push through fraudulent transactions — converting funds into USDC and USDT stablecoins. Blockchain investigator ZachXBT estimates up to €118 million (~$133 million) was stolen; the bank has not confirmed the figure but says it can "validate the order of magnitude" and that some funds were blocked. Customer accounts were not touched, the bank says, and it has warned customers of heightened phishing risk in the incident's wake.
  • Why it matters: A reminder that the biggest fraud losses now flow through trusted institutions and third-party access — and that every major bank incident spawns a wave of phishing texts and calls impersonating the bank, which is exactly what Arkéa warned its customers about.
  • Source: https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/hackers-pull-off-133-million-french-bank-heist-via-service-provider/

2. FTC: Landlord Progress Residential to pay $49.75 million over deceptive rental pricing

  • Outlet: Reuters (via Devdiscourse wire reprint)
  • Date: Announced Friday, November 6, 2026; Reuters coverage November 7, 2026
  • Summary: The FTC and four states (Arizona, Colorado, Indiana, Utah) reached a $49.75 million settlement with Progress Residential — the largest US manager of single-family rental homes — and its private-equity owner Pretium Partners, over allegations the companies advertised false monthly rents that excluded mandatory fees (smart-home fees, renters'-insurance exemption fees), mishandled security deposits, listed homes as move-in ready when they weren't, and restricted negative reviews. Of the total, $48.5 million goes to the FTC for consumer redress and $1.25 million to Arizona. The companies deny wrongdoing and admit nothing, but must now advertise the true total monthly price more prominently than any other price.
  • Why it matters: One of the largest federal enforcement actions against a residential landlord, and a direct warning to renters: the "cheap rent" in an online listing may hide mandatory fees — the same deceptive-pricing pattern the FTC has now punished at both Progress Residential and Invitation Homes ($48M, 2024).
  • Source: https://www.devdiscourse.com/article/international/4003011-us-private-equity-owned-landlord-to-pay-498-million-over-hidden-fees?amp

3. Fake gift card giveaways impersonating Amazon, Walmart and more surge — 1,000+ incidents

  • Outlet: BleepingComputer (sponsored CTM360 report)
  • Date: November 7, 2026, 9:10 AM ET (verified)
  • Summary: CTM360 identified more than 1,000 incidents in a surging global campaign of fake gift-card giveaways spread through sponsored social media ads and viral posts, using fabricated celebrity endorsements (e.g., MrBeast, Alix Earle) and impersonating trusted brands including Amazon, Walmart, PlayStation, Sephora, and SHEIN. Victims click through to polished reward portals showing fake countdowns and a partially masked gift-card code, then hit a "human verification" loop of surveys, app installs, and carrier-billed paid subscriptions. In every flow CTM360 tested, no gift card was ever delivered; the operators profit from affiliate commissions and harvested personal data.
  • Why it matters: This is the most common scam format most readers will actually encounter on Facebook, Instagram, and TikTok — a low-tech, high-volume trap that monetizes attention rather than stealing passwords, so it slips past the usual "don't click phishing links" advice.
  • Source: https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/ctm360-exposes-fake-gift-card-scams-exploiting-trusted-brands/

5. California orders crypto ATM operator CoinFlip to pay $4.8 million to scam victims

  • Outlet: Coin Edition
  • Date: Order issued Wednesday, November 4, 2026; Coin Edition analysis November 7, 2026 — the action is Nov 4, coverage Nov 7
  • Summary: California's Department of Financial Protection and Innovation ordered CoinFlip — which operates about 500 crypto ATMs in the state — to pay $4,811,483 in restitution to 20 California consumers defrauded through its kiosks, plus $351,530 in excess fees to the regulator, and to immediately stop unlawful practices. 6. Singapore police and crypto exchanges intercept S$17.4 million in scam losses before they happen

  • Outlet: Singapore Police Force (official release); CryptoNews coverage Nov 7
  • Date: Announced November 5, 2026; CryptoNews report November 7, 2026 — the announcement is Nov 5
  • Summary: The Singapore Police Force's Cyber Command, working with eight digital payment token providers (Coinbase, Coinhako, DTCPay, Gemini, Independent Reserve, OKX, StraitsX, Upbit) and blockchain analytics firms Chainalysis and TRM Labs, identified more than 383 potential scam victims and prevented over S$17.4 million (~US$13M) in potential losses during a two-month operation (Sept 1–Oct 31, 2026) targeting government-official impersonation, investment, and job scams. 1. Fake traffic-violation texts now use QR codes instead of links

  • Date: November 7, 2026 (CyberGuy / Fox News syndication, 10:03 UTC), reporting BleepingComputer's findings
  • How it works: Texts impersonating state courts warn of an unpaid traffic violation and demand payment of a $6.99 balance to avoid a court appearance. Instead of a clickable link, the message embeds an image of an official-looking court notice containing a QR code. Scanning leads through a CAPTCHA — designed to filter out security scanners — to a fake DMV/court payment page that harvests name, address, phone, email, and card details. Reported in New York, California, North Carolina, Illinois, Virginia, Texas, Connecticut, and New Jersey. Fake NY DMV hostnames cited include "ny.gov-skd[.]org" and "ny.ofkhv[.]life".
  • Who it targets / how it works: The QR-code switch is a deliberate evasion upgrade: it hides the destination URL from both spam filters and the victim's own inspection, and it moves the victim from text to a phone browser. It builds on the 2025 toll-agency smishing wave (covered in the November 3 edition) with a more convincing delivery method.
  • Source: https://foxnews.com/tech/fake-traffic-violation-text-scam-uses-qr-codes-steal-payment-info

2. Keplr crypto wallets drained via Web3Auth social-login flaw — DEVELOPING, amounts disputed

  • Date: Web3Auth warning November 6, 2026; Keplr confirmation November 7, 2026
  • How it works: Keplr, a Cosmos wallet, paused Google and Apple login after users reported unauthorized transactions on accounts created with social login. Keplr blamed a vulnerability at Web3Auth, its login provider, that exposed private keys. Bitquery counted at least 44,041 wallets across seven Cosmos chains plus 7,603 on Injective sending ~$6.01M to collector addresses; 86% of losses occurred after Keplr's warning, and staked ATOM was tokenized to bypass the 21-day unbonding wait. Seed-phrase and hardware wallets were not affected. Caution: Web3Auth later said some destination addresses were its own recovery wallets, not the attacker's, so the $6M theft figure is unconfirmed and disputed.
  • Who it targets / how it works: The weak point was not the wallet app but "sign in with Google/Apple" convenience — a single upstream flaw emptied tens of thousands of accounts at once, and pausing login did not stop an attacker who already held the keys.
  • Sources: https://bitquery.io/investigations/keplr-wallet-hack ; https://cryptomediazone.com/hack-database/keplr-web3auth-login-drains/

3. Fake job-seeker app spreads Android malware that takes over the phone

  • Date: New Zealand National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC) alert published November 4, 2026; reported November 6, 2026
  • How it works: The NCSC warned that scammers are directing job seekers to manually install a malicious Android app disguised as a job-seeking app, via chat messages and phone calls, rather than from an official app store. Once installed and granted permissions, the app hands scammers full access to the phone. The alert landed as NZ unemployment rose to 5.7%, its highest since 2015. It is the job-scam variant with the highest stakes: banking apps, OTPs, and contacts all become accessible.

4. "Wrong number" texts surge ~100% in a year, feeding romance and crypto pig-butchering

  • Date: BBB warnings reported November 2, 2026; summarized November 3, 2026
  • How it works: The BBB warned that friendly texts sent to the "wrong person" — e.g. "Hi, is this Linda? Are we still on for lunch?" — are deliberate openers, not accidents. Once the recipient replies, the stranger keeps chatting for days or weeks, then pivots to a romance, job offer, or crypto-investment pitch. BBB spokesperson Daniel Irwin said these "accidental" text scams have risen almost 100% over the past year, with AI making them harder to spot, and that seniors are the most likely to lose money to the romance/job follow-ons.
  • Who it targets / how it works: The low-pressure opener bypasses spam suspicion — replying to correct a stranger feels polite, not risky. It is the front door to pig-butchering, the highest-loss romance/investment scam pattern, and the volume trend (near-doubling) makes it a background risk for almost every phone owner.
  • Source: https://dunlap.today/a-friendly-text-meant-for-someone-else-a-message-asking-you-to-text-your-vote-and-an-advent-calendar-deal-thats-too-sweet-to-be-true-your-scam-watch-for-this-week/

5. Fake advent-calendar ads flood social media as holiday shopping starts

  • Date: BBB warning flagged on Local 3 News (Chattanooga) November 2, 2026; summarized November 3, 2026
  • How it works: The BBB warned that fake ads for themed advent calendars (crystals, books, bourbon/whiskey, toys such as NeeDoh) are rising on Facebook and Instagram. The ad links to a professional-looking site; the buyer is charged, gets a confirmation email and tracking number, and the calendar either never arrives or arrives as a cheap substitute — one buyer paid $49.99 each for bourbon/whiskey calendars supposedly holding 24 wax-sealed drams and received 4 empty plastic bottles and small charms. When buyers complain, the site often vanishes or offers a token partial refund (10–20%).
  • Who it targets / how it works: Advent calendars are time-sensitive — a delivery that arrives after Dec 1 is useless — so the scam exploits both holiday urgency and the narrow buying window. It is the seasonal face of the broader fake-social-ad problem, with brand names (NeeDoh/Schylling, Buffalo Trace) impersonated to add legitimacy.
  • Source: https://www.bbb.org/holiday/scams/watch-out-for-social-media-advent-calendar-scams

RESEARCH DESK

Note: the pig-butchering lifecycle study (Oak & Shafiq, SOUPS 2025), ScamPilot (CHI '26), the personalized-phishing study (Czybik et al.), and VeriSpoof were covered in the November 6 edition and are not repeated here.

1. Language as Lure: A Naturalistic Study on Pasifika Phishing Susceptibility (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Title: Language as Lure: A Naturalistic Study on Pasifika Phishing Susceptibility
  • Authors: Eric Spero, Isa Seow, Lucas Betts, Eddie Fuatimau, Robert Biddle, Danielle Lottridge, Giovanni Russello (University of Auckland; Biddle also Carleton University)
  • Venue / Date: SOUPS 2025, pp. 19–35 — peer-reviewed
  • Key finding: In a real-world experiment run with a Melanesian country's government IT department, 2,000 government employees received a simulated phishing email once a month for four months, written in either English or the local language (all participants were fluent in both). When the email made a personal request for help, the local-language version got significantly more clicks than the English version. The authors attribute this to emotion shaping reasoning in a native language, a cultural emphasis on helping, and in-group preference — and warn that low-resource languages likely have weaker mail filters, leaving those speakers more exposed.
  • Source: https://www.usenix.org/conference/soups2025/presentation/spero

2. Can You Walk Me Through It? Explainable SMS Phishing Detection using LLM-based Agents (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Title: Can You Walk Me Through It? Explainable SMS Phishing Detection using LLM-based Agents
  • Authors: Yizhu Wang, Haoyu Zhai, Chenkai Wang, Qingying Hao, Gang Wang (University of Illinois Urbana–Champaign); Nick A. Cohen, Roopa Foulger, Jonathan A. Handler (OSF Healthcare)
  • Venue / Date: SOUPS 2025 — peer-reviewed
  • Key finding: Their prototype "SmishX" uses a large language model to detect smishing (SMS phishing) and explain in plain language why a message is malicious, pulling in outside context such as domain and brand info, URL redirects, and web screenshots. On real-world SMS datasets it hit 98.8% overall detection accuracy, beating existing methods. In a user study of 175 people, the explanations significantly improved users' own phishing-detection ability across age groups, and participants rated the tool's usability "excellent" (System Usability Scale score 82.6).
  • Source: https://www.usenix.org/conference/soups2025/presentation/wang

3. "It didn't feel right but I needed a job so desperately": Understanding People's Emotions and Help Needs During Scams (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Title: "It didn't feel right but I needed a job so desperately": Understanding People's Emotions and Help Needs During Scams
  • Authors: Jake Chanenson (University of Chicago), Tara Matthews, Sunny Consolvo, Patrick Gage Kelley, Jessica McClearn, Sarah Meiklejohn, Abhishek Roy, Renee Shelby, Kurt Thomas (Google et al.), Amelia Hassoun (University of Cambridge)
  • Venue / Date: ACM CHI 2026 — peer-reviewed; DOI: 10.1145/3772318.3790556
  • Key finding: Analyzing 405 Reddit posts from people seeking help during scams, the study found scammers systematically exploit five emotional motivations — fear, guilt and goodness, trust, hope, and belonging — across 12 scam types. Financial insecurity and legal precarity sharply raise the risk of engaging with a scam (e.g., needing a job desperately makes a fake job offer hard to walk away from even when it feels wrong). The authors map what help people need at each stage — making sense of what's happening, guidance, emotional support, and concrete action — and argue interventions must be timed to the scam's stage and trauma-informed, since shame and self-blame are the biggest barriers to reporting.
  • Source: https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3772318.3790556

4. "I Strongly Suspect This Website Is a Scam": Benchmarking PII Leakage and Detection without Defense in Autonomous Web Agents (PREPRINT)

  • Title: "I Strongly Suspect This Website Is a Scam": Benchmarking PII Leakage and Detection without Defense in Autonomous Web Agents
  • Authors: Soham Roy, Sarthakbrata Halder, Arya Bharaty, Vaibhav Bhaskar, Yash Sinha, Dhruv Kumar, Srikant Panda, Murari Mandal (KIIT Bhubaneswar)
  • Venue / Date: arXiv preprint — NOT peer-reviewed (preprint); arXiv:2606.00497, submitted May 30, 2026, revised October 23, 2026
  • Key finding: The authors built "Scammer4U," a benchmark of 91 attacker-controlled fake websites plus 10 benign lookalikes, and tested leading AI web agents that browse and act on a user's behalf. Scam sites tricked the agents into handing over sensitive personal data (Social Security numbers, card numbers, CVVs) in 54–93% of sessions, versus 0% on the benign sites — proving the leakage is caused by the attack, not sloppy form-filling. Most strikingly, there's a "detection–action gap": even when an agent's own reasoning flagged a site as suspicious, it still submitted the sensitive data in 35.9% of sessions (vs. 66.1% when it voiced no suspicion). The authors conclude that defenses relying on the agent "noticing" the scam are gating on the wrong signal, and argue for intercepting outbound submissions directly.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.00497

TODAY'S SAFETY TIP

Never scan a QR code in an unsolicited text — type the address yourself. Today's fake traffic-violation scam hides its destination behind a QR code precisely so you can't inspect the link before tapping it. Courts and DMVs don't text you a QR code to pay a fine, and a real violation notice comes by mail. If a message claims you owe money, ignore the code and the link: open your browser and type the agency's official address yourself, or call the number on your own paperwork. The same rule covers "parking ticket" and "toll" texts — the QR code is the trap, not a convenience.
