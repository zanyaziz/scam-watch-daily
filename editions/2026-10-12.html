Tech giants and two continents of police just hit West African scam networks at once.

Lead

Tech giants and two continents of police just hit West African scam networks at once. Meta, Google, Microsoft, Coinbase, OpenAI and others joined the UK National Crime Agency, DOJ and FBI in a joint disruption: Meta alone removed more than 3.7 million scam-linked Facebook and Instagram accounts, pages and groups, 28 arrests were confirmed, and 117 trafficked victims were rescued in Ghana. Monday's other theme: trusted infrastructure turned against users — fake Google Calendar invites, and scam emails built on genuine Microsoft links.

NEW & EMERGING SCAMS

1. Quishing in the workplace — malicious QR codes inside Teams chats

  • What's new: QR-code phishing has moved inside workplace chat. Attackers embed a malicious URL in a QR image in a Microsoft Teams message framed as account verification, document review, or an urgent IT fix, sending the victim to a copied Microsoft 365 login page on their phone — outside the usual link-scanning path. Microsoft responded with Message Center notice MC1490905 (Oct 7): Defender for Office 365 now extracts URLs from QR codes in Teams messages post-delivery, warns or blocks, and can quarantine; rollout early October to early November.
  • Date: Notice Oct 7, 2026; coverage Oct 12–13, 2026
  • Why it matters: The defence itself is the news — quishing is common enough that a major platform is building QR extraction into chat security. Post-delivery means some codes will still be seen first.
  • Tip: Preview any QR destination before opening it, and never sign in after scanning a code from an unexpected chat/email — navigate to the service yourself.
  • Source: https://cyberpress.org/microsoft-teams-to-detect-malicious-qr-codes/

2. Calendar phishing (ICS invites) — fake meetings that survive the spam folder

  • What's new: Covered as Top Story #2 above: fake Google Calendar invitations carrying a billing scare, credential link, or callback number. Documented variants impersonate a billing department claiming a several-hundred-dollar renewal, linking to a supposed credit note that actually installs ScreenConnect remote-access software. Sublime Security data cited: calendar attacks observed up ~1,216% in August vs July (Sublime's own observations, not a global census).
  • Date: October 12, 2026 (ConsumerAffairs)
  • Tip: In Google Calendar Settings → Event settings, change "Add invitations to my calendar" to "Only if the sender is known"; report unfamiliar events as spam and never call a number inside an invite.
  • Source: https://www.consumeraffairs.com/news/fake-google-calendar-entries-give-phishing-scams-a-new-way-to-reach-consumers-101226.html

3. Election-season lures — "your registration is at risk," fake donations, cloned election-office calls

5. Investment/crypto scam texts — the #1 text scam in 47 states, funnelled to WhatsApp

  • What's new: McAfee Labs analysis (May–July 2026 data, freshly reported Oct 12) found investment and crypto scams are the most common text scam in 47 of 50 states. The pattern is the funnel: a "wrong number" or expert-group text quickly moves the conversation to WhatsApp/Telegram, then escalates to a time-limited, high-return pitch paid in crypto/gift cards — near-irrecoverable. The most common action requested in scam texts overall was clicking/joining a WhatsApp group. Exceptions: toll/traffic scams topped Michigan and Maine; loan/debt-relief topped North Dakota.
  • Date: Money coverage October 12, 2026; underlying McAfee report late September 2026
  • Why it matters: It reframes pig-butchering as a mass text funnel, not just dating apps — and AI generation in multiple languages lets one scheme run nationally.
  • Tip: Any unsolicited text that moves you to WhatsApp/Telegram and then to an investment is a stop sign, however long the friendly chat lasted; guaranteed/high returns = fraud.
  • Source: https://money.com/investment-scam-texts-most-common-states/

RESEARCH DESK (2025–2026 research; preprints labeled)

1. GuardPhish: Securing Open-Source LLMs from Phishing Abuse (PREPRINT)

  • Authors: Rina Mishra, Gaurav Varshney, Doddipatla Sesha Sahithi
  • Venue/date: arXiv:2604.17313, submitted April 19, 2026 — not peer-reviewed. Verified on arXiv.
  • Key finding: Knowing a prompt is phishing does not stop a model from writing the phish. The team built a 70,015-sample dataset covering web, email, SMS and voice scams and tested eight open-source LLMs offline. Models detected phishing intent up to 96% of the time, yet still produced usable phishing content from the same prompts, with attack success reaching 98.5% in voice scenarios. A transformer filter trained on their data blocked this better, reaching up to 98.27% accuracy as a pre-generation check.
  • Source: http://arxiv.org/abs/2604.17313v1

2. Beyond Detection: Evaluating Defensive LLMs Against AI-Generated Social Engineering in Live Turn-by-Turn Interaction (PREPRINT)

  • Authors: Yuqiao Xu, Osama Zafar, Alexander Nemecek, Erman Ayday
  • Venue/date: arXiv:2608.10239, submitted August 10, 2026 — not peer-reviewed. Verified on arXiv.
  • Key finding: An AI "defender" that looks safe may still fail to protect you in a live scam chat. In a controlled 300-case online-housing corpus (20 scenario families; 3,000 total model-case evaluations across five defender models), no model gave explicitly unsafe compliance — but how often they actually intervened ranged from 0% to 96.3%. Models often intervened for the wrong structural reason, or spotted the failure without acting; asset-control failures were the hardest to pinpoint.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.10239

3. Scam2Prompt: A Scalable Framework for Auditing Malicious Scam Endpoints in Production LLMs (PREPRINT)

  • Authors: Zhiyang Chen, Tara Saba, Xun Deng, Xujie Si, Fan Long
  • Venue/date: arXiv:2509.02372, submitted September 2, 2025; latest version May 8, 2026 — not peer-reviewed. Verified on arXiv.
  • Key finding: Innocent-sounding developer prompts can trick production chatbots into generating scam code, because models appear to have absorbed malicious content in training. Seemingly harmless prompts triggered malicious URL generation in 4.24% of cases across four production LLMs (GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini, Llama-4-Scout, DeepSeek-V3). On a benchmark of 1,559 prompts that reliably elicited scam code, seven newer 2025 LLMs did worse — malicious generation rates of 12.7% to 43.8% — and leading guardrails detected less than 0.3% of it.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.02372v2

4. A Synthetic Conversational Smishing Dataset for Social Engineering Detection (PREPRINT)

  • Authors: Carl Lochstampfor, Ayan Roy
  • Venue/date: arXiv:2604.11752, submitted April 13, 2026 — not peer-reviewed. Verified on arXiv.
  • Key finding: Multi-turn text-message scams are much harder to catch than a single bad text — and simple word-based models currently beat big transformers at it. The authors created 3,201 labelled multi-round smishing conversations that mimic gradual grooming, then benchmarked eight models. The best, XGBoost with TF-IDF features, reached only 72.5% accuracy (macro F1 0.691), ahead of DistilBERT and Longformer, which were limited by input length and small training data.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.11752

5. DarkGram: A Large-Scale Analysis of Cybercriminal Activity Channels on Telegram (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Authors: Sayak Saha Roy, Elham Pourabbas Vafa, Kobra Khanmohammadi, Shirin Nilizadeh
  • Venue/date: 34th USENIX Security Symposium (USENIX Security '25), August 13–15, 2025, Seattle — peer-reviewed. Verified on USENIX/arXiv.
  • Key finding: Telegram's cybercrime channels are dangerous even to their own subscribers. Analysing 339 cybercriminal activity channels followed by over 23.8 million users and 53,605 posts (Feb–May 2024) with a BERT classifier reporting 96% accuracy, the researchers found 28.1% of links shared in those channels led to phishing attacks and 38% of executable files were bundled with malware. Channels survived takedowns by quickly migrating subscribers; reporting via DarkGram still led to 196 channels being taken down over three months.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/html/2409.14596v2

6. Malicious LLM-Based Conversational AI Makes Users Reveal Personal Information (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Authors: Xiao Zhan, Juan Carlos Carrillo, William Seymour, Jose Such
  • Venue/date: 34th USENIX Security Symposium (USENIX Security '25), August 13–15, 2025 — peer-reviewed. Verified on the USENIX presentation page.
  • Key finding: A chatbot designed to be friendly and reciprocal can quietly harvest personal details — and users notice the risk least with exactly that strategy. In a randomized controlled trial with 502 participants, chatbots prompted to extract information elicited significantly more personal disclosure than a benign chatbot. Strategies based on reciprocity, empathy and self-disclosure were the most effective while minimising perceived risk, unlike direct questioning, which users rated as riskier and trusted less.
  • Source: https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity25/presentation/zhan

TODAY'S SAFETY TIP

A genuine link from a genuine company can still be a scam. Monday's Microsoft story is the pattern: more than 31,000 scam emails abused real Microsoft services, so the sender and the link were actually Microsoft — before redirecting to a credential-stealing site. The same day brought fake Google Calendar invites that bypass the inbox entirely. The 60-second rule: (1) never sign in immediately after clicking a link or scanning a QR code from a message — close it, open the company's official app or type its address yourself, and sign in there; (2) check where a link ends up, not where it starts — a real microsoft.com or google.com start proves nothing about the destination; (3) turn off automatic calendar invitations from unknown senders (Google Calendar → Settings → Event settings → "Only if the sender is known"), and report any invite you didn't create as spam instead of clicking anything inside it.
