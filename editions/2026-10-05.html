1. 'Godfather' Adam Iza sentenced to 78 months for $37M theft from Meta ad accounts

  • Outlet: justice.gov press release (U.S. Attorney's Office, Central District of California); same-day coverage by LA Times, CoinDesk (Oct 6)
  • Date: October 5, 2026 (DOJ release issued the same day; page says "sentenced today")

NEW & EMERGING SCAMS

1. "Milk Dragon" (NaiLong) phishing kit: FOMO-driven fake-discount marketplace listings

  • What's new: Instead of the classic fear/urgency playbook (fake fines, parcel problems, bank alerts), the kit's operators post big exclusive discounts on popular brands as ordinary social-media marketplace listings on Facebook and TikTok. Shoppers click through to fake WordPress/WooCommerce shops (custom "BytePress" plugin) that capture keystrokes character-by-character over a persistent WebSocket, then replay a spoofed 2FA/3D Secure page so the victim's one-time password approves a fraudulent transaction. Victim data is stored as reusable profiles (cards tagged by BIN/bank), and operators get a Telegram alert per victim.
  • Who flagged it: Group-IB threat research, reported by Help Net Security (Oct 5, 2026). Kit active since Oct 2025; linked to 258 phishing pages, 21 brands impersonated (LEGO, Calvin Klein, Aeon Malaysia), victims in 66 countries, fake-verification templates for 36 financial institutions.
  • Why it matters: Professionalized phishing-as-a-service built around FOMO rather than panic — victims "don't feel targeted" while scrolling on autopilot.
  • Source: https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2026/10/05/milk-dragon-phishing-fake-discounts/

2. Fake AI-trading ads hijacking Instagram accounts (I4C India warning)

  • What's new: Sponsored ads for AI/auto trading ("free access to the signal room," sample branded "Trade GPT") open an in-app sign-up form asking for name, email, phone. The form then claims a verification code was sent to the user's phone and asks for it — the code is actually Instagram's login verification code, which hands attackers the victim's account. Hijacked accounts get locked (email/phone/password changed), renamed and resold, or used to push scam ads and fake investment tips to followers.
  • Who flagged it: I4C/CyberDost advisory posted Oct 5, 2026; Meta cited ~16,000 such cases in one month.
  • Why it matters: An account-takeover pipeline driven by Meta's own ad-delivery infrastructure — ads as the spear, a login-code relay as the payload.
  • Source: https://ministryofcyberaffairs.com/news/government-of-india-warns-on-instagram-account-takeover-campaign-using-online-advertisements-c304e952-7d17-40b3-9aa0-93683d132f23

RESEARCH DESK (2025–2026 research; preprints labeled)

1. "Hey mum, I dropped my phone down the toilet": Investigating Hi Mum and Dad SMS Scams (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Title: "Hey mum, I dropped my phone down the toilet: Investigating Hi Mum and Dad SMS Scams in the United Kingdom"
  • Authors: Sharad Agarwal, Emma Harvey, Enrico Mariconti, Guillermo Suarez-Tangil, Marie Vasek (UCL, Stop Scams UK, IMDEA)
  • Venue/date: 34th USENIX Security Symposium (USENIX Security 25), Aug 2025, Seattle, WA — peer-reviewed
  • Key finding: The first empirical study of the "hi mum and dad" SMS scam — fraudsters posing as the victim's child asking for urgent financial help. Working with a UK mobile operator, the researchers honey-potted 711 scammer interactions over three months, harvesting 582 unique mule (money-laundering) accounts that asked them to pay more than £577,000. Unlike classic one-message smishing with a malicious link, these scammers interact conversationally and rely mostly on kindness, distraction, and urgency to win trust. (Author-reported results; no independent replication cited.)
  • Source: https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity25/presentation/agarwal-sharad

2. Preventing Artificially Inflated SMS Attacks through Large-Scale Traffic Inspection (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Title: "Preventing Artificially Inflated SMS Attacks through Large-Scale Traffic Inspection"
  • Authors: Jun Ho Huh, Hyejin Shin, Sunwoo Ahn, Hayoon Yi (Samsung Research); Joonho Cho, Taewoo Kim, Minchae Lim, Nuel Choi (Samsung Electronics)
  • Venue/date: USENIX Security 25, Aug 2025 — peer-reviewed
  • Key finding: "Artificially inflated traffic" (AIT) attacks — bots firing off huge volumes of fake SMS verification requests — quietly bleed businesses that pay per SMS, and this is the first paper to formally characterize them. Analyzing 9.4 million SMS request logs, the authors found attackers use short-lived email services and recycle common prefixes to mass-generate fake phone and device IDs, submitting a few requests per account before switching. Their three-level detection system reached 89.6% recall at a 0.2% false-positive rate on web traffic and 91.1% recall at 0.1% false-positive rate on native app traffic. Practical relevance: inflated SMS bills businesses write off as cost are often fraud, not user error. (Author-reported evaluations.)
  • Source: https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity25/presentation/huh

3. "Hello, is this Anna?": Unpacking the Lifecycle of Pig-Butchering Scams (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Title: ""Hello, is this Anna?": Unpacking the Lifecycle of Pig-Butchering Scams"
  • Authors: Rajvardhan Oak, Zubair Shafiq (University of California, Davis)
  • Venue/date: 21st USENIX Symposium on Usable Privacy and Security (SOUPS 2025), Aug 10–12, 2025, Seattle, WA — peer-reviewed
  • Key finding: The first qualitative deep-dive into pig-butchering (long-con romance/investment fraud) based on in-depth interviews with 26 actual victims. The researchers map an organized lifecycle: staged trust-building, fraudulent investment platforms, fabricated returns, repeated high-pressure re-engagement — plus a secondary-victimization effect where drained victims become targets for follow-up scams. Recommendations include intervention points for social-media and financial platforms and non-stigmatizing language so victims report instead of hiding. (Caution: N=26 interviews — phase frequencies aren't population estimates.)
  • Source: https://www.usenix.org/conference/soups2025/accepted-papers

4. Can You Walk Me Through It? Explainable SMS Phishing Detection using LLM-based Agents (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Title: "Can You Walk Me Through It? Explainable SMS Phishing Detection using LLM-based Agents"
  • Authors: Yizhu Wang, Haoyu Zhai, Chenkai Wang, Qingying Hao, Gang Wang (UIUC); Nick A. Cohen, Roopa Foulger, Jonathan A. Handler (OSF Healthcare)
  • Venue/date: SOUPS 2025, Aug 10–12, 2025 — peer-reviewed
  • Key finding: SmishX treats an LLM like a junior fraud analyst: for a short suspicious SMS, it gathers external evidence (domain/brand data, URL redirect chains, web screenshots) and reasons over it with chain-of-thought before answering — then explains the verdict in plain language. On real-world SMS datasets it hit 98.8% overall accuracy, beating existing methods, and in user studies with 175 participants the explanations measurably improved users' own phishing detection across age groups, earning an "excellent" usability rating (SUS score 82.6). Open problems: resolving human-AI disagreements and safely handling AI mistakes. (Author-reported results.)
  • Source: https://www.usenix.org/conference/soups2025/accepted-papers

5. Language as Lure: A Naturalistic Study on Pasifika Phishing Susceptibility (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Title: "Language as Lure: A Naturalistic Study on Pasifika Phishing Susceptibility"
  • Authors: Eric Spero, Isa Seow, Lucas Betts, Eddie Fuatimau (University of Auckland); Robert Biddle (University of Auckland / Carleton University); Danielle Lottridge, Giovanni Russello (University of Auckland)
  • Venue/date: SOUPS 2025, Aug 10–12, 2025 — peer-reviewed
  • Key finding: In an ecologically valid experiment, the team partnered with a Pacific nation's government IT department to send 2,000 employees a simulated phishing email each month for four months — each in either English or the employee's local language (all were competent in both). Phishing emails containing personal requests for assistance drew more clicks in the local language than in English — likely a mix of emotion overriding reasoning in a native tongue, a cultural emphasis on helping, and in-group preference. The authors warn that low-resource languages get weaker mail-filter protection, making exactly these communities more exposed. (Single-country study; numbers describe what was observed.)
  • Source: https://www.usenix.org/conference/soups2025/accepted-papers

6. Can Large Language Models Improve Phishing Defense? A Large-Scale Controlled Experiment on Warning Dialogue Explanations (PREPRINT — not peer-reviewed)

  • Title: "Can Large Language Models Improve Phishing Defense? A Large-Scale Controlled Experiment on Warning Dialogue Explanations"
  • Authors: Federico Maria Cau, Giuseppe Desolda, Francesco Greco, Lucio Davide Spano, Luca Viganò
  • Venue/date: arXiv:2507.07916v1 [cs.CR] (submitted 10 Jul 2025) — preprint
  • Key finding: A large between-subjects study (N = 750) compared browser warning dialogues with human-written explanations against ones auto-generated by Claude 3.5 Sonnet and Llama 3.3 70B, testing two explanation styles (feature-based vs. counterfactual). LLM-generated explanations matched or beat human-written ones at reducing susceptibility; Claude-generated warnings were particularly strong. Feature-based explanations worked best against real phishing, while counterfactual explanations were better at cutting false positives. Workload, gender, and prior familiarity with warnings all moderated effectiveness — a reminder that one-size-fits-all warnings leave gaps. (Caution: lab study of simulated warnings, not field deployment data.)
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.07916v1

Note: five of six items this round are peer-reviewed (2× USENIX Security 25, 3× SOUPS 2025); one is an arXiv preprint. All numbers come from the verified abstract pages; author-reported evaluations are flagged where relevant.

TODAY'S SAFETY TIP

A verification code belongs to the app that sent it — never type it into a different form. Today's biggest real-world scam mechanic is the Instagram login-code relay: fake AI-trading ads tell you to enter a "verification code" into an in-app sign-up form, and that code is actually Instagram's own login code — the moment you submit it, the scammers own your account. The 30-second rule: when any message asks you to enter a code, (1) check which app or service actually sent the code — your phone's SMS thread shows the sender's name; (2) only enter it on that service's official app or site; (3) if a form on Instagram/Facebook/Telegram/WhatsApp asks you for a code that arrived "from your phone," it's not verifying you — it's logging in as you. A code that arrives unrequested, or gets requested by a different app than the one that sent it, is a red flag every time.
