NEW & EMERGING SCAMS

1. AI robocall + NFC card-relay bank scam — police say the method was "not encountered before" (reported Oct 26)

  • What's new: Reported October 26, 2026 by Security Affairs, citing Dutch (Rotterdam) police. Police monitored the operation for about three weeks from mid-September; two suspects (ages 23 and 25) were arrested October 6 and ordered held at least another 90 days. Alleged losses: about €380,000 from 170 victims in three weeks.
  • How it works: An AI-generated recorded call warns of suspicious bank activity and says to press 1 if you don't recognise it. A live fake "bank employee" then pressures the victim to install an app and hold their bank card against their phone "to secure" the account. The app reads the card's NFC chip and relays the data to an accomplice waiting at a cash machine or payment terminal, who withdraws thousands.
  • Who's targeted: Bank customers across the Netherlands — explicitly not only seniors; police said dozens of victims were in their 20s and 30s.
  • Tip: If a caller ever asks you to install an app or tap/scan your bank card to your phone, hang up immediately and call your bank on the number printed on the card itself.
  • Source: https://securityaffairs.com/201786/cyber-crime/ai-generated-fake-bank-calls-and-nfc-app-enabled-e380000-fraud-dutch-police-say.html

2. Deepfake CEO voice — senior managers most likely to comply before checking (research published Oct 26)

  • What's new: KnowBe4 / Censuswide research published October 26, 2026 (Security Brief Asia): in a survey of 500 Singapore office workers, 35% of senior managers said they would comply with a cloned CEO voice before verifying — more than double clerical workers (16%) — even though 78% of senior managers believed they could spot a fake. Only 26% had received training on executive-impersonation AI scams.
  • How it works: A cloned voice or convincing message from "the boss" exploits hierarchy: staff fear delaying or questioning a senior leader and approve a payment, disclose credentials, or bypass checks.
  • Who's targeted: Employees, especially senior managers and finance staff who can authorise payments.
  • Tip: Put a mandatory callback rule between any executive request and any money movement — verify on a known number or second channel, no exceptions for urgency, and make it socially safe to question the boss.
  • Source: https://securitybrief.asia/story/singapore-senior-managers-most-likely-to-trust-deepfake-ceo

3. Microsoft Teams to support third-party deepfake detection in meetings from mid-November (reported Oct 26)

  • What's new: Reported October 26, 2026: Microsoft is opening Teams to certified third-party tools that scan meeting audio and video for deepfakes, with rollout scheduled from mid-November 2026. Teams itself will not analyse media; providers report a confidence score and risk level, and Teams surfaces warning pop-ups. Separately, Teams already gained QR-code URL checks this month (Microsoft Defender for Office 365, rollout early October to early November) that extract URLs hidden in QR codes in messages and warn or block when malicious.
  • How it works (the threat): Real-time deepfake video/voice in meetings is used to impersonate executives or officials and instruct payments or disclosure — the platform is now adding a detection layer because human spotting is unreliable.
  • Who's targeted: Organisations using Teams, especially finance and executive teams.
  • Tip: Treat any in-meeting payment instruction as unverified until confirmed out-of-band, even after detection tools roll out — a "no warning" is not proof a participant is real.
  • Sources: https://pbxscience.com/microsoft-teams-to-support-third-party-deepfake-detection-in-meetings-from-mid-november/ ; https://cyberpress.org/microsoft-teams-to-detect-malicious-qr-codes/

4. Fake FTC agents threaten to deactivate your phone number (FTC alert Oct 22)

  • What's new: FTC consumer alert released October 22, 2026, reported the same day by Popular Science and still circulating in the October 26 cycle.
  • How it works: Callers claiming to be FTC officials say criminals in another country registered a phone in your name/number. They offer fake badge numbers, "transfer" you to a fake police officer to file a report, and may direct you to "verify" via a web search that leads back to the scammers. They harvest ID and personal information for the bogus report, demand secrecy, threaten loss of your phone number — then claim you'll be arrested unless you send money for a "security deposit."
  • Who's targeted: General consumers, cold-called; people anxious about legal trouble or losing phone service.
  • Tip: Hang up. The FTC says its staff will never ask you to send money, threaten to disconnect your phone, demand secrecy, or text a photo of an employee ID. Verify independently at ftc.gov and report at ReportFraud.ftc.gov — never via a number or search the caller gives you.
  • Source: https://www.popsci.com/technology/ftc-phone-number-scam/

5. Quishing: fake QR stickers on parking meters, and QR codes in mystery packages (roundup Oct 23; October incidents)

  • What's new: A Fordham University information-security roundup published October 23, 2026 summarised FTC/FBI warnings and an October 2026 incident in Ann Arbor, Michigan, where police found fake QR stickers on downtown meters near the University of Michigan ("Scan to Park!" / "Park AA"); city staff inspected meters and removed the stickers. A Malwarebytes/Stacker report (October 22) cited Palo Alto Networks' Unit 42 detecting more than 11,000 malicious QR codes per day.
  • How it works: (a) A sticker QR placed over a legitimate meter code sends drivers to a fake payment site that steals payment/personal information. (b) "Brushing" variant: an unordered package arrives with a QR code to "identify the sender" — scanning leads to a phishing site or malware.
  • Who's targeted: Drivers paying for parking, students/campus users, and anyone who receives an unexpected package.
  • Tip: Check for a peelable sticker over an original code, preview the URL before opening it, and never enter payment or login details from a scanned code — pay via the official parking app or type the organisation's address yourself. Don't scan codes from packages you didn't order.
  • Sources: https://itsecurity.blog.fordham.edu/2026/10/23/qr-code-scams-why-you-should-think-before-you-scan/ ; http://randolphmn.com/premium/stacker/stories/scammers-are-finding-new-ways-to-target-your-phone-here-are-5-ways-to-protect-yourself,178483

RESEARCH DESK (2025–2026 research; peer-reviewed vs preprint labelled; all six are new to this newsletter)

1. Practising against a talking, multimodal scammer helps — but only a little (PREPRINT)

  • Authors / venue / date: Xiangzhe Yuan, Jiajun Wang, Huanchen Wang, Qian Wan, Siying Hu — arXiv:2601.06774, submitted January 11, 2026. Not peer-reviewed.
  • Key finding, in plain language: "ImmuniFraug" puts students inside LLM-driven scam simulations — text, voice and a visual avatar — across ten prevalent fraud types, with debriefs grounded in protection motivation theory. In a controlled study with 846 Chinese undergraduates, the interactive intervention significantly improved fraud awareness versus official text-based materials (p = 0.026), with high narrative immersion (M = 56.95/77) — even after controlling for participants' extensive prior anti-fraud education. The effect is real but modest (outside commentary notes Cohen's d = 0.151): simulation adds an increment on top of information, not a cure.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.06774

2. Voice-deepfake detectors that score ~100% in the lab can fall to chance on a new voice, language or generator (PREPRINT)

  • Authors / venue / date: Ioan-Paul Ciobanu, Andrei-Iulian Hiji, Nicolae-Catalin Ristea, Paul Irofti, Cristian Rusu, Radu Tudor Ionescu — arXiv:2506.00462, submitted May 31, 2025; revised January 18, 2026. Not peer-reviewed.
  • Key finding, in plain language: Most audio-deepfake detectors are tested "in-domain" — fake voices in training and test come from the same generators — and report accuracy close to 99–100%. XMAD-Bench removes that crutch: 668.8 hours of real and deepfake speech in which speakers, generative methods and real-audio sources all differ across training and test splits. Cross-domain performance of the same models is sometimes similar to random chance. Do not stake a wire transfer on a detector score earned on voices from the same generator that made the training fakes.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.00462

3. You can spot a malicious QR code from its pixels alone — without ever opening the link (PREPRINT)

  • Authors / venue / date: Fouad Trad, Ali Chehab — arXiv:2505.03451, submitted May 6, 2025; revised April 18, 2026. Not peer-reviewed.
  • Key finding, in plain language: Normal quishing detection decodes the code and checks the URL — which means touching the payload, and misses codes encoding Wi-Fi credentials or payment details instead of links. This paper proposes judging the code itself: the structure and pixel patterns of the QR image, with no content extraction. The best model (XGBoost) reached an AUC of 0.9106, improving slightly to 0.9133 after pruning non-informative pixels. Malicious codes carry structural fingerprints detectable before anyone visits the destination.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.03451

4. Teach the AI to think like the scammer — and the people most at risk trust it least (PREPRINT)

  • Authors / venue / date: Xue Wen Tan, Kenneth See, Stanley Kok — arXiv:2507.17543, submitted July 23, 2025; revised July 25, 2025. Not peer-reviewed.
  • Key finding, in plain language: Instead of only flagging a finished scam message, the ASR framework predicts what the scammer will say next, simulates the conversation, and explains the reasoning in real time inside messaging apps. Its engine, ScamGPT-J, is fine-tuned on real scam conversations: its simulated replies matched real scammer responses far better than the base GPT-J model (mean similarity 0.433 vs. 0.329; it beat GPT-J in 80 of 90 validation conversations), and it mimicked scammers convincingly in 14 of 16 scam conversations versus 3 of 19 for GPT-J, per the paper's evaluation tables. It substantially improved users' scam detection — but the authors flag a paradox: the users most vulnerable to scams were often the least receptive to AI assistance.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.17543

5. In real state scam-tracker data, crypto's worst losses come through a "person," not a platform (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Authors / venue / date: Brandon Dulisse, Chivon Fitch, Jaden Denis, Nathan Connealy (University of Tampa) — Journal of Economic Criminology, Vol. 9, article 100178, September 2025. Peer-reviewed. DOI: 10.1016/j.jeconc.2025.100178
  • Key finding, in plain language: Using data from two U.S. state scam trackers, the authors compare crypto scams with other online financial scams. The most prevalent crypto scam types are fraudulent trading platforms and pig-butchering scams; victims are predominantly male and are most often first contacted via social media or online messaging platforms. Scammers borrow legitimacy from titles — public figures, financial experts, known acquaintances — and scams initiated through personal connections, particularly romantic or familial identities, lead to the largest financial losses.
  • Source: https://doaj.org/article/19b9c4eef9914a75a142553175bbb381

6. In India, romance fraud increasingly leaves the app — 25 of 35 cases moved to a physical meeting (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Authors / venue / date: Shankey Verma, P. Gujarathi, Suleman Lazarus — Journal of Economic Criminology, Vol. 14, article 100248, published online September 13, 2026. Peer-reviewed. DOI: 10.1016/j.jeconc.2026.100248
  • Key finding, in plain language: A qualitative study of 35 media-reported Indian dating-app fraud cases from 2020 to 2025 found the fraud frequently does not stay online: 25 of the 35 cases showed an identifiable move to a physical meeting, where financial extraction, coercion or — in extreme cases — violence followed. The other 10 stayed mostly online as extortion, sextortion or fake-investment schemes. Reported losses ranged from ₹7,000 to ₹9 crore. Tinder was named in 8 cases and Bumble in 7. The authors are explicit about limits: English-language news reports may over-represent severe cases, and the study cannot estimate prevalence.
  • Source: https://www.datingindustryinsights.com/news/study-35-indian-romance-fraud-cases-tracks-move-offline

Never install an app, tap your bank card to your phone, or share your screen because a caller, "bank employee," "executive," or Marketplace buyer asked you to.
