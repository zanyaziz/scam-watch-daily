DOJ extradites alleged Cambodia pig-butchering money launderer in $36.9 million social-media crypto scam.

Lead

DOJ extradites alleged Cambodia pig-butchering money launderer in $36.9 million social-media crypto scam. On Friday, the Justice Department announced the arraignment in California of Zheng Zhao, 44, a Chinese national extradited from the Netherlands, accused of laundering proceeds from Cambodia-based scam centres that contacted 174 identified U.S. victims through social media, dating apps, phone calls and texts, steered them to fake crypto trading sites, and moved the money through U.S. shell companies to a Bahamas bank account and on to Tether wallets in Cambodia. The same day, coverage continued of Rhode Island's first-of-its-kind state lawsuit against Meta over scam ads, and new FTC data showed seniors reported about $3 billion in fraud losses in 2025 — with investment scams, often starting on social media, topping $1 billion.

TOP STORIES (ranked by importance)

1. DOJ: Chinese national extradited from Netherlands in $36.9M Cambodia crypto scam that reached victims via social media and dating apps

  • Outlet: U.S. Department of Justice announcement, reported by UPI / SecurityOnline / Crypto Briefing
  • Date: October 23, 2026 — verified Oct 23
  • Summary: The DOJ announced on Oct 23 the arraignment in the Central District of California of Zheng Zhao, 44, a Chinese national extradited from the Netherlands, on a conspiracy to commit money laundering charge (maximum 20 years; he is presumed innocent). Prosecutors allege scam centres in Cambodia contacted 174 identified U.S. victims via social media, dating apps, phone calls and texts, steered them to fake crypto trading websites showing fabricated gains, then routed more than $36.9 million through U.S. shell companies to a single Deltec Bank account in the Bahamas, converted it to Tether (USDT) and sent it to wallets in Cambodia. Eight co-conspirators have already pleaded guilty, including ShengSheng He (51 months) and Jingliang Su (46 months).
  • Why it matters: A textbook pig-butchering pipeline — social/dating contact → fake trading platform → stablecoin laundering — now paired with an extradition showing overseas operators can be brought into U.S. court. Assistant Attorney General A. Tysen Duva said: "This extradition should be a lesson to all those who seek to exploit vulnerable Americans from overseas."
  • Source: https://securityonline.info/cambodia-crypto-scam-extradition-doj/ ; UPI syndication: https://www.europesays.com/3328385/

2. Rhode Island sues Meta over alleged tolerance for scam advertising (CONTINUING — filed Oct 22, fresh coverage Oct 23)

  • Outlet: Reuters; PYMNTS coverage October 23, 2026
  • Date: Suit filed Thursday, October 22, 2026; Reuters article Oct 22, PYMNTS coverage Oct 23 — labelled continuing story (it led yesterday's edition; new Oct 23 analysis and Meta's response below)
  • Summary: Rhode Island Attorney General Peter F. Neronha sued Meta in Providence County Superior Court under the state's Deceptive Trade Practices Act, alleging Meta unlawfully profited from and participated in creating scam ads on Facebook, Instagram and WhatsApp even as it assured users it was fighting fraud. The complaint cites internal Meta documents reported by Reuters indicating about 15 billion "higher risk" scam ads shown daily and roughly 10% of Meta's 2024 revenue — about $16 billion — from fraudulent e-commerce, dubious health products and other banned marketing. It alleges Meta's AI ad tools helped optimise scam ads, that suspected fraudsters were sometimes charged higher "penalty" rates instead of being blocked, that more than 2,000 versions of an Alzheimer's "reverse in 45 days" ad were active, and that ads impersonating a senior Bank of America executive lured users into WhatsApp stock-trading scams.
  • Why it matters: The first scam-ads case brought against Meta by a U.S. state, following Santa Clara County's May 2026 suit. It seeks civil penalties per violation, restitution, disgorgement and a court order requiring Meta to address scam ads. Meta spokesperson Andy Stone called the suit "unfounded" and said Meta partners with law enforcement and fights scams daily.
  • Sources: https://www.reuters.com/world/rhode-island-sues-meta-over-alleged-tolerance-scam-advertising-2026-10-22/ ; https://www.pymnts.com/legal/2026/rhode-island-lawsuit-claims-meta-knowingly-profits-from-financial-scam-ads/

3. FTC data in Oct 23 coverage: seniors' reported fraud losses hit ~$3 billion in 2025; investment scams over $1 billion, often starting on social media

  • Outlet: FTC "Protecting Older Consumers 2025–2026" report to Congress (released Oct 19), reported by PYMNTS Oct 22–23 and Retirement Living (updated Oct 23)
  • Date: Report October 19, 2026; coverage October 22–23, 2026 — labelled
  • Summary: Adults 60+ reported about $3 billion in fraud losses in 2025, roughly five times the ~$600 million reported in 2020. Investment scams accounted for more than $1 billion, up 42% in one year, driven largely by losses over $100,000 to investment, romance and impersonation scams, with victims frequently reporting first contact on social media and losses on fake crypto platforms. The FTC estimates true losses, accounting for under-reporting, could be $13.3 billion to $119.5 billion. Median loss for those 80+ who lost money was $2,000; older adults were more likely than younger adults to report losses to tech-support, romance, lottery and government-impersonation scams.
  • Why it matters: The victim-side numbers behind the Rhode Island suit's focus on seniors and the Zhao pig-butchering case. It also undercuts a stereotype: seniors report losing money at lower rates than younger adults — but lose far more when they do.
  • Sources: https://www.pymnts.com/cybersecurity/fraud-prevention/2026/ftc-data-shows-investment-scams-fuel-fivefold-surge-in-seniors-fraud-losses/ ; https://www.retirementliving.com/fraud-losses-among-older-americans-climb-to-3-billion-ftc-reports

5. EU opens Digital Services Act investigation into Booking.com over fake listings and phishing scams (LATE-BREAKING — Oct 21)

  • Date: October 21, 2026 — labelled late-breaking
  • Summary: The European Commission said it will investigate whether Booking.com is doing enough under the EU Digital Services Act to weed out fraudulent listings and phishing scams, after a preliminary probe and review of the company's risk-assessment reports. 6. Fake Amazon Music × YouTube deal lures creators into a Google-login trap (Oct 21, still active)

  • Outlet: Malwarebytes Threat Intel
  • Date: October 21, 2026 — labelled; campaign still active this week
  • Summary: Malwarebytes found a wave of sites at amazonyt[.]com posing as an invite-only "Amazon Artists Music — Creator Portal," exploiting the real August 27 YouTube–Amazon Shopping Affiliate announcement. The site pulls a creator's real channel stats via YouTube's public API to build a personalised dashboard and payout calculator, then asks the creator to "verify" the channel. Clicking "Continue with Google" loads a fake sign-in from youtubechannelverification[.]com inside a full-screen frame while the address bar still shows the lure domain; the second stage collects email, password and MFA codes. Victims who comply are returned to an "unlocked" fake offer, so they wait instead of reacting.
  • Why it matters: Hijacked YouTube channels carry a trusted audience and have historically been resold or used to broadcast crypto scams. The personalisation plus in-page fake login defeats the usual "check the URL" advice unless creators confirm the real browser tab is accounts.google.com.
  • Source: https://www.malwarebytes.com/blog/threat-intel/2026/10/fake-amazon-music-x-youtube-deal-lures-creators-into-a-google-login-trap

NEW & EMERGING SCAMS

1. FTC impersonators threaten to deactivate your phone number — with a fake police transfer and a rigged "verification" search

  • What's new: A caller claiming to be an FTC official says a phone was registered under your name and number and is being used to commit scams in another country. They read out a fake badge ID and threaten to deactivate your number. They then "transfer" you to a fake police officer to file a report — or, if you hesitate, tell you exactly what to search online to find the police number, so your own "verification" call goes straight back to the scam crew. The fake officer harvests ID and personal information for the "report," insists on secrecy, and later escalates: you are supposedly under arrest unless you send money for a "security deposit."
  • Who's targeted: General consumers reached by phone.
  • Date/source: FTC consumer alert, October 22, 2026; CNET and trade coverage Oct 22–23 — verified on the FTC's own page.
  • Tip: A real FTC employee will never ask for money, threaten to disconnect your phone, demand secrecy, or text a photo of an employee ID. Hang up and report at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.
  • Source: https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2026/10/ftc-not-calling-deactivate-your-phone-number

2. AI deepfake voice and video calls move from celebrities to ordinary people — INTERPOL's "secret word" campaign

  • What's new: With only a small amount of audio/video pulled from the internet, criminals clone a relative's voice or a colleague's face for a live phone or video call — a frantic family "emergency," a co-worker asking for a bank transfer, or a fast-moving online romance. INTERPOL says the old tells (bad grammar, clumsy formatting) are disappearing, so a familiar voice or face no longer proves identity. Its recommended defence: a shared family "secret word," hanging up and calling back on a verified number, and 2FA. Malwarebytes (Oct 22) adds scale, citing CrowdStrike's 2026 Threat Hunting Report: voice phishing ("vishing") doubled in the first half of 2026, and 16% of adults in Malwarebytes' survey said they had received a voice-cloned call impersonating someone they knew in the past year.
  • Who's targeted: Families ("relative in trouble" calls), employees who can authorise transfers, dating-platform users.
  • Date/source: INTERPOL campaign announced in Singapore Oct 18, 2026; coverage Oct 19–20, with fresh summaries still publishing Oct 23. Campaign slogan: "Wait. Validate. Don't take the bait."
  • Sources: https://cybernews.com/ai-news/interpol-deepfake-scam-campaign/ ; https://www.malwarebytes.com/blog/scams/2026/10/scammers-are-finding-new-ways-to-target-your-phone-here-are-5-ways-to-protect-yourself

3. AI-generated celebrity and politician voices now sell unauthorised investment and crypto sites — Italy blocks five more

  • What's new: Italy's regulator CONSOB ordered five more websites blocked on Oct 23 (three unauthorised investment services — RLMAsstStrategy, Ava Invest, GMTrade — and two unauthorised crypto services, "Brelliozo IA" and IncomeMax), bringing its total blocked since July 2019 to 1,853. The newer ingredient it flagged: AI-generated images, voices and videos, cloned websites of authorised firms, and fake profiles of politicians and well-known people used to push investors into harmful decisions. Because operators simply move to new domains, CONSOB's practical advice is to check the firm in its authorised-intermediaries register (or ESMA's MiCA register for crypto) before any transfer.
  • Who's targeted: Retail investors and crypto buyers in Italy/EU, reached via ads, emails, cloned sites and social profiles.
  • Date/source: CONSOB press release October 23, 2026, reported Oct 23–24 — date verified in reporting citing the release directly.
  • Source: https://www.trade2spike.com/ai-in-action/consob-blocks-five-more-investment-scam-sites-ai-generated-voices-videos-1853-since-2019

4. "Claim hijacking" — fake unemployment specialists and AI phone calls harvest IDs to take over real benefit claims

  • What's new: People claiming to be unemployment specialists contact individuals, say they have unclaimed benefits available, and offer large payments — in the pattern first flagged in an August 2026 U.S. UI Integrity Center advisory, an AI-driven call operation told North Dakota residents they had $4,000 in unclaimed UI benefits. The call model could hold a conversation, answer generic questions and keep redirecting the person to hand over personal information; it reportedly spoke rapidly, operated at all hours, and used an in-state area code. Stolen details are then used to steal identities and hijack a legitimate benefit claim, or file for benefits in someone else's name. California's EDD amplified the warning on Oct 23, stressing it never asks for bank or login information by phone, email or text.
  • Who's targeted: Unemployment, disability and paid-family-leave claimants — notably, per the August advisory, people whose real accounts have already passed identity verification.
  • Date/source: California EDD warning published October 23, 2026; underlying pattern from August 2026 advisory — labelled.
  • Sources: https://www.einpresswire.com/article/952017625/fall-for-the-season-not-a-scam ; https://files.constantcontact.com/32962321501/82e4267c-827d-452a-a7c7-1e7584fa4fe5.pdf?rdr=true

5. "Quishing" surge — fake QR stickers on parking meters, QR codes in mystery packages, forged bank letters

  • What's new: A QR code hides its destination, so scammers put fake codes where people already expect to pay or log in. Recent cases: fake QR stickers placed over legitimate ones on parking meters — in Ann Arbor, Michigan in October 2026, stickers reading "Scan to Park!" near the University of Michigan sent drivers to fraudulent payment sites (the FTC warned about the parking-meter variant in September 2026); and the "brushing" variant — an unsolicited package of cheap items arrives with a QR code to "identify the sender," leading to a phishing site or malware (FTC warning, August 2026). A related European variant reported Oct 24 — forged bank letters with QR codes in Bamberg, Germany, followed by a fake bank-security call, two victims losing over €120,000 combined — is developing (single aggregator source).
  • Who's targeted: Drivers paying for parking, campus users, online shoppers receiving mystery packages, bank customers receiving mailed letters.
  • Date/source: Fordham University Information Security explainer published October 23, 2026, pulling together FTC/FBI warnings; CSU Channel Islands warning Oct 21, 2026.
  • Tip: Check whether a public QR code is a peelable sticker over the original, preview the URL before opening, and go to the organisation's official app/site directly instead.
  • Sources: https://itsecurity.blog.fordham.edu/2026/10/23/qr-code-scams-why-you-should-think-before-you-scan/ ; https://www.csuci.edu/news/releases/20261020-qr-code-phishing.html

6. Midterm-election scams — AI-cloned candidates, fake voter-registration pages, Scam PACs and "campaign investment pools"

  • What's new: With the November midterms close, scammers are borrowing candidate names, images, logos and slogans — increasingly with AI-generated voices and messages impersonating candidates, campaigns and government entities — to run several schemes at once: fake campaign contributions; "Scam PACs" that pocket donations; political merchandise never delivered; a "campaign investment pool" promising a guaranteed return after the candidate wins (the FBI stresses donations are not an investment); and texts/emails claiming you are not registered to vote, linking to a fake registration page built to steal personal information.
  • Who's targeted: U.S. voters and political donors nationwide.
  • Date/source: FBI Public Service Announcement I-101526-PSA, October 15, 2026; coverage Oct 16 and still circulating — labelled, timely rather than new on Oct 23.
  • Tip: Verify registration and donations only via vote.gov, your state election office, or the FEC — never via a link you were sent.
  • Sources: https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2026/10/16/usa-2026-midterm-election-scams/ ; https://www.lagovistatexas.gov/DocumentCenter/View/14243/Scammers-Exploit-2026-US-Midterm-Election-to-Perpetrate-Multiple-Fraud-Schemes---10152026PDF

7. Task scams that pay you first — small real payouts, then a crypto deposit to "unlock" earnings (DEVELOPING sourcing; pattern is FTC-established)

  • What's new: An unsolicited text, WhatsApp or Telegram message offers easy paid work — liking videos, rating products, "optimising" listings — in an app that shows commissions climbing. Crucially, the scam pays a small real amount first (the FTC pattern cited in Oct 23 explainers is usually $5–$20), so the next step feels earned. Then you must deposit your own money, usually in crypto, to "recharge," unlock the next task set, or withdraw. The in-app balance is fictional, and each withdrawal attempt produces a new fee or "combo task" demanding a larger deposit.
  • Who's targeted: Job-seekers and side-income seekers recruited via unsolicited messages who never applied for the "job."
  • Date/source: Fresh explainers Oct 21–23, 2026 synthesising the FTC's standing task-scam guidance — single-source explainers labelled developing; the underlying FTC description is well established.
  • Tip: Never pay anyone to get paid, or to get a job.
  • Sources: https://www.sheftali.net/2026/10/task-scam-why-payout-comes-first.html ; https://blog2.remoteshift.net/2026/10/online-jobs-that-pay-daily.html

Also spotted: a permit-fee email scam using applicants' real case numbers (fresh local alerts Oct 21, Marion/Acushnet MA; FBI first warned March 2026) — https://southcoast360.com/marion-acushnet-warn-scam-emails-permit-applicants-oct-21-2026.html ; and fake VA benefits texts (VA guidance dated Oct 23, 2026; secondary sourcing — developing) — https://truenews.bagleyenterprises.net/stories/390 . RESEARCH DESK (2025–2026 research; peer-reviewed vs preprint labelled; all six are new to this newsletter)

1. The Tip of the Iceberg: Revealing the Full Impact of Crypto Pig Butchering (PEER-REVIEWED — journal article)

  • Authors / venue / date: Damilola Akinleye, Carter Casey, Masooda Bashir, Micah Sherr — Journal of Cybersecurity (Oxford Academic), Volume 12, Issue 1, 2026, advance access Nov 18, 2025 (DOI 10.1093/cybsec/tyaf026).
  • Key finding, in plain language: Instead of relying only on victim reports, the team traced on-chain fund flows from 238 victim reports (2022–2025) and, using a heuristics-based tracing framework, expanded them into 5,369 victims, 11,246 addresses and 5,910 transactions moving $1,069,446,350 (about $1.07 billion) — suggesting reported losses are only the "tip of the iceberg" versus real on-chain flows. They also map pig-butchering typologies and the infrastructure that coordinates them.
  • Source: https://academic.oup.com/jcsec/article/12/1/tyaf026/8326860

2. Phishing in Wonderland: AI-accelerated Detection Dependency Cycle (PREPRINT — not peer-reviewed)

  • Authors / date: Samuel T. Hunter and Thalia Laing — arXiv:2602.06663, submitted Feb 6, 2026.
  • Key finding, in plain language: Using the "cyber kill chain," the authors argue that generative AI lowers attacker cost at early stages (recon, lure crafting) while defenders answer with more AI detection — creating a dependency cycle that can raise systemic risk instead of simply improving safety. They explicitly label it a theoretical framework grounded in qualitative analysis and prior detection literature, a hypothesis-generating discussion paper — not an experiment.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06663
