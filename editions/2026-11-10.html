TOP STORIES (ranked by importance)

1. Tokyo court dismisses billionaire's lawsuit against Meta over celebrity impersonation scam ads

  • Outlet: TV Asahi News / TBS News / Kyodo News (Japanese-language reports)
  • Date: November 10, 2026
  • Summary: The Tokyo District Court dismissed Japanese billionaire Yusaku Maezawa's lawsuit against Meta and its Japanese unit over fraudulent investment ads that used his name and likeness without permission. Maezawa, founder of Zozotown, had sought 1 yen in damages and an injunction against the ads. The court acknowledged the fake ads infringed Maezawa's publicity and portrait rights, but ruled Meta "is merely a platform operator and is not directly involved in the posting of the ads," noting Meta removed most flagged ads within two days. Meta said it views the ruling positively.
  • Why it matters: This is the highest-profile test yet of whether a platform can be held liable for scam ads it runs, and it went Meta's way — just weeks after a Yokohama court dismissed a similar victim suit on October 15. It sets a discouraging precedent for the wave of Japanese fraud victims suing Meta over celebrity-endorsement investment scams, even as Rhode Island sued Meta on October 22 on the same underlying issue.
  • Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_uUlYyjkcsQ (TV Asahi report, Nov 10, 2026); corroborated by TBS: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=r10ShcVN-Ys and Kyodo: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-6QVp_ZkPu0

2. FTC halts "phantom debt" collector that took more than $20 million

  • Outlet: Federal Trade Commission (official press release)
  • Date: November 9, 2026 (announced; widely covered November 10, 2026)
  • Summary: At the FTC's request, a federal court issued a temporary restraining order halting McDonnell & Associates LLC (also doing business as First Capital Group, Maxwell & Bryant Group, and Lewis, McDonnell & Associates) and froze its assets under a court-appointed receiver. The FTC alleges the Orange County, California operation falsely claimed to be a law firm or affiliated with attorneys and used high-pressure threats — lawsuits, wage garnishment, property liens, calls to employers and family — to coerce payments on debts consumers frequently did not owe, had already paid, or that were past the statute of limitations or discharged in bankruptcy. Consumers paid more than $20 million in total. The order was issued November 5, 2026; a preliminary-injunction hearing is set for November 16 in Santa Ana.
  • Why it matters: "Phantom debt" is one of the most reported phone-based fraud tactics, and this is a rare full shutdown with asset freeze rather than a fine. It also shows the FTC still actively using its enforcement powers in late 2026, with the complaint alleging violations of the FTC Act, the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act, and the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act.
  • Source: https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/11/ftc-action-results-court-issuing-temporary-restraining-order-halting-phantom-debt-collection

3. 110-domain fake YouTube copyright-strike phishing ring disrupted

  • Outlet: ZeroFox (security research blog)
  • Date: November 10, 2026
  • Summary: ZeroFox's Disruption and Signals Labs identified and helped take down an estate of 110 domain names running a single phishing kit that impersonated YouTube copyright enforcement. Creators received fake copyright-strike or DMCA takedown notices linking to a page titled "YouTube Copyright Strikes — Channel Status Check," which used a browser-in-the-browser attack — a fake Google sign-in window with a spoofed accounts.google.com address bar — to harvest the creator's Google email, password, session cookie, and second factor. The end goal is hijacking channels to repurpose them for cryptocurrency-scam livestreams. Of the 110 domains, 82 have been suspended by registrars/registries and none of the remaining 28 still serves the lure; the last one stopped working in early November.
  • Why it matters: This is a textbook example of how platform trust is weaponized against creators: a copyright strike is one of the few messages a YouTuber cannot ignore, and a hijacked channel with real subscribers is prime real estate for crypto-scam livestreams. The browser-in-the-browser technique also defeats the usual "check the URL bar" advice.
  • Source: https://www.zerofox.com/blog/how-we-disrupted-a-110-domain-fake-copyright-strike-phishing-estate/

NEW & EMERGING SCAMS

1. Holiday delivery smishing surge — fake USPS/FedEx "package held" texts

  • Date: Reported November 9–10, 2026
  • How it works: A seasonal wave of fake package-delivery texts (smishing) impersonating USPS, FedEx, UPS and DHL, spiking as holiday shipping ramps up. An unexpected text claims a package is held, undeliverable, or needs a small postage/redelivery fee, with a link to a look-alike carrier site that harvests card numbers, addresses, and sometimes a code that lets the scammer add the victim's card to their own phone. USPS only texts if you signed up for tracking on a specific number — and those messages never contain a link.
  • Who it targets: Anyone expecting parcels — i.e., nearly everyone in November–December.
  • Why it's new: Fake delivery alerts were the single most-reported text scam category in 2024, per the FTC; consumers lost an estimated $470 million to all text scams in 2024 — more than 5× the 2020 figure. International Fraud Awareness Week runs Nov 15–21, 2026.
  • Source: https://icameraplus.com/blog/is-that-delivery-failed-text-a-scam-2026-holiday-guide (Nov 9, 2026); https://www.gmork.com/scams/usps-package-redelivery-text-scam (updated Nov 10, 2026)

2. Quishing — QR-code phishing surges past email filters

  • Date: Fresh guides published November 9–10, 2026; Q1 2026 data
  • How it works: Phishing delivered via QR code ("quishing") — fake codes stuck over real ones on parking meters, menus and posters, or embedded in emails/PDFs that dodge link-based email filters. Scanning takes the victim to a spoofed login or payment page (or a malware download) on their phone, which typically lacks corporate web filtering. Parking meters are the classic target: a fake "scan to pay" sticker collects card details and plate numbers, sometimes signing victims up for a recurring "subscription."
  • Who it targets: Drivers and office workers; reporting through 2026 shows executives and senior staff falling for quishing at disproportionately high rates.
  • Why it's new: Quishing surged 146% in Q1 2026 (National Law Review); Mimecast counted 655,673 unique malicious QR codes in Q4 2025 and 1.7M+ between Oct 2024–Mar 2025, with an average 2.7M QR-carrying emails per day. Yesterday's edition covered the QR-code traffic-violation smishing wave; this is the wider quishing picture.
  • Source: https://www.fraudprotect.ai/blog/en/qr-code-phishing-quishing.html (updated Nov 10, 2026); https://www.techgeek.com.au/qr-code-scams-quishing-how-it-works-australia/ (Nov 9, 2026)

3. Fake Facebook Marketplace listing uses YOUR name to lure a reply

  • Date: Malwarebytes, October 6 & 13, 2026
  • How it works: A personalized "conversation lure": an unsolicited text (often iMessage) asks "Is this still available for purchase?" and attaches a fabricated Marketplace listing showing the recipient's own name as the seller. The scammer pairs a breached name+phone-number combo with a photo lifted from another Facebook user with the same name, making the fake listing look plausible. Replying — even "That's not me" — confirms the number is live and valuable, inviting follow-on fraud, account-takeover attempts, or malware links. It does not by itself mean the victim's Facebook account was hacked.
  • Who it targets: Anyone whose name and phone number appear in breached data.
  • Why it's new: The scam lives in the reply, not in a fake post — and Malwarebytes notes AI agents could generate such personalized lures at scale, though AI use in the examined example was not established.
  • Source: https://www.malwarebytes.com/blog/threat-intel/2026/10/facebook-marketplace-phish-uses-your-name-and-number (Oct 6, 2026); https://www.malwarebytes.com/blog/threat-intel/2026/10/your-whos-this-reply-is-exactly-what-text-scammers-want (Oct 13, 2026)

5. Telegram "prepaid task" scam — likes and ratings escalate to crypto-deposit extortion — DEVELOPING

  • Date: November 3, 2026 (single-author Medium account; the victim case study is anecdotal and unverified, though the pattern matches FTC warnings)
  • How it works: A task/job scam run over WhatsApp and Telegram: victims are recruited to "like" YouTube videos, rate hotels, or subscribe to channels for easy daily pay, then pushed into "prepaid merchant tasks" requiring deposits. Small real payouts (e.g., ₹350) build trust; a VIP Telegram group of 200+ members (mostly bots/syndicate actors) posts fake earnings screenshots. Victims then "recharge" merchant accounts for crypto-liquidity ranking — small deposits return a profit, then withdrawals are blocked ("credit score low") until ever-larger "GST clearance" and "risk reserve" payments are made.
  • Who it targets: Job seekers, students, and people looking for side income, especially in India/UAE/Pakistan.
  • Why it's new: One reported victim (a 28-year-old Hyderabad software engineer) lost ₹720,000 (~$8,100) after escalating deposits of ₹1,000 → ₹10,000 → ₹50,000 → ₹150,000 → ₹200,000. FTC: task-scam reports jumped from ~5,000 in all of 2023 to ~20,000 in H1 2024; job-scam losses topped $220M in H1 2024.
  • Source: https://medium.com/@drivesyncinfotechpvtltd/the-telegram-rating-trap-inside-the-multi-million-dollar-prepaid-task-scam-e9e67287e71d (Nov 3, 2026)

6. Instagram ad → hijacked WhatsApp: the "DarkSword" exploit chain — DEVELOPING

  • Date: November 2, 2026 (single-victim forensic write-up on Medium; the DarkSword kit and iVerify reporting are corroborated, but the specific infection chain is the author's analysis)
  • How it works: The victim tapped an Instagram ad that opened a survey page in Instagram's in-app browser; the page exploited known iOS vulnerabilities to plant memory-only code that exfiltrated ~17 MB of data. Hours later, scam messages (e-commerce impersonation, "do tasks, earn money" links to Telegram) went out from the victim's WhatsApp to 30+ groups, apparently using a copied WhatsApp identity. The exploit kit is "DarkSword," reported by Google and iVerify in 2026 and later circulating publicly; all vulnerabilities used had been patched months earlier.
  • Who it targets: iPhone users on outdated iOS — iVerify/Report URI reported in October 2026 that this kit family was being spread through ads at scale, without picking targets.
  • Why it's new: No SIM swap, no shared verification code, no new device added — a reminder that an outdated phone can be compromised by simply tapping an ad.
  • Source: https://medium.com/@semiihozsoy/from-the-instagram-feed-to-a-hijacked-whatsapp-account-63985e1bf1ce (Nov 2, 2026)

RESEARCH DESK

1. Restricting the Link: Effects of Focused Attention and Time Delay on Phishing Warning Effectiveness (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Title: Restricting the Link: Effects of Focused Attention and Time Delay on Phishing Warning Effectiveness
  • Authors: Justin Petelka (University of Washington), Benjamin Berens (Karlsruhe Institute of Technology), Carlo Sugatan (University of Michigan), Melanie Volkamer (KIT), Florian Schaub (University of Michigan)
  • Venue / Date: Peer-reviewed — 2025 IEEE Symposium on Security and Privacy (IEEE S&P); DOI: 10.1109/SP61157.2025.00007
  • Key finding: The study tested two ways of slowing people down before they click a suspicious link: "focused attention" (you can only proceed by clicking the link's real destination inside the warning, forcing you to look at it) and a "time delay" (the link is frozen for a few seconds). In an online experiment with 1,320 participants judging emails in a simulated inbox, both tricks independently cut click-through rates, but focused attention worked better than a time delay, and combining the two cut clicks further still. Trade-off: the same friction also slowed people on legitimate emails the warning had wrongly flagged, and users found time delays more annoying than focused attention when they believed the warning was a false alarm. The authors suggest matching the strength of the friction to the risk level of the link.
  • Source: https://jpetelka.github.io/files/ieee_2025_phishing.pdf

2. Does Phishing Training Work? A Large-Scale Empirical Assessment of Multi-Modal Training Grounded in the NIST Phish Scale (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Title: Does Phishing Training Work? A Large-Scale Empirical Assessment of Multi-Modal Training Grounded in the NIST Phish Scale
  • Authors: Andrew T. Rozema and James C. Davis (Purdue University)
  • Venue / Date: Peer-reviewed — USENIX Symposium on Usable Privacy and Security (SOUPS) 2025, August 10–12, 2025, Seattle
  • Key finding: A real-world test at a US fintech firm with ~4,000 employees compared no training, traditional lecture-style training, and lecture training plus an interactive phishing exercise, then sent simulated phishing emails of varying difficulty (rated on the NIST Phish Scale). Interactive training did not dramatically stop people clicking, but it changed what they did next: the interactive group reported phishing attempts 37% more often than the untrained baseline and 25% more often than the lecture-only group. The authors' verdict is measured — interactive training helps, but the effect is modest, and lure difficulty (how convincing the phish is) matters more than the training format for click rates.
  • Source: https://roze.ma/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/Rozema_SOUPS_25__Phish_Scale-.pdf

3. Fake it till you make it: the psychological and communication tactics behind "Pig Butchering" scams (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Title: Fake it till you make it: the psychological and communication tactics behind "Pig Butchering" scams
  • Authors: Ayşe Nur Asyalı, Muriel-Larissa Frank, Pol Hölzmer
  • Venue / Date: Peer-reviewed — Journal of Cybersecurity (Oxford University Press), Vol. 12, No. 1, 2026, article tyag003; DOI: 10.1093/cybsec/tyag003
  • Key finding: Rather than interviewing victims, the researchers analysed actual scam manuals — the playbooks that train pig-butchering scammers (romance + fake-investment fraud). They found scammers systematically work through a staged playbook that exploits relationship-building, trust, commitment, and victims' desire for self-improvement, and they built a unified stage model mapping the psychological and communication tricks used at each stage. The practical takeaway: because the manipulation is staged and deliberate — not opportunistic — prevention can be targeted at specific stages (e.g., the early trust-building phase) rather than relying on generic "don't invest with strangers" warnings.
  • Source: https://doi.org/10.1093/cybsec/tyag003

4. Judging Phishing under Uncertainty: How Do Users Handle Inaccurate Automated Advice? (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Title: Judging Phishing under Uncertainty: How Do Users Handle Inaccurate Automated Advice?
  • Authors: Tarini Saka, Kalliopi Vakali (University of Edinburgh), Adam D. G. Jenkins (King's College London), Nadin Kokciyan (University of Edinburgh), Kami Vaniea (University of Waterloo)
  • Venue / Date: Peer-reviewed — CHI 2025, April 26–May 1, 2025, Yokohama, Japan; DOI: 10.1145/3706598.3714267
  • Key finding: In an online experiment with 489 participants, people who got advice tailored to the specific email in front of them spotted phishing significantly better than people who got generic "how to spot phishing" guidance. But there's a catch that matters as AI email assistants spread: when the tailored advice contained occasional errors, detection accuracy and user confidence both dropped — though even imperfect tailored advice still beat no guidance at all. The warning for tool-builders: an AI phishing helper that is sometimes wrong can actively mislead users, so showing uncertainty and the reasoning behind a verdict matters.
  • Source: https://www.pure.ed.ac.uk/ws/portalfiles/portal/576093090/SakaEtalCHI25JudgingPhishingUnderUncertainty.pdf

5. ScamAgents: How AI Agents Can Simulate Human-Level Scam Calls (PEER-REVIEWED WORKSHOP PAPER)

  • Title: ScamAgents: How AI Agents Can Simulate Human-Level Scam Calls
  • Author: Sanket Badhe (Rutgers University)
  • Venue / Date: Peer-reviewed workshop paper — Conference on Applied Machine Learning for Information Security (CAMLIS) 2025; August 2025, arXiv:2508.06457
  • Key finding: The researcher built "ScamAgent," an autonomous AI system that runs full multi-turn scam phone calls — it remembers the conversation, adapts when the "victim" pushes back, and uses deception tactics across many turns, then turns the script into lifelike speech with text-to-speech. Tested across real-world scenarios (medical-insurance verification, impersonation, prize/lottery fraud, government-benefit enrolment), it showed that today's LLM safety guardrails — built to catch a single bad prompt — fail against this kind of attack, because the harmful intent is split into small, innocent-looking steps spread over a whole conversation. The paper argues safety checks need to work across entire conversations, not one message at a time. (Note: this is a demonstration/red-teaming study; the abstract reports high deception success qualitatively rather than a single headline percentage, so no specific rate is quoted here.)
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/html/2508.06457v2/

6. SoK: Large Language Model-Generated Textual Phishing Campaigns (PREPRINT)

  • Title: SoK: Large Language Model-Generated Textual Phishing Campaigns
  • Authors: Chen, Wu, Nguyen & Rudolph
  • Venue / Date: Preprint — arXiv:2508.21457, submitted Aug 29, 2025, v4 Aug 29, 2026 (not peer-reviewed)
  • Key finding: A systematization of LLM-generated phishing with a Generation-Characterization-Defense framework — mapping how large language models can generate, tailor, and scale phishing campaigns, and where defenses currently fall short. No single effect-size numbers are reported; included as a preprint survey of the field, not as peer-reviewed evidence.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.21457

TODAY'S SAFETY TIP

A copyright-strike notice is one of the few messages you can't ignore — which is exactly why scammers fake it. Real YouTube copyright notices arrive by email from YouTube and appear inside YouTube Studio; they never ask you to sign in through a pop-up window or a "channel status check" page. If you get a strike or DMCA warning, don't click the link in the message — open YouTube Studio yourself and check your channel status there. The same rule covers the day's other big theme: a celebrity-endorsed investment ad on Facebook or Instagram is almost always fake, no matter whose face is on it — real billionaires don't sell crypto schemes in sponsored posts. And if a delivery text about a package you weren't tracking asks you to tap a link or scan a QR code, delete it: carriers never text you a payment link out of the blue.
