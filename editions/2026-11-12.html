TOP STORIES (ranked by importance)

1. FBI disrupts North Korean "FaceStealer" scam that stole $400M+ via fake Zoom and Teams calls — and DOJ moves to seize $1.2B in Binance loot

  • Outlet: Reuters
  • Date: November 12, 2026
  • Summary: The FBI and U.S. Department of Justice announced on November 12 that they dismantled cloud infrastructure behind "FaceStealer," a North Korean social-engineering campaign that lured victims into video calls on imposter versions of Zoom, Microsoft Teams and Google Meet, using surreptitiously recorded webcam footage — and increasingly AI deepfakes — of people victims knew to gain trust and steal cryptocurrency. The scheme stole more than $400 million over 18 months, and the FBI seized more than 900 victim webcam videos used to impersonate them in later attacks. In the same announcement, the DOJ said it obtained court authorization to seize or restrain over $1.2 billion stolen in last month's $6.3 billion Binance heist. The FBI attributes FaceStealer to the North Korean cluster "Sapphire Sleet" (UNC1069), a Lazarus Group offshoot.
  • Why it matters: This is the biggest single scam-takedown story of the day — it shows state-backed actors industrializing deepfake video-call impersonation, and it directly implicates the trust users place in Zoom, Teams and Google Meet. The seizure of 900 victim videos also means anyone who has been on a video call with a stranger could have their likeness reused.
  • Source: https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/north-korean-hackers-steal-more-than-400-million-using-video-images-fbi-says-2026-11-12/
  • Corroboration: The Register, Nov 12, 2026: https://www.theregister.com/cyber-crime/2026/11/12/doj-takes-aim-at-12b-in-binance-loot-as-fbi-disrupts-north-korean-scam/5309523

2. FTC weighs making Big Tech platforms liable for impersonation scam ads

  • Outlet: Payments Dive
  • Date: November 12, 2026 (published 10:58 AM)
  • Summary: The Federal Trade Commission is taking public comments this month on updating its rule against impersonating government and business officials to make large technology companies share responsibility for policing scam ads on their platforms. The FTC said in its October 1 proposal that consumers lost $16 billion to fraud last year, including about $3.5 billion in imposter schemes reported by more than one million people, and it named Google, Facebook and Apple sites as examples of platforms through which the scams take place. The agency argues platforms "profit from optimizing online ads for third parties, regardless of whether the third parties are legitimate," and has received nearly 200 comments so far, mostly from consumers, ahead of a deadline at the end of November.
  • Why it matters: This would be a major shift in who bears the cost of scam ads — from banks and victims to the platforms (Google, Meta, Apple) that profit from the ads. It lands the day after the Wall Street Journal investigation covered in yesterday's edition found Meta accounted for nearly half of all scam complaints tied to Zelle transactions at JPMorgan Chase — and it could reshape how scam ads are policed across search and social media.
  • Source: https://www.paymentsdive.com/news/ftc-aims-to-better-fight-scams/874509/

3. Global Microsoft 365 / Outlook outage — and the scam wave experts expect to follow it

  • Outlet: Reuters
  • Date: November 12, 2026 (18:32 UTC)
  • Summary: Microsoft 365, Outlook and Teams went down for thousands of U.S. users on Thursday, with Downdetector recording more than 130,000 Outlook problem reports at 1:27 p.m. ET and over 46,000 for Microsoft 365. The outage — which Microsoft blamed on server problems and said showed no sign of malicious activity — ran into a second day and hit millions worldwide, including banks, traders, Australia's spy agency and federal police, and European exchange operator Euronext. Security experts warned the real danger comes after the fix: after past outages, researchers observed scam campaigns springing up within hours, using fake "verify / restore your Microsoft account" emails and texts linking to convincing copies of the Microsoft login page — made worse because Microsoft Defender for Office 365, the tool many companies rely on to catch phishing, was itself on the affected-services list.
  • Why it matters: A mass outage primes millions of people to expect account emails from Microsoft, which is exactly the opening phishing scammers exploit. Readers should know Microsoft never calls or emails unsolicited to fix an account, and its error messages never include a phone number.
  • Source: https://www.reuters.com/technology/microsoft-365-outlook-down-thousands-us-users-downdetector-shows-2026-11-12/

4. TikTok joins the Global Signal Exchange to share scam intelligence across platforms

  • Outlet: Global Signal Exchange (op-ed by CEO Emily Taylor, carried by East Midlands Connected)
  • Date: November 12, 2026 (published 20:03 UTC)
  • Summary: TikTok announced it is taking first steps to join the Global Signal Exchange (GSE) — the cross-industry infrastructure for sharing fraud signals — with the announcement made this week in Bangkok, alongside banks, tech platforms and government agencies already in the exchange. The piece notes recent cross-platform wins: Microsoft took down RedVDS, a criminal marketplace linked to more than $66 million in reported U.S. fraud losses, then used the GSE to alert Google when its operators moved to Google infrastructure, leading to a 97% reduction in RedVDS's footprint; Google intelligence also helped Microsoft shut down a fake helpdesk campaign impersonating Microsoft's own brand.
  • Why it matters: Scammers hop platforms faster than any one company can follow; TikTok — one of the largest remaining holdouts — joining the shared scam-signal network closes a major gap and shows the industry moving from solo takedowns to coordinated defense.
  • Source: https://eastmidconnected.co.uk/scammers-don-t-stay-on-one-platform-neither-can-the-people-stopping-them-an-opinion-piece-by-emily-taylor-ceo-of-the-global-signal-exchange/

5. Canada disrupts nearly 11,000 cybercrime operations in nationwide "Maple Disruption 2.0" crackdown

  • Outlet: ImmuniWeb Security Blog, Issue #198 (summarizing RCMP / Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre)
  • Date: November 12, 2026 (digest date); campaign ran November 2–6, 2026
  • Summary: Canadian law enforcement and more than 50 private-sector organizations carried out 10,987 disruptive actions against infrastructure tied to cybercrime and online fraud during the nationwide Maple Disruption 2.0 campaign, led by the RCMP's National Cybercrime Coordination Centre and the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre. NEW & EMERGING SCAMS

1. FATF: AI has industrialized the global scam trade

  • Date: FATF report published November 5, 2026; widely covered November 12, 2026
  • How it works: The Financial Action Task Force's report "Combating End-to-End Illicit Financial Flows Linked to Scam Compounds" documents scam compounds using large language models, chatbots, voice cloning and synthetic video to generate convincing identities, localize messages and automate victim engagement at scale — with tens of billions in annual losses. Operators run a seven-stage pipeline: setting up operations, recruiting workers and victims, executing scams, collecting payments, layering proceeds, cashing out and reinvesting. Agentic AI that can autonomously plan multi-step tasks is flagged as an emerging risk to monitor.
  • Who it targets: Victims worldwide; the report documents compounds or related activity across Africa, Europe, the Americas and Asia.
  • Why it's new: This is the first major global-standard-setter report to describe AI not as a future threat to the scam trade but as its current industrial base — the same week the FBI disrupted an AI-deepfake video-call operation (see Top Stories).
  • Source: https://gbhackers.com/global-scam-networks/amp/

2. AI romance scams with live deepfake video calls — DEVELOPING

  • Date: Fresh coverage November 2026; the Singapore case dates to May 2026
  • How it works: Romance scammers now use real-time deepfake video calls — live face-swapping plus AI voice synthesis — so even a face-to-face video chat no longer proves the person on screen is who they claim to be. Fully automated AI personas can sustain emotionally convincing relationships for months. The scammer builds a relationship on Facebook, then moves it to WhatsApp; repeated deepfake video calls build trust before the money ask. One documented 2025 Los Angeles case: fraudsters impersonating actor Steve Burton took a victim's life savings (over $80,000) via gift cards, Zelle and bitcoin. In May 2026, Singapore police reported a single victim losing $3.8 million to a scammer posing as Prime Minister Lawrence Wong in a fabricated Zoom call.
  • Who it targets: People seeking relationships online, especially older adults; also fans of public figures whose likenesses are impersonated.
  • Why it's new: The video call used to be the gold-standard check against romance scams — "just get on a call with them." That check no longer works, which is the same lesson as today's FBI FaceStealer takedown.
  • Source: https://medium.com/@ayvataskenan/ai-romance-scams-the-fake-lover-built-to-steal-your-heart-and-your-savings-7402015b43db (Nov 2026; single-source Medium piece — the newer figures are reported, not independently verified)

3. SafePal crypto wallets targeted by physical-mail QR-code phishing — DEVELOPING

  • Date: November 13, 2026, 9:30 AM EST — late-breaking, just after the Nov 12 coverage window
  • How it works: Italian customers of hardware wallet maker SafePal are receiving fraudulent physical letters at their home addresses warning of a critical firmware security flaw, with personalized QR codes that lead to a counterfeit SafePal site. The letters use accurate customer details (full names, home addresses, specific order info) apparently taken from an earlier data breach affecting nearly 40,000 customers (orders March 2025–April 2026). Scanning the QR code directs victims to a fake site hosted on Arweave, a decentralized storage network, designed to harvest wallet credentials/seed phrases. SafePal says an Oct 30 independent audit found no vulnerabilities and urges recipients to discard the letters without scanning.
  • Who it targets: SafePal hardware wallet owners in Italy; similar campaigns have recently hit Trezor, Ledger and Coldcard users.
  • Why it's new: Phishing has jumped off the screen and into the mailbox — a physical letter with your real order details is far more convincing than an email, and a QR code bypasses the link-checking habits people have learned for email.

RESEARCH DESK

1. Phishing detectors trained on one AI model fail on another — but a quick retune fixes them (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Title: Cross-model evaluation of phishing detectors against LLM-generated emails
  • Authors: Rommel Gutierrez, William Villegas-Ch, Jaime Govea
  • Venue / Date: Peer-reviewed — Frontiers in Big Data, published July 7, 2026 (DOI: 10.3389/fdata.2026.1883452)
  • Key finding: Phishing detectors trained to spot AI-written scam emails work well only against the same AI model they were trained on. On a balanced set of 9,986 phishing emails (4,986 generated by GPT-4.1, DeepSeek 3.2, and Llama 3.3 70B, plus 5,000 human-written ones), accuracy (F1) fell by 28 percentage points when the detector faced emails from a different AI model. The good news: simply recalibrating the decision threshold on a small sample shrank the gap to 4 points (an 86% reduction), and a detector trained on all three models at once scored F1 = 0.997 on each. In plain terms, detectors don't break when scammers switch AI tools — they just need retuning.
  • Source: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/big-data/articles/10.3389/fdata.2026.1883452/full

2. Pig-butchering scams run on a rehearsed psychological playbook, not improvisation (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Title: Fake it till you make it: the psychological and communication tactics behind "Pig Butchering" scams
  • Authors: Ayşe Nur Asyalı, Muriel-Larissa Frank, Pol Hölzmer
  • Venue / Date: Peer-reviewed — Journal of Cybersecurity (Oxford University Press), Vol. 12, Issue 1, 2026 (DOI: 10.1093/cybsec/tyag003)
  • Key finding: Rather than interviewing victims, the researchers analysed actual scam training manuals — the scripts scam operations use to train their workers. They found scammers systematically exploit relationship-building, interpersonal communication, and victims' needs for personal growth and self-improvement to win trust and commitment, and the paper maps these into a unified stage-by-stage model of how a pig-butchering scam unfolds. The takeaway: these scams run on a rehearsed psychological playbook, not improvisation — which means prevention can target specific stages rather than just warning people in general.
  • Source: https://academic.oup.com/cybersecurity/article/12/1/tyag003

3. Crypto fraud's playbook: more manipulation, far bigger losses (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Title: The scammer's playbook: Exploring the psychological techniques and tactics used by scammers in the social engineering of cryptocurrency fraud
  • Authors: Brandon Dulisse, Chivon Fitch, Nathan Connealy
  • Venue / Date: Peer-reviewed — Journal of Economic Criminology, Vol. 11, March 2026, Article 100211 (DOI: 10.1016/j.jeconc.2026.100211)
  • Key finding: Coding 282 verified victim reports from California and Wisconsin state crypto-scam trackers, the researchers found fake trading platforms made up 51.5% of cases and pig-butchering schemes 33.7%. Scammers leaned overwhelmingly on impersonation and persuasion, averaging 1.77 psychological tactics and 1.86 techniques per incident. Crucially, more psychologically complex scams cost victims far more: in fake trading-platform cases, losses averaged $135,346 when 4–6 manipulation elements were combined, versus $63,034 for simpler scams (p = .029). Crypto fraud, the authors conclude, is a repeatable, engineered playbook — not random opportunism.
  • Source: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2949791426000060

4. AI agents that run scam calls can slip past today's safety guardrails (PREPRINT)

  • Title: ScamAgents: How AI Agents Can Simulate Human-Level Scam Calls
  • Author: Sanket Badhe (Rutgers University)
  • Venue / Date: Preprint — not peer-reviewed. arXiv:2508.06457, submitted Aug 8, 2025, revised Dec 9, 2025; also presented at the 2025 Conference on Applied Machine Learning for Information Security (CAMLIS) workshop
  • Key finding: The paper demonstrates "ScamAgent," an autonomous AI agent that holds realistic multi-turn scam phone calls — remembering the conversation, adapting to what the "victim" says, and using deceptive persuasion across turns — then converting the script into lifelike voice calls with text-to-speech. The author shows that current LLM safety guardrails (refusals, content filters) fail against this agent-based approach, because prompts can be broken up, disguised, or delivered gradually. The paper reports no headline success-rate numbers in its abstract; the finding is qualitative — existing single-prompt safety checks are not enough, and multi-turn safety auditing is needed.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.06457

TODAY'S SAFETY TIP

After a big outage, the phishing wave follows within hours — don't click any "verify / restore your account" link. Microsoft's global outage has millions of people expecting account emails, and scammers are already sending fake "verify / restore your Microsoft account" messages linking to convincing copies of the login page. Microsoft never emails or calls you unsolicited to fix your account, and its error messages never include a phone number. If you're worried about your account, open the Microsoft app or type the address yourself — never follow a link from a message. And after the FBI's FaceStealer takedown, remember the same rule applies to video calls: a face on Zoom or Teams is no longer proof of identity. If a "colleague" or "client" on a video call asks you to move crypto or approve a payment, hang up and call them back on a number you already have.
