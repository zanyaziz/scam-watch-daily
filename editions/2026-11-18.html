1. Scattered Spider hacker jailed six years over $11.6M crypto theft via text-message phishing

  • Outlet: BBC News (Joe Tidy, Cyber correspondent)
  • Date: November 18, 2026 (sentencing was Tuesday, Nov 17, in Santa Ana, California; BBC reported Nov 18)
  • Summary: Tyler Buchanan, 24, from Dundee, Scotland, was sentenced to six years in a U.S. federal prison for a text-message phishing and SIM-swapping scheme that stole more than $11.6 million (£8.8M) in cryptocurrency. Buchanan, a member of the Scattered Spider hacking collective, sent hundreds of smishing texts to employees of large tech and telecom companies, harvested their credentials via an automated Telegram bot, then used SIM-swapping to drain crypto investors' wallets. He pleaded guilty in April to conspiracy to commit wire fraud and aggravated identity theft; he is the fifth and final defendant sentenced in the DOJ case charged in November 2024. One victim alone lost $1.67 million in December 2022; a restitution hearing is set for December 11.
  • Why it matters: The final sentencing closes a years-long, four-country investigation into one of the most prolific young hacking crews — and it shows the playbook (smishing employees → Telegram credential harvesting → SIM swap) that still powers major crypto thefts today. U.S. prosecutors said the thefts had "severe, life-altering consequences" for victims.
  • Source: https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cm87987w1qz5o

NEW & EMERGING SCAMS

1. Phishing rides the Microsoft 365 outage — fake "messages held" emails

  • Date: Microsoft Threat Intelligence warned publicly November 14, 2026; fresh coverage November 16–17, 2026
  • How it works: After a real Microsoft 365/Exchange Online outage on Nov 12, 2026 (incident EX1550543, resolved Nov 13), attackers sent high-volume phishing emails impersonating Microsoft 365 service-health notices. The messages quote the real incident number and claim 4 messages were held during the outage and will be permanently deleted within 24 hours unless the recipient clicks "RESTORE MESSAGES." The links run through recipient-specific SendGrid tracking links to an unrelated site. Microsoft Threat Intelligence spotted the campaign Nov 13 and assesses the pattern as consistent with credential phishing, though credential theft and malware delivery are unconfirmed. No attacker group has been named.
  • Who it targets: Employees at organizations across multiple sectors, especially anyone whose Outlook went down during the Nov 12 outage.
  • Why it's new: The scammers borrow a real incident number and a real disruption victims experienced, so the message feels legitimate — and the 24-hour deletion threat forces a fast click. Check outage status only through the Microsoft 365 admin centre or the official service-health page, never through a link in an email.
  • Source: https://neotechcloudsolutions.com/en/fake-microsoft-365-outage-email-phishing-smb/

2. AI fake storefronts flood Black Friday — a new fake shop every ~3 minutes

  • Date: November 18, 2026 (fresh coverage; underlying Savi Security report October 20, 2026)
  • How it works: Scammers are no longer faking single products — they fake entire stores. Savi Security's Scamwise 2026 Holiday Shopping Scam Report (based on 1M+ consumer submissions) found users report a new fake store roughly every 3 minutes (~500/day), with fake holiday-themed stores up 5x in 60 days; at least 1 in 5 fake stores traced back to an ad on a major platform, Meta and Google the preferred channels. Generative AI now produces flawless shop text, realistic product photos and fake reviews, and lookalike domains. Fresh Nov 17–18 coverage warns of AI-generated celebrity endorsement videos and cloned-voice "customer support" calls, plus platform-steering that moves buyers off marketplaces into text threads where buyer protection doesn't apply.   • Who it targets: Holiday shoppers, especially anyone clicking social-media ads for 50–90%-off deals on phones, consoles and appliances.

3. Holiday delivery smishing peaks — fake USPS/Australia Post texts

  • Date: November 18, 2026 (7NEWS Australia report); surge coverage November 12–13, 2026
  • How it works: The classic "package on hold / delivery failed" smishing wave is surging into the holiday season, when nearly everyone has a parcel in transit. Texts impersonating USPS, FedEx, UPS, DHL, Amazon or Australia Post claim an incomplete address or unpaid fee and link to lookalike sites that harvest card and personal details.   • Who it targets: Anyone expecting parcels — effectively everyone in November–December.
  • Why it's new: A seasonal surge of a known scam, timed to the holiday shopping rush. Delete the text, forward it to 7726, and track parcels only in the carrier's own app or site.

RESEARCH DESK

1. Practicing against a simulated scammer beats reading warnings (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Title: ScamPilot: Simulating Conversations with LLMs to Protect Against Online Scams
  • Authors: Owen Hoffman, Kangze Peng, Sajid Kamal, Zehua You, Sukrit Venkatagiri (Swarthmore College)
  • Venue / Date: Peer-reviewed — Proceedings of the 2026 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems (CHI '26), April 13–17, 2026, Barcelona; arXiv:2601.22426
  • Key finding: ScamPilot trains people by letting them watch two LLM agents — a scammer and a target — role-play a scam in real time, while the user advises the target and answers embedded quizzes. In a controlled study of 150 people, the combined quiz-plus-advice version improved scam recognition by 8% and raised users' confidence that they could respond effectively by 9%, without making them more suspicious of legitimate messages. Self-efficacy rose 19%, though that gain was not statistically significant. The practical point: practicing against a simulated scammer beats just reading warnings.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.22426

2. Pig-butchering scams run in seven stages over weeks or months (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Title: "Hello, is this Anna?": Unpacking the Lifecycle of Pig-Butchering Scams
  • Authors: Rajvardhan Oak, Zubair Shafiq (University of California, Davis)
  • Venue / Date: Peer-reviewed — USENIX Symposium on Usable Privacy and Security (SOUPS 2025), August 10–12, 2025, Seattle; arXiv:2503.20821
  • Key finding: The first interview-based study of pig-butchering victims — in-depth interviews with 26 victims — maps the scam into seven distinct stages, from the opening "wrong number" text through staged trust-building, fake investment platforms showing fabricated returns, repeated high-pressure tactics, and an "encore" scam that targets victims again after the first loss. The paper puts total pig-butchering losses at nearly $75 billion since 2020. The takeaway: these scams are long cons that weaponize a relationship built over weeks or months, not one-off messages.
  • Source: https://www.usenix.org/conference/soups2025/technical-sessions

3. Ten Instagram posts are enough for AI to write spear-phishing that fools people (PREPRINT)

  • Title: Context-Aware Spear Phishing: Generative AI-Enabled Attacks Against Individuals via Public Social Media Data
  • Authors: Elham Pourabbas Vafa, Sayak Saha Roy, Shirin Nilizadeh (University of Texas at Arlington / Louisiana State University)
  • Venue / Date: Preprint — not yet peer-reviewed. arXiv preprint, submitted May 11, 2026, revised September 30, 2026; arXiv:2605.11268
  • Key finding: Using only public Instagram posts from 200 users, researchers showed that as few as 10 posts per person give an AI enough personal detail to generate convincing, tailored spear-phishing emails — across seven attack styles (impersonation, baiting, scareware, honey trap, and more) and five AI models. They generated roughly 18,000 emails and compared them with 4,000 real-world phishing samples: the AI messages were more personalized and persuasive, and in a study of 70 participants, people rated the AI-generated emails as significantly less suspicious than real phishing — mean suspiciousness 2.75 vs. 4.44 on a 5-point scale (p < 10⁻⁶). On the defensive side, the authors' prompt-level detector blocked the malicious prompts with 98.1% accuracy. Caveat: this is a preprint, and the human study measured perceived suspiciousness, not real-world click behavior.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.11268

4. AI email agents fail badly when phishing is tuned to attack them specifically (PREPRINT)

  • Title: AgentPhishing: A Benchmark of LLM Email Agents Under Adaptive Attack
  • Authors: Justin Tan, Miao Li, Ding Zhao (Carnegie Mellon University)
  • Venue / Date: Preprint — not yet peer-reviewed. arXiv preprint, submitted October 22, 2026; arXiv:2610.31404
  • Key finding: AI email assistants that read your inbox and act on messages look safe on standard phishing tests — all six agents tested caught every old phishing email and complied with none. But when the researchers adapted the attack to each agent (their "PhishOpt" method), the picture flipped: in an evaluation of nine agents, four detected fewer than 10% of the adapted phishing emails and complied with over 45% of the malicious requests inside them. One agent's susceptibility score jumped from 0.12 to 0.95 after the attack was optimized against it. The lesson: a detector that passes a fixed phishing test is not proof an AI inbox agent is safe — attacks tuned to the agent itself are the real test.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31404

TODAY'S SAFETY TIP

Never release goods — or pay — on a payment screenshot alone; verify the money yourself before anything changes hands. Today's victim reports show why: a North Carolina woman lost $25,000 on a Marketplace deal routed through Venmo, and a South African seller handed over a camera to a courier on the strength of a fake payment voucher that never cleared. A screenshot is trivially faked. Before you hand over an item, log in to your own bank or payment app and confirm the money has actually arrived — and if you're the buyer, pay in person, in cash, after inspecting the item. The same "verify it yourself" rule covers the holiday scams in today's edition: check a Black Friday store's domain and seller before paying (pay by credit card, never by transfer, gift card, or crypto), and track parcels only in the carrier's own app — never through a link in a text.
