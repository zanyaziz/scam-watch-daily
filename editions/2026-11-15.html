TOP STORIES (ranked by importance)

1. Telegram account-takeover kit exposed: fake "Telegrαm" checklists, 244 domains, chat history and wallet swaps stolen

  • Outlet: OSINT researcher "osintph" on Medium (independent technical investigation)
  • Date: November 15, 2026 (published Nov 16, 01:52 UTC = Nov 15, 17:52 PST — verified by opening the article)
  • Summary: A phishing operation sends Telegram "checklist" messages from homoglyph accounts ("Telegrαm" with a Greek alpha) claiming the account will be deleted in 24 hours unless a link is followed. The link leads to a cloned Telegram Web K client that harvests the victim's phone number, login code and two-step password, then uploads the session, contact list and full chat history to a server in Singapore — and swaps TRON/USDT wallet addresses in the victim's chats (a crypto clipper). The operator holds 244 domains (bgpNN/.sbs, bgpNN/.icu, jpgNN/.sbs), registered in scripted batches at NameSilo; analysts who visit directly see a decoy Chinese calendar app instead. The researcher reported the operation to NameSilo, Cloudflare, Telegram, Netcraft and Google Safe Browsing; Netcraft flagged a landing host as malicious in under a minute, while Cloudflare initially cleared the landing pages as "no abusive content."
  • Why it matters: A concrete, currently active Telegram takeover infrastructure with named indicators (domains, sender IDs 7568365933 and 6710998852, backend jpg71.sbs, Turnstile sitekey) — and a reminder that Telegram never sends verification links by DM, and that two-step verification doesn't help if you type the password into the attacker's page yourself.
  • Source: https://osintph.medium.com/a-calendar-for-scanners-inside-the-telegram-takeover-kit-hiding-behind-244-domains-ed3c14871edd

2. FTC's proposed scam-ad rule: 190 comments in as Nov 30 deadline nears; Meta's ~$16B scam-ad revenue figure in the record

  • Outlet: ecorpit (analysis of the FTC docket and Federal Register notice)
  • Date: November 15, 2026 (docket status as of Nov 15) — the underlying advance notice was published Oct 1, 2026
  • Summary: The FTC is weighing whether search engines, social networks and marketplaces should carry legal duties when their ad-optimization tools help create or target impersonation scam ads. The advance notice of proposed rulemaking (91 FR 62347, RIN 3084-AB90) was approved 2-0 on Sept 24; comments are due Nov 30, 2026, and the docket held 190 comments as of Nov 15. Measures under consideration include pre-screening ads, denying ad tools to impersonators, a consumer reporting tool, removing confirmed scam ads, and record-keeping — possibly as a safe harbor. The FTC cites more than 1 million imposter reports and nearly $3.5 billion in reported 2025 losses; the notice also cites Reuters reporting that internal Meta documents estimated ~10% of Meta's 2024 revenue (~$16 billion) came from scam ads.
  • Why it matters: This is the regulatory story behind the scam-ad problem: whether platforms that profit from scam ads bear responsibility for them. The Nov 30 comment deadline is two weeks out, and the $16B figure is the most striking number in the docket.
  • Source: https://ecorpit.com/ftc-scam-ad-rule-comments-november-2026/

3. Google Messages' new "Enhanced protection" scam filter reaches more Android phones

  • Outlet: Android Police
  • Date: November 13, 2026 — late-breaking story from Nov 13
  • Summary: Google Messages now offers an opt-in "Enhanced protection" setting (announced Oct 14) that adds cloud Gemini analysis of conversations with unknown senders — catching slow-burn scams that unfold over several messages — plus on-device Gemini Nano checks of unfamiliar links and clearer warnings before opening links from strangers. It only analyzes chats with unknown senders, runs in Private AI Compute, and sits under Messages settings → Protection & safety. Google says its existing scam detection already flags more than 5 million suspected scam calls and texts a month. The cloud check works on Pixel 6+ and Galaxy S24+; the Nano link checks need Pixel 10+ / Galaxy S26-class hardware. Rollout is gradual and the setting is off by default.
  • Why it matters: The most practical platform defense story of the week — a free, built-in scam filter most Android users don't know exists, and it specifically targets the conversational "wrong-number / package / hello" scams that evade single-message filters.
  • Source: https://www.androidpolice.com/google-messages-new-scam-protection-why-how-turned-it-on/

4. XRP holders targeted by ~11.5 million scam posts in 30 days

  • Outlet: Finbold
  • Date: November 11, 2026 — late-breaking story from Nov 11
  • Summary: Fraudulent accounts are flooding X with fake XRP giveaways, airdrops and reward schemes. XRP Ledger Foundation community director Hussein "Vet" Zangana estimates ~11.5 million posts targeting XRP users appeared in the past 30 days — about 383,000 a day, nearly 16,000 an hour — based on analysis through X's API. Victims are directed to external sites and asked to connect wallets or disclose recovery information. The campaigns also exploit compromised trusted accounts: Ireland's national police X account was hijacked in October to promote a fake XRP/Xaman airdrop, and in June one holder reportedly lost 14,646 XRP after approving a payment disguised as reward verification.
  • Why it matters: The sheer scale — 11.5 million scam posts in a month against a single crypto community — shows how industrialized social-media impersonation has become, and how even verified/official accounts can't be trusted at face value.
  • Source: https://finbold.com/warning-xrp-scam-activity-is-operating-at-massive-scale/

5. The top "customer service" number in your search results may not be the store's

  • Outlet: InvestigateTV interview with ConsumerAffairs writer Kyle James, reported Nov 12, 2026 (via Dunlap.Today roundup)
  • Date: November 12, 2026 — late-breaking story from Nov 12
  • Summary: Scammers are buying sponsored search listings for big stores' "customer service" so a fake phone number shows up above the real one, alongside fake "store closing" ads and too-good-to-be-true "ghost" websites. Callers who dial the fake number are steered into handing over payment details or buying gift cards. The same roundup notes Tennessee's attorney general announced Nov 13 that the state is part of the $63 million Family Dollar settlement (covered in the Nov 13 edition), and flags Google's new Messages scam filter (see story 3 above).
  • Why it matters: Search is where most people start when something goes wrong with an order — and the first result is now for sale to whoever pays, including scammers. Always get a support number from the company's own website or the back of your card, never from a search ad.
  • Source: https://dunlap.today/a-customer-service-number-at-the-top-of-your-search-that-isnt-the-stores-real-money-set-aside-for-family-dollar-shoppers-no-fee-to-claim-it-and-a-free-scam-filter-already-sitting-i/

NEW & EMERGING SCAMS

1. Fake FBI/IC3 "recovery agents" re-target prior victims on Facebook

  • Date: Nov 9, 2026 (report); pattern ongoing per FBI alerts
  • How it works: Scammers run Facebook ads and fake profiles posing as FBI or IC3 personnel, "recovery experts," or "Directors of Fraud Investigations," offering to help people who already lost money to a scam get it back — for an upfront fee. Some use AI-generated deepfake videos of supposed federal officials and look-alike IC3 complaint sites to look legitimate; one ad even falsely claimed victims who lost over $4,000 qualify for federal review. The real IC3 has no official social media presence, never investigates or recovers funds via Facebook/Telegram, and never charges a fee to recover funds.
  • Who it targets: People who have already been scammed — a "second hit" on victims at their most vulnerable.
  • Why it's new: The FBI's Honolulu field office issued a fresh alert Nov 2, and the FBI reports nearly 61,000 government-impersonation complaints with over $1.6B in losses from Jan 2025–Jul 2026. The ads were removed after being reported to Meta.
  • Source: https://www.neowin.net/news/facebook-scammers-are-targeting-fraud-victims-through-fake-fbi-profiles/

2. Fake "FTC agent" phone-deactivation scam

  • Date: Nov 6–7, 2026 (FTC warning this month; AVASC daily briefs Nov 6–7)
  • How it works: A caller claiming to be an FTC employee says a phone number registered in your name is being used to commit fraud overseas and threatens to deactivate/disconnect your service unless you pay — via wire, gift cards, or a crypto kiosk deposit. The FTC has warned explicitly that real FTC staff never ask for fund transfers, gift cards, or crypto-kiosk deposits and never threaten to shut off phone service.
  • Who it targets: Anyone with a phone number — the threat of losing service creates instant compliance pressure.
  • Why it's new: A brand-new impersonation script flagged by the FTC in November, spreading fast alongside the toll-text wave; it weaponizes the FTC's own name, an authority victims would not expect scammers to impersonate.
  • Source: https://www.avasc.org/briefings/fake-ftc-agent-calls-spread-as-toll-texts-post-election-cons-per-2026-11-06

3. AI voice-clone "family emergency" calls — now a few seconds of audio is enough

  • Date: Nov 15, 2026 (guide updated Nov 15; related guides Nov 11 and Nov 5)
  • How it works: A scammer clones a loved one's voice from a short audio clip lifted from a video or reel posted online, then calls sounding like that family member in crisis — car accident, arrest, hospital — demanding urgent, secret money by wire, gift card, or crypto. The FTC warns only a short clip plus a cheap/free cloning program is needed. Social chatter this week includes dramatized voice-clone scam videos and warnings that deepfake detection by ear is unreliable (~50% accuracy in one cited test).
  • Who it targets: Parents and grandparents — the cloned crying voice creates instant panic, and the "don't hang up" instruction stops victims from verifying.
  • Why it's new: The tactic is now cheap and mainstream enough that multiple consumer guides were published/updated Nov 5–15, and TRM Labs' 2026 AI-in-Crime Adoption Index reports deepfake-scam losses so far in 2026 already exceed all of 2025 by 263%. Defense remains procedural, not technical: hang up, call back on a known number, use a family code word.
  • Source: https://www.chainpursuit.com/blog/ai-voice-cloning-urgent-call-scam/

4. Unpaid-toll smishing wave — still spreading with fresh local warnings

  • Date: Nov 6–7, 2026 (ongoing wave; new local alerts that week)
  • How it works: Mass text campaigns claim a small unpaid toll (often ~$4–$12) with a $45–$50 late-fee threat and a link to a look-alike payment page that harvests card and personal data. Fresh warnings this week came from the Woodford County (IL) Sheriff's Office, Maryland's Transportation Authority, and E-ZPass New York; toll agencies stress they never ask for payment or personal details by text or email link.
  • Who it targets: Drivers — nearly everyone has driven a toll road, so the message feels plausible.
  • Why it's new: Despite being active since 2024, the wave hasn't slowed — AVASC calls it one of the fastest-growing imposter scams, contributing to a reported 40% jump in government-imposter complaints, and social posts this week show the exact scam texts still circulating widely. Imposter scams overall are tied to ~$3.5B in losses.
  • Source: https://www.avasc.org/briefings/ftc-phone-deactivation-scam-spreads-as-toll-texts-post-election-cons-per-2026-11-06

5. Task/job scams gain a new twist: AI "biometric interview" harvesting face and eye scans — developing

  • Date: Nov 12, 2026 (guide updated); underlying advisory May 29, 2026
  • How it works: The familiar WhatsApp/Telegram task scam — like videos, rate hotels, earn $200–$900/day, small real payouts first, then a "prepaid task" deposit that is never returned — now has a newer variant flagged by India's Ministry of Home Affairs (I4C cybercrime unit): fake online interviews that harvest victims' face and eye biometrics on unknown websites, presumably for deepfake/identity fraud. A real employer never asks you to pay to get a job, deposit money to unlock earnings, or complete a face/eye scan on an unknown site.
  • Who it targets: Job seekers, especially in India and Southeast Asia, where task scams are the fastest-growing employment-fraud category (FTC: task-scam reports jumped from ~5,000 in all of 2023 to ~20,000 in just the first half of 2024; job-scam losses $750.6M in 2024).
  • Why it's new: The biometric twist is the newest evolution of the category — face and eye scans collected in a "job interview" can feed deepfake and identity fraud later.
  • Source: https://www.techyami.in/2026/11/fake-job-offer-scams-in-india-2026-task.html
  • Note: Developing — the biometric-interview claim rests on a single MHA/I4C advisory as reported secondhand; no independent corroboration found in this run.

6. Quishing: fake QR-code stickers on parking meters and EV chargers

  • Date: Nov 14, 2026 (guide updated); Nov 11, 2026 (related guide)
  • How it works: Scammers print official-looking QR stickers and paste them over real codes on parking meters, pay stations, EV chargers, restaurant tables, and posters. Scanning opens a look-alike payment or login page that harvests card details — and because a QR code hides its destination, victims can't inspect the link first; the codes also slip past email filters when sent in messages. Some fake pages even enroll victims in recurring "subscriptions."
  • Who it targets: Drivers, EV owners, café and gym customers — anyone who scans a code to pay in the physical world.
  • Why it's new: A cluster of fresh guides and local warnings in early-to-mid November (including small-business warnings in Frederick, MD, Nov 11) shows the trick spreading from parking meters into cafés, gyms, and event posters; the FTC has warned about QR codes hiding scam links (sometimes called "quishing").
  • Source: https://smartaikit.com/how-to-spot-a-fake-or-tampered-qr-code

7. AI-industrialized romance-investment ("pig butchering") scams — developing

  • Date: Nov 9, 2026 (AVASC weekly brief, week of Nov 9)
  • How it works: Romance-investment scams — a fake online relationship that gradually steers the victim into a bogus crypto "investment" — are increasingly AI-assisted: voice-altering tools, deepfake video calls, and synthetic profiles make the fake partner harder to spot before money is lost. The FBI's Operation Level Up has proactively warned thousands of likely victims.
  • Who it targets: People open to online romance or "investment mentorship" — the relationship builds trust over weeks before any money is mentioned.
  • Why it's new: AI has removed the old tells (bad grammar, stolen photos, refusal to video-chat) — the scammer can now appear live on video as a real-looking person — and the FBI recorded 22,000+ AI-related complaints in 2025 with losses approaching $893M.
  • Source: https://www.avasc.org/briefings/this-week-in-scams-fake-recovery-agents-ai-romance-cons-toll-texts-2026-11-09
  • Note: Developing — the AVASC brief is a weekly roundup rather than a single incident report; the trend is well-attested but the specific "week of Nov 9" framing is AVASC's own synthesis.

RESEARCH DESK

1. AI-personalized spear phishing triples clicks — for 3 cents an email (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Title: A Large-Scale Study of Personalized Phishing using Large Language Models
  • Authors: Stefan Czybik, Anne Josiane Kouam, Peter Heubl, Jan Magnus Nold, Konrad Rieck (BIFOLD/TU Berlin, Inria, Ruhr-University Bochum)
  • Venue / Date: Peer-reviewed — 35th USENIX Security Symposium, August 2026 (Baltimore)
  • Key finding: In a controlled field experiment with 7,700 participants, AI-personalized spear-phishing emails achieved an average click rate of 10.0%, versus 3.9% for untargeted generic emails — almost triple. The effect held whether the generic emails were written by humans or by LLMs. Personalization cost only about $0.03 per email (4,010 personalized emails generated for ~$120), making large-scale spear phishing cheap for even small-budget criminals.
  • Source: https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity26/presentation/czybik

2. A friendly chatbot got over 90% of people to hand over personal data (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Title: Malicious LLM-Based Conversational AI Makes Users Reveal Personal Information
  • Authors: Xiao Zhan, Juan-Carlos Carrillo, William Seymour, Jose Such (King's College London; VRAIN, Universitat Politècnica de València)
  • Venue / Date: Peer-reviewed — 34th USENIX Security Symposium, August 2025 (Seattle)
  • Key finding: In a randomized controlled trial with 502 participants, chatbots deliberately tuned with malicious system prompts got users to disclose far more personal data than benign chatbots or a plain form. Over 90% of participants talking to a malicious chatbot disclosed personal information, versus 24% filling in a form; over 80% answered every personal-data request, versus 6% for the form. The "reciprocity" strategy (the bot shares something first, then asks) extracted the most data while being rated the least privacy-risky — users barely noticed the danger. King's College London's summary puts the overall effect at up to 12.5 times more personal information disclosed. Most commonly disclosed: age, hobbies, and country, followed by gender, nationality, and job title; a minority also disclosed health conditions and income.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.11680

3. Plain-language AI explanations of scam texts lifted detection accuracy — including for older adults (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Title: Can You Walk Me Through It? Explainable SMS Phishing Detection using LLM-based Agents
  • Authors: Yizhu Wang, Haoyu Zhai, Chenkai Wang, Qingying Hao, Nick A. Cohen, Roopa Foulger, Jonathan A. Handler, Gang Wang (University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign; OSF Healthcare)
  • Venue / Date: Peer-reviewed — 21st Symposium on Usable Privacy and Security (SOUPS 2025), August 10–12, 2025 (Seattle)
  • Key finding: The researchers built "SmishX," an LLM agent system that checks a suspicious text message against external context (sender identity, domain/brand info, URL redirects, web screenshots) and explains why it is a scam. On a real-world dataset of 1,200 SMS messages it reached 98.8% detection accuracy, beating existing methods. In user studies with 175 participants, reading SmishX's plain-language explanations raised people's own phishing-detection accuracy from 0.712 to 0.928 overall — and older adults reached 0.942 after reading the explanations.
  • Source: https://www.usenix.org/conference/soups2025/presentation/wang

4. AI models that can spot phishing still write it — up to 98.5% attack success in voice scenarios (PREPRINT)

  • Title: GuardPhish: Securing Open-Source LLMs from Phishing Abuse
  • Authors: Rina Mishra, Gaurav Varshney, Doddipatla Sesha Sahithi
  • Venue / Date: Preprint — not yet peer-reviewed. arXiv, submitted 19 April 2026
  • Key finding: The authors built GuardPhish, a dataset of 70,015 phishing prompts across web, email, SMS, and voice scenarios drawn from real campaigns. They found a striking "detection–generation gap" in eight open-source LLMs: models that correctly identified phishing intent up to 96% of the time still generated usable phishing content from the same prompts, with attack success rates reaching 98.5% in voice-based scenarios. In other words, knowing a phish when it sees one doesn't stop the model from writing one. A lightweight filter trained on the dataset blocked the abuse with up to 98.27% accuracy without modifying the underlying model.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.17313

5. The first full map of the LLM-phishing landscape — and why detectors must be tested against many generators (PREPRINT)

  • Title: SoK: Systematizing Generation, Characteristics, and Defenses in LLM-Generated Phishing
  • Authors: Fengchao Chen, Tingmin Wu, Van Nguyen, Carsten Rudolph
  • Venue / Date: Preprint — not yet peer-reviewed. arXiv, submitted 29 August 2025, last revised 29 August 2026 (v4)
  • Key finding: This is a systematization paper — the first comprehensive map of the LLM-phishing landscape. It builds a taxonomy of how LLMs are manipulated to create phishing, what makes AI-generated phishing different from human-written phishing, and which defenses match each attack method. Crucially, the authors benchmarked five academic and five industrial phishing detectors across datasets made with different LLM generation methods, and concluded that defenses must be tested against many LLM generators and manipulation strategies — a detector that works against one model or one trick may fail against another. (No single headline accuracy number is stated in the abstract; the value is the taxonomy and the cross-detector benchmark.)
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.21457

TODAY'S SAFETY TIP

Never click a "verify your account" link that arrives by DM — open the app yourself, and never pay anyone to recover stolen money. Today's top story is the proof: the Telegram takeover kit works because victims type their login code and two-step password into a convincing clone of Telegram Web reached from a DM link. Telegram will never send you a verification link by message — if an account warning looks urgent, close the message and open Telegram (or any app) directly to check. The same rule covers the fake FBI/IC3 "recovery agents" in today's scams section: no real agency recovers funds through Facebook, and anyone who charges a fee to get your money back is running a second scam on top of the first. If you have already been scammed, report it free at reportfraud.ftc.gov and ic3.gov — and if you were paid by bank transfer, call your bank the same day; one victim in today's edition got £150 back in two days by doing exactly that.
