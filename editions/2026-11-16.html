TOP STORIES (ranked by importance)

1. France's lottery operator sues Meta in Paris over fake casino ads

  • Outlet: iGaming Times (reporting an AFP dispatch; also covered by AFP via Boursorama/TradingView and CasinoBeats)
  • Date: November 16, 2026
  • Summary: France's lottery operator FDJ United has launched urgent interim court proceedings (a référé) against Meta to force the removal of Facebook and Instagram ads that copy its brands to promote online casinos, which are illegal in France. FDJ United says it counted 226 new illicit posts on Facebook and Instagram over just four days (May 18–21), many near-identical to content it had already reported, with some copying FDJ partner shops and newspaper front pages to look credible. The disclosure came with FDJ United's first national campaign against illegal gambling, launched Nov 16 for European Safer Gambling Week.
  • Why it matters: It follows a Paris Court of Appeal ruling (January 2026) that already went against Meta in a similar case brought by the Barrière casino group — a court can order Meta not just to remove individual ads but to prevent them appearing. A second order would make proactive filtering the expected remedy for brand owners, and the case spotlights how scam/illegal-gambling ads keep resurfacing on Meta's platforms even after being reported.
  • Source: https://igaming-times.com/news/regulatory/fdj-united-takes-meta-to-court-over-fake-brand-ads

2. Attacker abused LearnWorlds free trials to send 13,000 fake YouTube copyright notices

  • Outlet: The Register
  • Date: November 16, 2026
  • Summary: LearnWorlds, a Cyprus-based online-course platform, confirmed an attacker created around 570 free trial accounts between November 7–14 and used them to send 13,000 phishing emails to YouTube creators posing as fake copyright-infringement notices. The emails pushed recipients to a fake page that harvested Google credentials; the first wave (9,853 emails, Nov 7–9) was followed by a second wave from a paid account (3,723 emails, Nov 14). LearnWorlds says its systems were not breached and that it has strengthened bot checks and reported the phishing site for takedown. Victims included security researcher Marcus Hutchins, who received one from the company's official mail server.
  • Why it matters: It weaponizes creators' fear of losing their YouTube channel — fake copyright strikes are a proven lure for account takeover, with hijacked channels typically repurposed for crypto-scam livestreams. It also shows how legitimate SaaS free trials can be industrialized into a phishing factory, and that the emails came from a real company's mail server, defeating "check the sender" advice.
  • Source: https://www.theregister.com/security/2026/11/16/crook-turns-learnworlds-free-trials-into-phishing-factory-sends-13000-fake-youtube-copyright-notices/5310122

3. Microsoft warns of high-volume phishing campaign riding its own 365 outage

  • Outlet: Microsoft Threat Intelligence (official Microsoft Security Blog)
  • Date: November 16, 2026 (campaign observed November 13, 2026)
  • Summary: Microsoft Threat Intelligence observed a coordinated, high-volume phishing campaign on November 13 targeting organizations worldwide with emails disguised as legitimate Microsoft 365 service-health notifications. The messages claimed emails had been held during a service disruption — exploiting the real Nov 12 Microsoft outage — and would be deleted within 24 hours unless the recipient clicked through. The campaign used randomized "Outage – " subjects, spoofed sender domains (moments-notice[.]com, planetmaison[.]com, newbooker[.]com) and recipient-specific tracking links leading to a common non-Microsoft destination. Microsoft assesses it was likely credential phishing; no credential capture or malware delivery was confirmed, and no actor was attributed.
  • Why it matters: It's a textbook case of scammers exploiting a real, widely reported outage within hours — the urgency is plausible because the outage was real. Anyone who received "held email" notices after the Nov 12 outage should treat them as suspect and check service health only via the official Microsoft 365 admin center.
  • Source: https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/security/blog/2026/11/16/high-volume-phishing-campaign-uses-microsoft-365-outage-lures-target-multiple-industry-sectors-2/

4. Chase software bug disrupts major U.S. banks — prime conditions for bank-impersonation scams

  • Outlet: CBS News (also AP, Reuters, NBC News, Fortune)
  • Date: November 16, 2026
  • Summary: Customers at JPMorgan Chase, Bank of America, U.S. Bank, Wells Fargo, Capital One and Truist reported trouble with online banking, balances, transfers and ATM access on Monday, Nov 16. Chase attributed the problem to a software bug in on-premise hardware systems, said it was not cyber-related, and said transactions were posting normally again by Monday evening. Downdetector logged nearly 4,800 Chase reports by 2 p.m. ET, plus ~2,000 for Bank of America and ~1,000 each for U.S. Bank and Wells Fargo. Several banks said their own systems were operating normally and the disruption was limited to transactions routed through Chase.
  • Why it matters: Outages are a classic scammer trigger: fake "bank fraud alert" texts and calls spike whenever customers can't reach their real bank to verify. Readers should expect a wave of Chase/BofA impersonation messages piggybacking on this outage and verify any alert only inside the bank's official app.
  • Source: https://www.cbsnews.com/news/banks-online-service-outages-jpmorgan-chase-bank-of-america-wells-fargo/

6. AI content mill copyright-struck a real musician's own recording off YouTube

  • Outlet: 404 Media
  • Date: November 16, 2026
  • Summary: 404 Media reports that Media Nov Records — a company that says it operates about 500 YouTube channels of AI-generated music loops — issued a copyright claim that took down musician Samantha Shokin's 50-second cover of the Bulgarian folk song "Bre Petrunko," a public-domain song she recorded herself. YouTube's automated system rejected both of her counter-notifications. Only after 404 Media contacted the company did Media Nov withdraw the claim, saying the video "has not generated revenue or received views"; YouTube then restored the video about 24 hours later. Media Nov, founded just over a year ago despite claiming roots in 1996, advertises automated DMCA takedowns and AI-powered content recognition, and said it intends to initiate legal proceedings.
  • Why it matters: It exposes how YouTube's automated copyright system can be gamed by AI content mills — the same fake-copyright-strike machinery that phishers use to steal creator accounts (see story 2 above). For creators, a strike from an entity claiming rights it doesn't hold can still remove their work, and the appeal path failed twice before press intervention.
  • Source: https://www.404media.co/how-an-ai-content-mill-put-a-real-musician-through-copyright-hell/

7. FTC and 17 states' $63 million Family Dollar settlement: Alabama joins as Nov 16 announcement

  • Outlet: Alabama Attorney General's office / FTC (press release; also covered by Inc. on Nov 16)
  • Date: November 16, 2026 (Alabama announcement; the settlement itself was covered in the Nov 13 edition)
  • Summary: The FTC and 17 state attorneys general secured a $63 million settlement with Family Dollar Stores over allegations the chain routinely charged customers more at the register than the prices displayed on shelves, price tags, advertisements, its website and mobile app. The proposed order bars Family Dollar from misrepresenting prices and requires an independently assessed, annually certified pricing-accuracy program. The settlement includes a $40 million consumer redress fund and a $20 million discount program. The complaint says thousands of stores overcharged shoppers despite thousands of failed inspections, hundreds of consumer complaints and multiple prior enforcement actions.
  • Why it matters: A major federal-state consumer-fraud enforcement action — and part of a broader FTC crackdown on discount chains serving low-income communities (a similar suit against Dollar General was announced in October). It also comes with a practical consumer angle: shoppers who were overcharged may be eligible for redress from the $40 million fund.

NEW & EMERGING SCAMS

1. GTA 6 launch phishing surge — gaming lures up 1,300%

  • Date: November 16, 2026 (KnowBe4 Threat Lab blog)
  • How it works: With Grand Theft Auto 6 launching November 19, 2026, KnowBe4's Threat Lab documented a coordinated phishing surge impersonating Rockstar Games. Two waves: (a) mass-market blank-body emails with a single link leading to Telegram-hosted pages (graph.org) styled with Vice City neon art and AI-generated images of characters Lucia and Jason, pushing a "$4,000 bonus" with promo code ROCKSTAR that redirects to fake casino/crypto sites; (b) a targeted "employee benefit" wave posing as HR emails offering a "free GTA 6 pre-order" to the first 250 employees, expiring in 48 hours. The enterprise wave uses look-alike vendor domains that pass SPF/DKIM/DMARC checks, and hides QR codes built from Unicode text-block characters (not images) so scanners miss them — scanning with a personal phone moves the victim off the corporate network, bypassing web filtering and endpoint detection.
  • Who it targets: Gamers and corporate employees.
  • Why it's new: Gaming-related phishing emails up 1,300% vs. the March–August 2026 monthly average; GTA-themed lures were 9.6% of all phishing volume in the week starting Oct 26 and 42.4% of all phishing emails over the Oct 31–Nov 1 weekend (KnowBe4 telemetry).
  • Source: https://blog.knowbe4.com/gta-6-phishing-hype-weaponization

2. Fake GTA 6 PC downloads spread "Warden Stealer" malware — developing

  • Date: November 14–16, 2026 (social coverage; UK ICO warning dated Nov 16)
  • How it works: Fake "GTA 6 for PC" download pages and APKs are circulating ahead of launch; there is no PC version — the game launches Nov 19 on PlayStation 5 and Xbox Series X|S only. Security firm Cynet found a fake installer containing no game files, only ~796 KB of malware dubbed "Warden Stealer," which injects into browsers, steals saved passwords and Steam/Roblox logins, and swaps copied crypto wallet addresses. Some pages tell victims to run as admin and disable antivirus. Gamers searching for early access, beta keys, or PC downloads are reached via email, Discord, YouTube, and search results. Netcraft researchers counted 121 new fake "leaker" websites created in a few months under GTA 6-themed domains (e.g., gta6-leaker-pc-50.click); one sample went from 0/68 to 34/68 antivirus detections. Separate lures demand $100–$250 in crypto to "unlock" a download or "VIP early access."
  • Who it targets: Gamers searching for early access, beta keys, or PC downloads.
  • Why it's new: The UK Information Commissioner's Office issued a GTA 6 phishing warning dated Nov 16, 2026.
  • Source: https://www.gta6realm.com/2026/07/gta-6-beta-scam-warning.html and https://www.maxiofunstudio.com/blog/gta-6-apk-download-scam/

3. WhatsApp "zero-click" account takeover — 359 cases in Spain's Basque Country — developing

  • Date: November 16, 2026 (story published Nov 16, 16:28 PST)

4. WhatsApp OTP theft and "ghost pairing" surge prompts police warnings

  • Date: November 15, 2026
  • How it works: Telangana Cyber Security Bureau (TGCSB) and Malkajgiri Police issued fresh advisories about a sharp rise in WhatsApp account takeovers. Fraudsters register the victim's WhatsApp on another device using their mobile number, then trick them — via fake messages, calls, or links — into sharing the six-digit verification OTP or a pairing code. A related "ghost pairing" method secretly links the attacker's device without logging the victim out.   • Who it targets: WhatsApp users, primarily in India (Hyderabad/Telangana alerts); once inside, attackers read chats and impersonate the victim to contacts.
  • Why it's new: Police stress no genuine organization — including WhatsApp, banks, or government agencies — ever asks for an OTP.

5. Holiday delivery-text smishing surges as shopping season opens

  • Date: November 15, 2026 (Better Business Bureau warning)
  • How it works: Fake package-delivery texts — the most reported text scam in the US — are spiking with the holiday shopping season. Messages impersonating USPS, FedEx, or UPS claim a package can't be delivered due to an "incomplete address" or demand a small "redelivery fee" ($1–$3); the link leads to a look-alike carrier page that harvests card and personal details.
  • Who it targets: Anyone expecting parcels — in November and December, nearly everyone. USPS will not text about a delivery problem unless you signed up for tracking alerts on that number, and those messages never contain an action link.
  • Why it's new: The FTC's April 2025 data spotlight named fake delivery alerts the single most-reported text scam of 2024, with total text-scam losses of $470 million that year — more than five times 2020's figure. International Fraud Awareness Week runs Nov 15–21, 2026, coinciding with the surge.

RESEARCH DESK

1. AI voice phishing: up to 36% said they might comply — and the danger is the economics, not "superhuman" persuasion (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Title: Evaluating AI Models' Capability to Automate Voice Phishing Attacks
  • Authors: Fred Heiding (Harvard Kennedy School), Claudio Mayrink Verdun, Simon Lermen, Andrew Kao, Vitor Albiero, Lauren Deason, Irina-Elena Veliche, Christine Lehane (Meta Platforms)
  • Venue / Date: Peer-reviewed — Expert Systems with Applications, vol. 332 (2027), article 133620; received Apr 2026, accepted July 10, 2026, published online July 17, 2026
  • Key finding: In a nationally representative experiment with 4,100 U.S. adults exposed to AI-generated voice-phishing calls (from Llama Full-Duplex, Sesame, Gemini, OpenAI, Play.AI, ElevenLabs) vs. human baselines, up to 36.1% of participants said they would or might comply with a "relative-in-distress" scam, and 16.5% would or might comply averaged across all five scam categories. Caller persuasiveness — not how human the voice sounded — was the strongest predictor of compliance; Sesame's voice scored statistically indistinguishable from human voices. The authors' economic analysis found human-operated vishing unprofitable at U.S. wages, but AI-powered vishing economically viable — so the present danger is the economics of automation, not "superhuman" persuasion.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.09970v2

2. Brands' anti-smishing guidance is full of gaps — half don't even say how to report it (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Title: What Are Brands Telling You About Smishing? A Cross-Industry Evaluation of Customer Guidance
  • Authors: Dev Vikesh Doshi, Mehjabeen Tasnim, Fernando Landeros, Chinthagumpala Muni Venkatesh, Muhammad Lutfor Rahman (California State University San Marcos); Daniel Timko (Smishtank.com)
  • Venue / Date: Peer-reviewed — Symposium on Usable Security and Privacy (USEC) 2026, Feb 27, 2026, San Diego, CA
  • Key finding: A content analysis of 149 well-known brands across 25 categories found big gaps in the anti-smishing guidance brands give customers: only 46% even mentioned a definition of smishing, only 35% gave examples, less than 1% had a video tutorial, and only 50% told customers how to report a smishing attempt. Some brands even recommended potentially ineffective advice such as "ignoring suspicious messages." Context: the FTC estimated text-message scams cost $470 million in 2024, and fewer than 35% of North Americans know what smishing is.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/html/2601.20999v1/

3. Coaching a simulated victim through a live scam call raised recognition 8% (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Title: ScamPilot: Simulating Conversations with LLMs to Protect Against Online Scams
  • Authors: Owen Hoffman, Kangze Peng, Sajid Kamal, Zehua You, Sukrit Venkatagiri (Swarthmore College)
  • Venue / Date: Peer-reviewed — CHI 2026 (ACM Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems), April 13–17, 2026, Barcelona
  • Key finding: ScamPilot lets users coach a simulated victim through a live LLM-driven scam conversation, mixing quizzes with real-time advice-giving. In a between-subjects study (N=150), the quiz-plus-advice version significantly increased scam recognition by +8% and response efficacy by +9% versus a control, without making users more suspicious of legitimate messages. Self-efficacy rose 19%, though that gain was not statistically significant.
  • Source: https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2601.22426

4. The first qualitative map of the pig-butchering lifecycle, from 26 victims' accounts (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Title: "Hello, is this Anna?": Unpacking the Lifecycle of Pig-Butchering Scams
  • Authors: Rajvardhan Oak and Zubair Shafiq (University of California, Davis)
  • Venue / Date: Peer-reviewed — USENIX Symposium on Usable Privacy and Security (SOUPS) 2025, Aug 11–13, 2025, Philadelphia
  • Key finding: The first qualitative study of pig-butchering scams, based on in-depth interviews with 26 victims, maps an organized lifecycle: staged trust-building, fake investment platforms showing fabricated returns, repeated high-pressure tactics, and persistent re-engagement that amplifies losses. Victims also reported lasting psychological and financial harm, including heightened vulnerability to follow-on (secondary) scams. The authors propose intervention points for social media and financial platforms and call for non-stigmatizing language so victims report and seek help.
  • Source: https://www.usenix.org/conference/soups2025/presentation/oak-butchering

5. Phishing in your native language gets clicked far more often — 18.5% vs. 11.1% (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Title: Language as Lure: A Naturalistic Study on Pasifika Phishing Susceptibility
  • Authors: Eric Spero, Isa Seow, Lucas Betts, Eddie Fuatimau, Robert Biddle, Danielle Lottridge, Giovanni Russello (University of Auckland; Biddle also Carleton University)
  • Venue / Date: Peer-reviewed — USENIX SOUPS 2025, Aug 11–13, 2025, Philadelphia
  • Key finding: In a real-world simulated phishing campaign with 2,000 government employees in a Melanesian country (one email per person per month for four months, in English or the local language), phishing emails in the recipients' native language were clicked far more often: 18.5% click rate vs. 11.1% in English (overall click rate 14.5%; open rates 72.7% vs. 67.0%). The gap was driven by personal "request for help" emails — evidence of a "foreign language effect" in phishing, where people reason more emotionally in their native tongue and a cultural emphasis on helping raises susceptibility. Low-resource languages likely also face weaker email filters.
  • Source: https://www.usenix.org/conference/soups2025/technical-sessions

6. Production LLMs can be tricked into generating scam URLs and code — guardrails barely help (PREPRINT)

  • Title: Scam2Prompt: A Scalable Framework for Auditing Malicious Scam Endpoints in Production LLMs
  • Authors: Zhiyang Chen, Tara Saba, Xun Deng, Xujie Si, Fan Long
  • Venue / Date: Preprint — not yet peer-reviewed. arXiv, submitted Sept 2, 2025; latest version May 8, 2026 (v3)
  • Key finding: Because LLMs are trained on uncurated web data, they can absorb scam-site content and reproduce it. The authors' Scam2Prompt framework turns real scam sites into innocent-sounding developer prompts; these triggered malicious URL generation in 4.24% of cases across four production LLMs (GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini, Llama-4-Scout, DeepSeek-V3). On seven additional 2025 production LLMs, malicious code generation rates ranged from 12.7% to 43.8%. Existing guardrails barely helped — overall detection rate under 0.3%.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.02372v2

TODAY'S SAFETY TIP

If an email or message says your account, channel, or payment is at risk — or offers early access to something everyone wants — don't click it. Open the platform yourself. Today's edition is full of proof: 13,000 fake YouTube copyright notices arrived from a real company's mail server, Microsoft outage emails exploited a real outage, and fake GTA 6 downloads are stealing passwords and crypto wallets. The pattern is always the same — urgency plus a link. Check your channel in YouTube Studio, check your bank in its own app, check a game's availability on the official store. And if your bank calls about fraud, hang up and call the number on the back of your card: one victim in today's edition handed over $10,000 in cash to fake "FDIC" callers who told him his own bank was stealing from him.
