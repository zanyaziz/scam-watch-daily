1. Coldcard's X account hijacked to push wallet phishing — fake "firmware" warning

  • What's new: The official @COLDCARDwallet X account published an unauthorized post posing as an urgent firmware security alert, directing Bitcoin hardware-wallet users to a fake migration site (migrate.coldcardwallet.io) designed to harvest recovery credentials. The post was deleted; Coldcard says its internal review found no matching login or session records, and it has asked X to investigate a possible platform-level compromise. Coldcard says the fake post even referenced a real past vulnerability (a July 2026 flaw linked to ~1,700 BTC in losses) to add credibility.
  • Date: October 10, 2026 (evening PDT; post appeared ~02:00 UTC Oct 11) — verified across crypto.news, TradingView/Crypto Briefing, Crypto Daily UK, Daily Star.
  • Why it matters: Trusted-vendor account takeover targeting self-custody holders — a fake post from a hardware wallet maker can look identical to a genuine security advisory. Pairs with today's Ledger/Google story: wallet-recovery-phrase theft is the weekend's through-line.
  • Sources: https://crypto.news/coldcard-warns-bitcoin-users-about-phishing-scam-on-x/ ; https://www.tradingview.com/news/cryptobriefing:47f3e8e9a094b:0-coldcard-investigates-phishing-link-posted-on-its-x-account-after-exploit/

2. "You have a meeting": calendar phishing growing exponentially

  • What's new: Scammers are exploiting calendar invitations to drop fake meeting/renewal/voicemail entries directly into victims' Google or Outlook calendars; clicking the event link leads to fake Google, Microsoft, or PayPal login pages. Cofense researchers quoted say some attackers now send invites through legitimate platforms like Zoom, making them hard to block — "even AI-backed blockers struggle."
  • Date: October 11, 2026 (The Guardian; published ~06:00 GMT = ~11 PM PDT Oct 10) — labeled as just past midnight the edition date.
  • Why it matters: A major-outlet spotlight on a delivery channel that bypasses email filters and "borrows credibility" by appearing alongside your real dentist appointment. Simple defense: disable auto-adding of calendar invites from unknown senders.
  • Sources: https://www.europesays.com/ie/729895/ ; https://www.15minutenews.com/article/2026/10/11/284895863/you-have-a-meeting-the-calendar-phishing-scam-growing-exponentially/

3. 31,000+ phishing emails abusing Microsoft's own authentication links

  • What's new: Attackers register a real Microsoft Entra app, set its redirect URI to a malicious site, then send victims emails containing genuine Microsoft redirect links. Clicking the legitimate-looking Microsoft link routes the user to a credential-stealing or malware page. Kaspersky blocked over 31,000 such emails in seven weeks.
  • Date: Reported Oct 9, 2026 (Kaspersky); campaign ran Aug 1–Sep 18, 2026.
  • Why it matters: The phishing link is genuinely issued by Microsoft's auth servers, so it passes casual inspection and filter checks — a trust-abuse variant of credential phishing to watch.
  • Source: https://www.brandiconimage.com/2026/10/kaspersky-detects-over-31000-scam.html

RESEARCH DESK (2025–2026 research; preprints labeled)

1. A Large-Scale Study of Personalized Phishing using Large Language Models (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Title: "A Large-Scale Study of Personalized Phishing using Large Language Models"
  • Authors: Stefan Czybik, Anne Josiane Kouam, Peter Heubl, Jan Magnus Nold, Konrad Rieck (BIFOLD/TU Berlin, Inria, Ruhr University Bochum)
  • Venue/date: USENIX Security Symposium 2026, Baltimore — peer-reviewed, presented Aug 12, 2026 — verified on USENIX program page.
  • Key finding: In an experiment with 7,700 participants, LLM-generated spear phishing that auto-harvested personal details from web searches almost tripled the click rate compared to generic phishing — and the effect held whether the generic emails were human-written or LLM-written. Personalization costs almost nothing: roughly $0.03 per email. The authors conclude defenses must limit publicly linkable personal info and train users on personalized (not just generic) phishing.
  • Why it matters: Puts a number on the AI-phishing upgrade — 3× the clicks for three cents a target — and shifts the defense conversation from "spot the typo" to "assume the attacker knows you."
  • Source: https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity26/presentation/czybik

2. Effective Interventions Against AI-Enhanced Scams (PREPRINT)

  • Title: "Effective Interventions Against AI-Enhanced Scams"
  • Author: Kyle Fredrickson
  • Venue/date: arXiv preprint, submitted Sep 1, 2026 (arXiv:2609.00806) — not peer-reviewed — verified on arXiv abs page.
  • Key finding: The paper puts the scale at $442 billion in global scam losses in 2025, with US reported losses up nearly 400% between 2020 and 2025 (estimated to undercount real losses ~7×). Building a simple profit model of scam operations, it finds three levers — reporting rate, centralization of reporting, and report accuracy — multiply in their effect on victims per scam channel: even modest reporting rates against high-value scam infrastructure can materially cut scam profitability. Chainalysis is cited for AI-enabled scams being 4.5× more profitable than non-AI scams.
  • Why it matters: Makes the economic case that reporting fraud — even when you got your money back — is one of the highest-leverage defenses available.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.00806

3. Understanding Scam Trends and Rail Paths from Reddit Self-Disclosure Narratives (PREPRINT)

  • Title: "Understanding Scam Trends and Rail Paths from Reddit Self-Disclosure Narratives"
  • Authors: Yangjun Zhang, Mirko Bottarelli, Mark Hooper, Carsten Maple
  • Venue/date: arXiv preprint, submitted Jun 15, 2026 (arXiv:2606.16874) — not peer-reviewed — verified on arXiv abs page.
  • Key finding: From 21,304 Reddit posts in scam-related subreddits (2023–2025) plus 1,800 posts labeled with explicit scam chains, the study maps how scams evolve year to year and shows scam processes are predominantly "multi-rail" — chaining identity, communication, platform, and payment rails in ordered sequences rather than isolated tricks. Different scam types differ systematically in path complexity, and victim-support advice in the community has grown more detailed over time.
  • Why it matters: A longitudinal map of how multi-stage scams actually unfold in victims' own words — useful for designing interventions at the right stage of a scam, not after the money is gone.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.16874

4. An Analysis of the Impact of Psychological Factors and Techniques Across Different Types of Social Engineering (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Title: "An Analysis of the Impact of Psychological Factors and Techniques Across Different Types of Social Engineering"
  • Authors: Helin Omer, Daniela Pöhn
  • Venue/date: ARES 2026 International Workshops (Springer proceedings, chapter DOI 10.1007/978-3-032-35576-8_27) — peer-reviewed; arXiv:2608.25670, submitted Aug 26, 2026. Note: small exploratory lab study (n=12 participants), so treat percentages as indicative, not population estimates.
  • Key finding: Testing 5 psychological triggers (authority, trust, greed, fear, curiosity) × 5 attack types, spear-phishing was the most effective attack for authority, trust, and greed; the single strongest combination was spear-phishing exploiting greed (75% success rate) vs. 66.6% for generic phishing with greed. The weakest combos — pop-ups using authority, smishing using authority, and vishing using curiosity — had zero success. Fear worked best through phishing/spear-phishing (41.6% each); skepticism/distrust was the top reason people refused.
  • Why it matters: The data says the most dangerous mix is a message that knows you and promises you something — and that a healthy dose of distrust is measurably the best defense.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.25670

5. Victim as a Service: Designing a System for Engaging with Interactive Scammers (PREPRINT)

  • Title: "Victim as a Service: Designing a System for Engaging with Interactive Scammers"
  • Authors: Daniel Spokoyny, Nikolai Vogler, Xin Gao, Tianyi Zheng, Yufei Weng, Jonghyun Park, Jiajun Jiao, Geoffrey M. Voelker, Stefan Savage, Taylor Berg-Kirkpatrick (UC San Diego)
  • Venue/date: arXiv preprint, submitted Oct 27, 2025 (arXiv:2510.23927) — not peer-reviewed — verified on arXiv abs page.
  • Key finding: The team built Chatterbox, an LLM-based system that automates long-term engagement with real scammers — fighting back the way scammers engage victims. It places pig-butchering-type interactive scam losses at at least $75 billion and shows how automated personas can satisfy or evade the "milestones" in scammers' own workflows, opening a path to studying and disrupting these scams at scale.
  • Why it matters: Turns the AI-fraud economics on their head — using the same automation against the scammers to waste their time and map their operations.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.23927

Note: two of five items this round are peer-reviewed (USENIX Security '26, ARES 2026/Springer); three are arXiv preprints. TODAY'S SAFETY TIP

Your wallet's recovery phrase NEVER gets typed into a website — even a real-looking one, even one that outranks the real site. Saturday's Ledger story is the pattern to memorize: a fake Ledger-branded site sitting at the top of Google Search, built for one purpose — capturing the 24 words that control your crypto. The Coldcard X hijack used the same weapon from a different angle: a trusted account telling you to "migrate." The 60-second rule for any crypto-security message: (1) never Google your way to your wallet vendor's site — type the URL you already know or use the bookmarked one; (2) no legitimate wallet maker, support agent, or firmware alert will ever ask for your recovery phrase, on any site, in any app, on any call; (3) treat "urgent security action required" from any account — even a verified one — as suspect until confirmed through a second, independent channel you initiated yourself.
