Scam Watch Daily

Monday, November 9, 2026

A Wall Street Journal investigation says Meta tolerated thousands of scam ads on Facebook and Instagram — and insiders say the company held back on stricter advertiser checks to protect ad revenue.

Lead

A Wall Street Journal investigation says Meta tolerated thousands of scam ads on Facebook and Instagram — and insiders say the company held back on stricter advertiser checks to protect ad revenue. The report, circulating November 9–10, found Meta accounted for nearly half of all scam complaints tied to Zelle transactions at JPMorgan Chase in a one-year window. The same day, the DOJ moved to seize $456,602 in crypto from Facebook romance scams, fake AI-company event invites were found stealing Microsoft and Google work accounts, and Apple was reported to have quietly dropped a retail metric at the center of an unauthorized-subscription controversy. It was a quiet Monday for brand-new major news, but the day's biggest story goes straight at the platform where most of the scams in this newsletter begin.

TOP STORIES (ranked by importance)

1. Meta faces mounting scrutiny over widespread scam ads on Facebook and Instagram

  • Outlet: Wall Street Journal investigation, covered by Fox News / CyberGuy (Kurt "CyberGuy" Knutsson)
  • Date: Investigation circulated November 9–10, 2026; syndicated copies dated November 10, 2026 — exact WSJ publication date not independently verified, label date
  • Summary: A major Wall Street Journal report found thousands of fraudulent ads running on Facebook and Instagram, and that Meta accounted for nearly half of all scam complaints tied to Zelle transactions at JPMorgan Chase between mid-2023 and mid-2024. Meta calls it an "epidemic of scams" and says it removed more than 2 million accounts linked to scam centers this year, but current and former employees told the Journal the company was reluctant to tighten advertiser onboarding for fear of hurting ad revenue, reportedly tolerating 8–32 fraud "strikes" before banning accounts.
  • Why it matters: This is the most consequential platform-accountability story of the day — it ties the world's largest social ad platform directly to the scale of consumer scam losses, with internal-employee sourcing that undercuts Meta's public enforcement narrative. It also frames the policy debate (Section 230, ad-revenue incentives) around scam ads.
  • Source: https://www.qldtourism.com/news/meta-faces-increasing-scrutiny-over-widespread-scam-ads/35780

2. DOJ moves to seize $456,602 in crypto from Facebook romance scams

  • Outlet: U.S. Department of Justice, U.S. Attorney's Office for the District of Massachusetts (official press release)
  • Date: November 9, 2026 (verified on justice.gov)
  • Summary: Federal prosecutors filed a civil forfeiture action (case No. 26-cv-15037) to recover 456,602 USDT (Tether), worth about $456,602, allegedly the proceeds of romance fraud schemes that targeted victims in Massachusetts and elsewhere via online dating sites and Facebook. According to the complaint, scammers built relationships of trust, steered victims to fake crypto investment platforms showing fake profits, then blocked withdrawals and demanded extra "fees and taxes." One Norwood, Massachusetts victim reportedly lost about $1.89 million across 26 transactions. The funds were traced to two crypto wallets and seized; the complaint's claims are allegations only.
  • Why it matters: A clean, on-date federal enforcement action that illustrates the classic pig-butchering playbook in concrete numbers — and a rare case where seized funds may eventually be returned to victims once third-party claims are resolved. It is the latest in a run of Massachusetts forfeiture actions against romance-linked crypto fraud in 2026.
  • Source: https://www.justice.gov/usao-ma/pr/united-states-attorneys-office-files-civil-forfeiture-action-recover-cryptocurrency-1

3. Fake OpenAI, SpaceX and Anthropic event invites used to steal Microsoft and Google work accounts

  • Outlet: BleepingComputer, reporting Push Security research
  • Date: November 5, 2026 (verified) — late-breaking, label date
  • Summary: Attackers are sending invitations to fake AI-company events — an OpenAI livestream, a private SpaceX briefing on Nov 12, an Anthropic event at AWS re:Invent — promising free ChatGPT Pro or Grok Heavy to lure employees into signing in with work accounts. The pages chain two phishing-as-a-service platforms: Kali365 for Microsoft device-code phishing and VoidProxy as an adversary-in-the-middle proxy that intercepts Google sign-ins and steals session cookies, defeating ordinary MFA. Links arrive via trusted Google and Zapier URL shorteners and sit behind Cloudflare CAPTCHAs. Push Security detected and blocked visits in late October; a related wave in July abused legitimate Zoom event pages.
  • Why it matters: This is the most technically notable scam campaign of the week — it shows phishing-as-a-service platforms being combined to defeat MFA on both Microsoft and Google accounts at once, using the hottest brands in tech as bait. The Nov 12 SpaceX date in the lure means the campaign was still live heading into this week.
  • Source: https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/fake-openai-spacex-events-used-to-steal-microsoft-google-accounts/

NEW & EMERGING SCAMS

1. Fake traffic-violation texts now hide the phishing link in a QR code

  • Date: Reported November 10, 2026 (CyberGuy / BleepingComputer reporting) — late-breaking, label date
  • How it works: A smishing wave impersonating state courts and DMVs is hitting drivers across the U.S. (New York, California, North Carolina, Illinois, Virginia, Texas, Connecticut, New Jersey and more). The text claims an outstanding traffic violation — a $6.99 balance in every documented case — and demands payment via a QR code embedded in a fake court notice image, rather than a clickable link. The QR code routes through a CAPTCHA page (designed to filter out security scanners) to a spoofed DMV site that harvests name, address, phone, email and credit-card details.
  • Who it targets: Drivers. The shift from links to QR codes makes the message harder for spam filters to flag, and the fake notice's formal language and threats of court appearances lend it credibility. It follows the 2025 toll-agency smishing wave and an April 2026 FTC-warned "hearing notice" variant. Real courts communicate by mail, never by text with a QR code.
  • Why it's new: QR-code phishing ("quishing") keeps mutating — this wave moves the QR code from email into text messages and hides it inside a fake court document.
  • Source: https://www.mackaytourism.com.au/news/fake-traffic-violation-text-scam-uses-qr-codes-to-steal-payment-info/53857

2. "SkyW4lker" phishing kit takes over Microsoft 365 accounts — without stealing a password

  • Date: Research reported November 2026; kit active since June 23, 2026
  • How it works: A phishing-as-a-service kit called SkyW4lker (branded "SkyW4lker x365 Sentinel") abuses Microsoft's legitimate OAuth device-code sign-in flow to hijack Microsoft 365 accounts. Victims are tricked into typing a code into a real Microsoft login page; the attacker receives the valid session token — no password theft and no MFA bypass needed. Researchers at ANY.RUN tracked 618 sandbox analyses tied to the kit from June to October 21, 2026, with monthly submissions climbing from 20 in June to 200 in September; roughly 46–47% of submissions came from the U.S.
  • Who it targets: U.S. enterprises on Microsoft 365, especially tech, manufacturing and government. The kit shares code with the Gabagool credential-phishing kit (active since April 2026) and has been continuously updated since its June 23, 2026 debut. The tell: any unexpected prompt to enter a device code at microsoft.com/devicelogin.
  • Why it's new: Device-code phishing turns a legitimate Microsoft feature into a full account takeover that defeats MFA without ever touching the victim's password.
  • Source: https://cybersecuritynews.com/skyw4lker-phishing-campaign-expands-threatening-us-enterprises-with-microsoft-365-account-takeovers/

3. The "Comment Clone Scam": fake CEO accounts strike in the replies, then move victims to Telegram

  • Date: November 9, 2026
  • How it works: CoinDCX co-founder and CEO Sumit Gupta warned on X on November 9, 2026 (10:41 UTC) about a new impersonation tactic his team calls the "Comment Clone Scam." Fraudsters create fake social-media profiles using his identity, reply to comments underneath his genuine posts, and try to move the conversation to Telegram or other private channels — where investment tips and money transfers are then pitched. Gupta clarified he never privately offers investment tips or asks anyone to transfer money.
  • Who it targets: Anyone who comments on a crypto executive's or influencer's posts. The reply from a lookalike account feels like a personal response from a trusted figure, and the shift to Telegram removes platform moderation. No financial losses or victim counts were disclosed — the warning is preventive.
  • Why it's new: The scam lives in the replies, not in a fake post — it hijacks the trust of a real conversation the victim already joined.
  • Source: https://www.cryptotimes.io/2026/11/09/coindcx-ceo-sumit-gupta-warns-of-comment-clone-scam/

4. Fake CAPTCHA trick ("ClickFix") called "the scariest scam of 2026" — DEVELOPING

  • Date: November 8, 2026 (Rossen Reports segment). Developing: single creator video; the technique itself is well-documented by security researchers through 2025–2026, but treat the "scariest scam of 2026" framing as the creator's characterization.
  • How it works: A consumer-protection segment from Jeff Rossen (Rossen Reports, posted November 8, 2026) warns about fake CAPTCHA verification pages spreading in 2026. The page shows a normal "I'm not a robot" checkbox, then instructs the user to press Windows Key + R, paste code with Ctrl+V, and hit Enter — which secretly runs a command that installs malware and gives scammers remote access to the computer.
  • Who it targets: Anyone browsing compromised or malicious sites. The victim does the infecting themselves, so no download warning fires. If it happens, the advice is to disconnect from the internet immediately, run an antivirus scan, change all passwords, and add two-factor authentication.
  • Why it's new: Fresh mainstream coverage of a technique that weaponizes the victim's own keyboard — no download, no warning, just a "verification" step.
  • Source: https://www.facebook.com/reel/1478096147552634/

5. Task scams keep surging: "rate products, get paid daily" — then pay to withdraw

  • Date: Fresh explainers published November 2–3, 2026
  • How it works: Task scams — unsolicited WhatsApp/Telegram messages offering easy remote work "optimizing" apps, rating hotels or boosting products for $200–$900/day — remain one of the most-reported job scams of 2026. The FTC has reported task-scam reports jumping from about 5,000 in all of 2023 to about 20,000 in just the first half of 2024, and job-scam losses of more than $220 million in the first half of 2024. A November 2026 field census of ~160 crypto-paying "online work" sites also found the same patterns alive and well: pay-first tasks, stake-to-claim bounties that never complete, and laptop-farm/mule recruitment.
  • Who it targets: Students, job seekers and people looking for side income. Small real payments for early tasks build trust; then a "VIP" or prepaid task requires depositing your own money (usually crypto) to continue or to "unlock" earnings — and the money is gone. The rule that catches it: a real job never asks you to pay to get paid.
  • Why it's new: Not a new format, but fresh November 2026 reporting shows it still scaling — and the FTC's numbers show how fast it grew.
  • Source: https://www.gmork.com/scams/task-job-scam-app-optimization

RESEARCH DESK

1. Evaluating AI Models' Capability to Automate Voice Phishing Attacks (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Title: Evaluating AI Models' Capability to Automate Voice Phishing Attacks
  • Authors: Fred Heiding, Claudio Mayrink Verdun, Simon Lermen, Andrew Kao, Vitor Albiero, Lauren Deason, Irina-Elena Veliche, Christine Lehane
  • Venue / Date: Peer-reviewed — Expert Systems With Applications, vol. 332 (2027), article 133620; received Apr 2026, accepted Jul 2026; arXiv:2607.09970 (submitted Jul 10, 2026)
  • Key finding: In a large survey experiment (N=4,100 U.S. adults) exposed to AI-generated voice-phishing scenarios, 16.5% overall would or might comply with the scam requests — and up to 36% in the "relative-in-distress" scenario (a loved one supposedly in trouble). Some AI voices, most notably Sesame, were rated as persuasive as human voices, sometimes slightly more. The authors' economic analysis found human-operated vishing unprofitable at U.S. wages, but AI-powered vishing economically viable — so the danger is the economics of automation, not superhuman persuasion.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.09970

2. Can AI Models be Jailbroken to Phish Elderly Victims? An End-to-End Evaluation (PREPRINT)

  • Title: Can AI Models be Jailbroken to Phish Elderly Victims? An End-to-End Evaluation
  • Authors: Fred Heiding, Simon Lermen
  • Venue / Date: Preprint — arXiv:2511.11759, submitted Nov 13, 2025 (not peer-reviewed)
  • Key finding: The researchers jailbroke six frontier LLMs to generate phishing messages aimed at seniors, then tested them on 108 senior volunteers — 11% of the seniors were successfully compromised by the AI-generated phishing emails. Several models showed "near-complete susceptibility" to certain attack vectors. Context: Americans 60+ reported nearly $4.9 billion in online-fraud losses in 2024 (FBI IC3). The study was done in collaboration with Reuters journalists.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.11759

3. AI-in-the-Loop: Privacy Preserving Real-Time Scam Detection and Conversational Scambaiting by Leveraging LLMs and Federated Learning (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Title: AI-in-the-Loop: Privacy Preserving Real-Time Scam Detection and Conversational Scambaiting by Leveraging LLMs and Federated Learning
  • Authors: Ismail Hossain, Sai Puppala, Md Jahangir Alam, Sajedul Talukder
  • Venue / Date: Peer-reviewed — accepted at the 26th Privacy Enhancing Technologies Symposium (PETS 2026); arXiv:2509.05362, submitted Sep 4, 2025, revised Jan 20, 2026
  • Key finding: A system that detects scam conversations in real time and talks back to the scammer (scambaiting) to waste their time, while keeping users' data private via federated learning. It produced fluent, engaging replies (engagement ≈0.80) and kept PII leakage very low (≤0.0085) even after 30 rounds of federated training — and adding differential privacy barely hurt performance. The trade-off: stricter safety moderation leaks less personal data but also engages scammers less effectively.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.05362

4. ScamPilot: Simulating Conversations with LLMs to Protect Against Online Scams (PEER-REVIEWED)

  • Title: ScamPilot: Simulating Conversations with LLMs to Protect Against Online Scams
  • Authors: Owen Hoffman, Kangze Peng, Sajid Kamal, Zehua You, Sukrit Venkatagiri (Swarthmore College)
  • Venue / Date: Peer-reviewed — Proceedings of CHI 2026 (ACM Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems, Barcelona, Apr 13–17, 2026); arXiv:2601.22426, submitted Jan 30, 2026
  • Key finding: Instead of lecturing people about scams, ScamPilot lets users practice by coaching a simulated victim through a live LLM-powered scam conversation. In a 150-person study, combining advice-giving with quiz questions improved scam recognition by 8% without making people distrust legitimate messages, and raised users' confidence in their ability to respond by 19% and their sense of response efficacy by 9%.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.22426

5. Talking Like a Phisher: LLM-Based Attacks on Voice Phishing Classifiers (PREPRINT)

  • Title: Talking Like a Phisher: LLM-Based Attacks on Voice Phishing Classifiers
  • Authors: Wenhao Li, Selvakumar Manickam, Yung-wey Chong, Shankar Karuppayah
  • Venue / Date: Preprint — arXiv:2507.16291, submitted Jul 22, 2025 (not peer-reviewed)
  • Key finding: Attackers can use commercial LLMs to rewrite real vishing (voice-phishing) scripts so automated detectors miss them while the scam intent stays intact. Transcripts rewritten by GPT-4o cut detector accuracy by up to 30.96%, and each rewrite took under 9 seconds at negligible cost — showing that today's ML-based vishing detectors are brittle against cheap LLM-assisted evasion.
  • Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.16291

TODAY'S SAFETY TIP

If a login page asks you to enter a device code you didn't request, stop — that is the scam. The SkyW4lker kit and the fake OpenAI/SpaceX event invites both work by getting you to type a code into a real Microsoft login page (microsoft.com/devicelogin) or sign in through a lookalike page — and once you do, the attacker holds your session, password and MFA notwithstanding. Device codes are only for signing in a device you own, and only when you started the sign-in. If an email, event invite, or "support" message tells you to enter a code, close the page, go to the service yourself, and check your account's recent sign-in activity. And if a CEO or celebrity replies to your comment asking you to move to Telegram, treat it as an impersonator until proven otherwise — real executives don't pitch investments in DMs.
